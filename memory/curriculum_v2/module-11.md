# SESSION 11.1: The Sector Analysis Framework — How to Decode Any Industry

> "An airline and a soap company can both grow sales 15% a year — yet one struggles to ever make money while the other mints it. The difference isn't management. It's the *structure* of the industry. Learn to read that structure, and you can decode any sector on earth."

---

## 🎣 HOOK: "Two industries, same growth — but only one makes money"

Airlines fly millions of passengers and grow fast. Yet many barely earn a profit for years.

FMCG companies sell cheap everyday products — and earn fat, consistent returns.

Why? Not luck. Not management.

It's the *structure* of the industry — competition, suppliers, switching costs.

Some sectors are structurally great businesses. Others are structurally brutal. Reading that is step one.

🧠 **Remember:** A sector's *structure* — not just its growth — decides whether companies in it can make money.

---

## 💡 CONCEPT: "Porter's Five Forces — The X-Ray of Any Industry"

To judge if a sector is attractive, analysts use Porter's Five Forces.

**Rivalry:** How fierce is competition between existing players?

**New entrants:** How easily can newcomers join and steal share?

**Substitutes:** Can a different product replace this one?

**Supplier power:** Can suppliers squeeze the industry's margins?

**Buyer power:** Can customers demand lower prices?

Weak forces = a profitable sector. Strong forces = a brutal one.

🧠 **Remember:** Porter's Five Forces — rivalry, entrants, substitutes, suppliers, buyers — reveal whether a sector is easy or brutal to profit in.

---

## 🔬 DEEP DIVE: "The Universal Sector Checklist"

Whatever the sector, run it through this checklist to decode it.

- **Drivers:** What fundamentally moves it? (Consumption, commodity prices, interest rates, tech cycles, regulation.)
- **Structure:** Five Forces — is the sector consolidated (few strong players) or fragmented and cut-throat?
- **Key metrics:** Every sector has signature KPIs — NIM for banks, ARPU for telecom, EBITDA/tonne for cement.
- **Moats:** What protects the leaders? (Brand, scale, network effects, switching costs, regulation.)
- **Regulation:** Who governs it? (RBI for banks, TRAI for telecom, IRDAI for insurance, USFDA/NPPA for pharma.)
- **Cyclicality:** Is it cyclical, defensive, or a structural growth story?

🧠 **Remember:** Decode any sector by its drivers, structure, signature metrics, moats, regulator, and cyclicality.

---

## 🔢 FORMULA: "Market Share"

**Formula:** Market Share (%) = (Company's Sales ÷ Total Industry Sales) × 100

| Variable | Meaning |
|----------|---------|
| Company's Sales | The company's revenue (or volume) |
| Total Industry Sales | The whole sector's revenue (or volume) |

**Example:** A company sells ₹5,000 cr in a ₹25,000 cr industry.
Market Share = (5,000 ÷ 25,000) × 100 = **20%**.

**Signal:** Rising market share means → the company is winning versus rivals (a key bullish signal). Falling share means → it's losing the competitive battle.

**Analyst Insight:** *Gaining* share in a growing industry is the best combination — the company grows faster than the market itself, compounding its advantage.

🧠 **Remember:** Market share shows who's winning the sector battle — and rising share in a growing market is the dream setup.

---

## 📊 REAL WORLD: "Five Forces — Why FMCG Thrives and Airlines Struggle"

Apply Porter's Five Forces to two industries and the difference is stark.

**FMCG:** Strong brands keep rivalry manageable, switching is emotional, and customers rarely have power over a beloved brand — favourable forces, high returns.

**Airlines:** Brutal price rivalry, powerful suppliers (fuel, aircraft makers), zero switching cost for travellers, and easy price comparison — punishing forces, thin or no profits.

Same effort, opposite economics — because the *structures* differ completely.

🧠 **Remember:** FMCG's favourable Five Forces make it a great business; airlines' brutal forces make profit a constant struggle.

---

## ⚠️ MISTAKE: "Judging every sector by the same yardstick"

**The error:** A student applies the same metrics and expectations to a bank, an IT firm, and a steel maker.

**Why they make it:** They learn one toolkit and assume it works everywhere.

**The correct understanding:** Each sector has its own drivers and signature KPIs — you must learn the rules of each game separately.

**The consequence:** Using FMCG logic on a cyclical steel maker, or P/E on a loss-making new-age firm, leads to badly wrong conclusions.

🧠 **Remember:** One yardstick doesn't fit all sectors — learn the specific rules of each industry before judging its companies.

# SESSION 11.2: Banking & Financial Services (BFSI) — Banks, NBFCs & Insurance

> "A bank is the only business that *sells money*. It borrows at one price (deposits) and lends at a higher one — and the gap, minus the loans that go bad, is its profit. Master that one idea, and you can analyse any lender in India."

---

## 🎣 HOOK: "A bank is a business that rents out money"

Most companies make and sell a product. A bank's product *is* money.

It borrows your savings (deposits) cheaply and lends them out at a higher rate.

The gap between the two is its core profit — the net interest margin.

But there's a catch: some borrowers never repay. Those bad loans eat the profit.

So a bank is a spread business with one giant risk: getting paid back.

🧠 **Remember:** A bank rents out money — its profit is the lending spread, minus the loans that go bad.

---

## 💡 CONCEPT: "The Three Pillars of a Lender"

Every bank or NBFC rests on three pillars.

**Profitability:** The spread between lending and borrowing rates (the net interest margin).

**Funding:** How cheap and stable its money is — deposits for banks, borrowings for NBFCs.

**Asset quality:** How many loans go bad — measured by non-performing assets (NPAs).

A great lender has a healthy spread, cheap funding, and clean assets.

Weakness in any one pillar can bring the whole thing down.

🧠 **Remember:** A lender stands on three pillars — profitability (spread), funding (cost), and asset quality (bad loans).

---

## 🔬 DEEP DIVE: "The Sub-Sectors and Signature Metrics of BFSI"

BFSI isn't one thing — it's banks, NBFCs, and insurers, each measured differently.

- **Banks:** NIM (spread), CASA (low-cost deposits), GNPA/NNPA (asset quality), CAR (capital cushion, set by RBI).
- **Private vs PSU:** Private banks (HDFC, ICICI) have historically had better asset quality and valuations than PSU banks.
- **NBFCs:** Borrow from markets/banks to lend; watch their cost of funds and asset-liability matching (the IL&FS lesson).
- **Insurance (life):** Measured by VNB margin (Value of New Business), persistency, and embedded value — not NIM.
- **Regulators:** RBI governs banks and NBFCs; IRDAI governs insurance.

🧠 **Remember:** Banks → NIM/CASA/NPA/CAR; NBFCs → cost of funds and ALM; insurers → VNB margin and persistency.

---

## 🔢 FORMULA: "Gross NPA Ratio (Asset Quality)"

**Formula:** Gross NPA Ratio (%) = (Gross Non-Performing Assets ÷ Gross Advances) × 100

| Variable | Meaning |
|----------|---------|
| Gross NPAs | Total value of loans that have gone bad (borrowers stopped paying) |
| Gross Advances | Total loans the bank has given out |

**Example:** Gross NPAs ₹2,000 cr, gross advances ₹1,00,000 cr.
Gross NPA Ratio = (2,000 ÷ 1,00,000) × 100 = **2%**.

**Signal:** A low and stable GNPA ratio (~1–3%) means → healthy lending and good underwriting. A high or rising ratio means → loans are going bad, threatening profits.

**Analyst Insight:** Asset quality is the single biggest swing factor for a bank's stock — rising NPAs force higher provisions, which hit profits fast and can wipe out years of earnings.

🧠 **Remember:** The Gross NPA ratio is a bank's asset-quality scorecard — the lower and steadier, the healthier the lender.

---

## 📊 REAL WORLD: "Why Private Banks Trade at a Premium to PSU Banks"

For years, India's top private banks — like HDFC Bank and ICICI Bank — have traded at higher valuations than public-sector banks.

The reason is the three pillars: private banks generally had cleaner asset quality, stronger growth, better technology, and sharper underwriting.

Many PSU banks struggled with higher NPAs and slower growth, despite their huge deposit bases.

So the market consistently paid a premium for the lenders with healthier books and faster, cleaner growth.

It's a textbook case of asset quality and growth driving sector valuations.

🧠 **Remember:** Private banks' cleaner books and faster growth earned them premium valuations over PSU banks.

---

## ⚠️ MISTAKE: "Judging a bank cheap on a low P/E without checking asset quality"

**The error:** A student sees a bank on a low P/E or below book value and calls it a bargain.

**Why they make it:** A low valuation looks like obvious value.

**The correct understanding:** A bank can look cheap precisely because its loan book is deteriorating — hidden bad loans can destroy the equity.

**The consequence:** Buying a "cheap" bank with a rotting loan book is a classic value trap that can wipe out capital when the NPAs surface.

🧠 **Remember:** A cheap-looking bank with poor asset quality is a trap — always check the loan book before the valuation.

## INTERVIEW QUESTIONS

### 🎙️ IQ1: "How would you analyse a bank?"
*Banking / BFSI round — Kotak Securities, ICICI Securities, HDFC Securities.*

**Answer Framework:**
- I'd start with profitability — the net interest margin, the spread between lending and deposit rates.
- Then funding — the CASA ratio, since cheap, stable deposits support a stronger margin.

- I'd check capital adequacy (CAR) for the regulatory safety cushion, and ROA for overall return.
- I'd judge whether growth is healthy and provisioning adequate before forming a view.

**🔑 Power Phrase:** "I read a bank through three pillars — its spread, its funding, and its bad loans — and asset quality is the one that decides its fate."

**⚠️ Trap:** Applying generic ratios like debt-to-equity or EV/EBITDA. Banks demand their own toolkit — NIM, CASA, NPAs, CAR.

### 🎙️ IQ2: "Why do private banks generally trade at a premium to PSU banks?"
*BFSI / valuation round — DSP, Nuvama, Axis Capital.*

- Private banks have generally had cleaner asset quality and lower NPAs.
- They've grown faster, with sharper underwriting and stronger technology.

- Many PSU banks faced higher bad loans and slower growth despite large deposit bases.
- So the market pays up for the lenders with healthier books and faster, cleaner growth.

**🔑 Power Phrase:** "The market pays a premium for clean books and quality growth — that's the structural edge private banks have long held."

---

### 🎙️ IQ3: "What's the key difference between analysing a bank and an NBFC?"
*Lending / BFSI round — Edelweiss, IIFL, Systematix.*

**Answer Framework:**
- Banks fund themselves mainly through deposits, which are cheap and relatively sticky.
- NBFCs fund themselves by borrowing from banks and markets, so their cost of funds matters more.
- NBFCs are more exposed to asset-liability mismatch and liquidity risk.
- For NBFCs, I'd focus heavily on funding access, spreads, and ALM, alongside asset quality.
- The IL&FS crisis showed how funding and ALM stress can sink an NBFC quickly.

**🔑 Power Phrase:** "A bank's strength is its deposits; an NBFC's vulnerability is its funding — so for NBFCs I watch the cost and stability of money most closely."

**⚠️ Trap:** Treating banks and NBFCs identically. The funding model and liquidity risk are the crucial differences.

# SESSION 11.3: Information Technology — India's Export Engine

> "An Indian IT giant employs hundreds of thousands of engineers, bills its clients in dollars, pays its people in rupees, and barely owns a factory. It's one of the great business models — and it built India's reputation as the back office of the world."

---

## 🎣 HOOK: "A company with no factory, billing the world in dollars"

India's IT firms own almost no machinery. Their asset is *people*.

They hire engineers in India (paid in rupees) and serve global clients (billed in dollars).

Low capital, high cash generation, and a built-in currency advantage.

When the rupee weakens, their dollar revenue converts into even more rupees — free margin.

It's a capital-light, cash-rich, export machine.

🧠 **Remember:** Indian IT earns in dollars, spends in rupees, owns no factories — a capital-light, cash-rich export model.

---

## 💡 CONCEPT: "The IT Services Business Model"

IT services firms sell skilled engineering time and solutions to global companies.

Their edge has long been *cost arbitrage* — world-class talent at a fraction of Western costs.

They run a "pyramid": many junior engineers, fewer seniors — keeping costs low.

Profit comes from billing clients more than it costs to employ the team.

The model is people-driven, so talent, wages, and productivity are everything.

🧠 **Remember:** IT services sell engineering talent globally — profit is the gap between what clients pay and what people cost.

---

## 🔬 DEEP DIVE: "What Drives an IT Company — and Its Signature Metrics"

To analyse IT, watch these drivers and KPIs.

- **Demand:** Global tech spending, especially from the US and Europe (banking, retail verticals).
- **Margins:** Driven by utilisation (how busy engineers are), the pyramid mix, and wage inflation.
- **Currency:** A weaker rupee boosts margins; a stronger one is a headwind.
- **Signature metrics:** Revenue per employee, utilisation rate, attrition, deal wins (TCV), and constant-currency growth.
- **The big question:** AI and automation — both a threat to routine work and an opportunity for new services.

🧠 **Remember:** IT is driven by global demand, utilisation, wages, and currency — and AI is the defining swing factor ahead.

---

## 🔢 FORMULA: "Revenue per Employee"

**Formula:** Revenue per Employee = Total Revenue ÷ Number of Employees

| Variable | Meaning |
|----------|---------|
| Total Revenue | The company's annual revenue |
| Number of Employees | Total headcount |

**Example:** Revenue ₹2,00,000 cr, employees 5,00,000.
Revenue per Employee = 2,00,000 cr ÷ 5,00,000 = **₹40 lakh per employee**.

**Signal:** Rising revenue per employee means → higher productivity, premium work, or better automation. Falling means → commoditised, low-value work or bloated headcount.

**Analyst Insight:** As AI automates routine coding, revenue per employee becomes a key lens — the firms that lift it are climbing the value chain, not just adding bodies.

🧠 **Remember:** Revenue per employee shows how productive an IT firm is — rising means it's moving up the value chain, not just hiring more people.

---

## 📊 REAL WORLD: "How Cost Arbitrage and the Rupee Built Indian IT"

India's IT industry grew into a global powerhouse on one core advantage: cost arbitrage.

Companies like TCS, Infosys, and Wipro offered world-class engineering at a fraction of Western costs.

Because they bill in dollars but pay engineers in rupees, a depreciating rupee has long acted as a tailwind to their margins (a direct macro link from Module 10).

Over time, they moved beyond basic coding into digital, cloud, and consulting to protect margins as wages rose.

The model now faces its biggest test from AI — which can both disrupt routine work and create new demand.

🧠 **Remember:** Cost arbitrage plus a weak-rupee tailwind built Indian IT — and AI is now reshaping the model.

---

## ⚠️ MISTAKE: "Ignoring attrition and wage inflation when margins look fine"

**The error:** A student looks only at current margins and revenue growth, ignoring the people side.

**Why they make it:** Headline numbers feel sufficient; HR metrics seem like noise.

**The correct understanding:** High attrition and rising wages can quietly erode future margins — the talent engine is the whole business.

**The consequence:** Praising an IT firm's margins while ignoring a spike in attrition or wage costs misses the pressure building beneath the surface.

🧠 **Remember:** In IT, people *are* the product — attrition and wage inflation are core margin drivers, not side details.

## 💡 CONCEPT: "Why FMCG Is a Wonderful Business"

Two moats make it powerful: **brands** (trust and habit) and **distribution** (reach into millions of stores).

A trusted brand lets a company charge a bit more — *pricing power*.

A vast distribution network is incredibly hard for newcomers to replicate.

Together, they produce high returns on capital and durable, defensive growth.

🧠 **Remember:** FMCG's twin moats — brands and distribution — give it pricing power, high returns, and defensive durability.

---

## 🔬 DEEP DIVE: "What Drives FMCG — and Its Signature Metrics"

To analyse FMCG, watch these drivers and KPIs.

- **Demand:** Rural and urban consumption, monsoons (rural income), and premiumisation (trading up).
- **Costs:** Raw materials are often commodities, so input inflation hits gross margins (the Module 10 link).
- **Volume vs value growth:** Analysts split revenue growth into *volume* (real demand) and *price/mix* (inflation/premiumisation).
- **Signature metrics:** Volume growth, gross/EBITDA margin, A&P (advertising) spend as % of sales, and distribution reach.
- **The key tell:** Strong *volume* growth signals genuine demand; price-led growth alone can mask weak underlying demand.

🧠 **Remember:** FMCG is driven by consumption and input costs — and the key tell is *volume* growth, not just value growth.

---

## 🔢 FORMULA: "Volume vs Value Growth"

**Formula:** Value (Revenue) Growth ≈ Volume Growth + Price/Mix Growth

| Variable | Meaning |
|----------|---------|
| Value Growth | Total revenue growth (in rupees) |
| Volume Growth | Growth in actual units/quantity sold (real demand) |
| Price/Mix Growth | Growth from higher prices or selling premium products |

**Example:** A company's revenue grows 10%. Volume grew 3%; the rest came from price hikes and premium mix.
So Value Growth (10%) ≈ Volume Growth (3%) + Price/Mix Growth (7%).

**Signal:** Strong volume growth means → genuine, healthy demand. Growth that's mostly price-led means → demand may be weak, and growth is just inflation pass-through.

**Analyst Insight:** Analysts obsess over the volume number — a company growing revenue only by raising prices (while volumes stagnate) is a warning that real demand is faltering.

🧠 **Remember:** Always split FMCG growth into volume and price — strong volume is real demand; price-only growth can hide weakness.

---

## 📊 REAL WORLD: "When FMCG Growth Was All Price and No Volume"

During the high-inflation period around 2022, many Indian FMCG companies reported decent revenue growth.

But beneath the surface, much of that growth was *price-led* — they raised prices to offset soaring raw-material costs.

*Volume* growth, the measure of real demand, was often weak, especially in rural markets squeezed by inflation.

Analysts flagged this volume-versus-value gap as a sign of underlying demand stress, even as headline revenue looked fine.

It was a live lesson: in FMCG, the volume number tells the true story that the revenue figure can hide.

🧠 **Remember:** Headline FMCG revenue can look healthy on price hikes alone — weak volumes reveal the real demand picture.

---

## ⚠️ MISTAKE: "Cheering FMCG revenue growth without checking volumes"

**The error:** A student sees an FMCG company's revenue rising and assumes demand is strong.

**Why they make it:** Revenue is the headline; the volume/value split feels technical.

**The correct understanding:** If growth is purely from price hikes while volumes are flat or falling, real demand is actually weakening.

**The consequence:** Calling an FMCG company healthy on price-led growth misses the demand stress that eventually shows up in the stock.

🧠 **Remember:** In FMCG, revenue growth means little without volume growth — always check the split.

# SESSION 11.5: Pharmaceuticals & Healthcare — Generics, Pipelines & Hospitals

> "Indian pharma supplies a huge share of the world's affordable generic medicines. It's a story of science, scale, and one ever-present risk: a single inspection by the US drug regulator can send a stock soaring or crashing."

---

## 🎣 HOOK: "An inspector's report can move a pharma stock more than its earnings"

A pharma company can report great profits — and still crash on one piece of news.

That news? A bad inspection report from the US FDA on one of its plants.

Or soar, when it wins approval to sell a lucrative drug in America.

In pharma, regulation and pipelines often matter as much as the financials.

It's a sector where a single regulatory event can rewrite the story overnight.

🧠 **Remember:** In pharma, a regulator's inspection or a drug approval can move a stock more than its quarterly earnings.

---

## 💡 CONCEPT: "How Indian Pharma Makes Money"

Indian pharma is built largely on **generics** — copies of drugs whose patents have expired.

It makes them at huge scale and low cost, supplying India and the world (especially the US).

There are a few engines: the **domestic** market (steady branded generics), **US generics** (big but price-competitive), and **APIs/CDMO** (making ingredients and manufacturing for others).

Newer drugs and complex generics earn more; plain generics face brutal price erosion.

The pipeline of future drug approvals is the engine of growth.

🧠 **Remember:** Indian pharma runs on low-cost generics across domestic, US, and API/CDMO engines — and the approval pipeline drives growth.

---

## 🔬 DEEP DIVE: "What Drives Pharma — and Its Signature Metrics"

To analyse pharma, watch these drivers, risks, and KPIs.

- **The pipeline:** Drug approvals (ANDAs) for the US market drive future growth.
- **US price erosion:** Intense competition steadily pushes generic prices down — a constant headwind.
- **USFDA risk:** A plant warning letter or import alert can block US sales and hammer the stock.
- **Domestic stability:** The Indian branded-generics business is steadier, less price-eroding.
- **Signature metrics:** R&D as % of sales, US revenue mix, gross margin, and the base business versus one-off exclusivity gains.

🧠 **Remember:** Pharma is driven by the approval pipeline, US price erosion, USFDA compliance, and steady domestic sales.

---

## 🔢 FORMULA: "R&D Intensity"

**Formula:** R&D Intensity (%) = (R&D Spend ÷ Revenue) × 100

| Variable | Meaning |
|----------|---------|
| R&D Spend | Money invested in research and developing new drugs |
| Revenue | Total revenue |

**Example:** R&D spend ₹800 cr, revenue ₹10,000 cr.
R&D Intensity = (800 ÷ 10,000) × 100 = **8%**.

**Signal:** Higher R&D intensity means → a company investing heavily in a future pipeline (especially complex, high-value drugs). Very low intensity means → reliance on plain generics facing price erosion.

**Analyst Insight:** R&D spend is an *investment in the future pipeline*, not just a cost — but it must translate into approvals and products, or it's money burned.

🧠 **Remember:** R&D intensity shows how much a pharma firm is investing in its future pipeline — but it only matters if it converts into approved, profitable drugs.

---

## 📊 REAL WORLD: "The USFDA Inspection — Pharma's Defining Risk"

For Indian pharma exporters, the US FDA is both gatekeeper and judge.

To sell in the lucrative US market, their manufacturing plants must pass FDA inspections.

Over the years, several Indian pharma companies have received warning letters or import alerts on specific plants, citing quality concerns.

Such actions can block products from the US market — and the affected stocks have often fallen sharply on the news.

It's the sector's defining risk: a single regulatory event at one plant can outweigh strong financial results.

🧠 **Remember:** USFDA compliance is pharma's make-or-break risk — one adverse inspection can hit a stock harder than weak earnings.

---

## ⚠️ MISTAKE: "Treating a one-time exclusivity gain as recurring profit"

**The error:** A student sees a profit jump from a lucrative US drug launch and projects it forward forever.

**Why they make it:** The bumper quarter looks like a new, higher baseline.

**The correct understanding:** Some US gains come from limited exclusivity periods that fade as competitors enter — they're one-off, not recurring (the Module 8 quality-of-earnings link).

**The consequence:** Building a forecast on a temporary exclusivity windfall produces an inflated target that collapses when the gain disappears.

🧠 **Remember:** Separate the steady base business from one-off exclusivity gains — only the recurring part should drive your valuation.

# SESSION 11.6: Automobiles & Auto Ancillaries — The Cyclical Consumption Play

> "When the economy is booming and loans are cheap, India buys cars, bikes, and trucks. When times are tough, those purchases are the first to be postponed. Autos sit at the crossroads of consumption and the cycle — and a once-in-a-century shift to electric is rewriting the whole game."

You'll always buy soap. But a new car or bike? That can wait.

When the economy is strong and loans are cheap, vehicle sales surge.

When money is tight, those big purchases are postponed first.

So auto demand swings with the economy — it's a *cyclical* consumption play.

And right now, the entire industry is being reshaped by electric vehicles.

🧠 **Remember:** Vehicles are big-ticket, postponable purchases — so auto demand swings with the economy and financing.

## 💡 CONCEPT: "The Auto Sector's Moving Parts"

The auto sector has distinct segments, each with its own demand driver.

**Passenger vehicles (PVs):** Cars and SUVs — urban and aspirational demand.

**Two-wheelers (2W):** Bikes and scooters — mass-market, rural-sensitive.

**Commercial vehicles (CVs):** Trucks and buses — tied directly to economic activity (a leading indicator).

**Tractors:** Rural and monsoon-linked.

**Ancillaries** make the parts — components for all of the above.

🧠 **Remember:** Autos split into PVs, two-wheelers, CVs, and tractors — plus ancillaries — each driven by a different demand engine.

## 🔬 DEEP DIVE: "What Drives Autos — and Its Signature Metrics"

To analyse autos, watch these drivers and KPIs.

- **Premiumisation:** A shift toward SUVs and higher-end models lifts the average selling price (ASP).
- **Operating leverage:** High fixed costs mean profits swing sharply with volumes — great up, painful down.
- **Signature metrics:** Volume growth, ASP/realisation, market share, EBITDA margin, and (increasingly) EV penetration.
- **The big shift:** The transition to electric vehicles — a structural disruption for makers, ancillaries, and the whole supply chain.

🧠 **Remember:** Autos are driven by consumption, financing, fuel, and premiumisation — with the EV transition reshaping everything.

## 🔢 FORMULA: "Auto Revenue = Volume × ASP"

**Formula:** Revenue = Volume (units sold) × Average Selling Price (ASP)

| Variable | Meaning |
|----------|---------|
| Volume | Number of vehicles sold |
| ASP | Average price per vehicle (realisation) |

**Example:** A company sells 5,00,000 vehicles at an average ₹8 lakh each.
Revenue = 5,00,000 × ₹8,00,000 = **₹40,000 crore**.
If buyers shift to pricier SUVs and ASP rises to ₹9 lakh at the same volume, revenue jumps to ₹45,000 cr.

**Signal:** Rising volume means → strong demand. Rising ASP means → premiumisation or pricing power, lifting revenue even without more units.

**Analyst Insight:** In recent years, much of the passenger-vehicle growth has come from *ASP* (the SUV shift), not just volumes — so analysts watch the mix, not only unit sales.

🧠 **Remember:** Auto revenue is volume times price — and a richer mix (more SUVs) can lift revenue even when unit sales are flat.

# SESSION 11.7: Commodity Cyclicals — Metals, Cement, Oil & Gas, and Energy

> "Steel, cement, and oil companies don't really control their own destiny — global commodity prices do. These are the great cyclicals: they print money at the top of the cycle and bleed at the bottom. Analysing them is less about the company and more about the commodity and the cycle."

---

## 🎣 HOOK: "Companies whose profits are decided by a price they don't control"

A steel company can run brilliantly — yet its profit is set largely by the global steel price.

A cement maker's fortunes ride on demand cycles and energy costs.

An oil refiner lives and dies by crude prices and refining margins.

These companies don't fully control their selling price; the *commodity* does.

So analysing them means analysing the commodity and the cycle as much as the company.

🧠 **Remember:** For commodity cyclicals, the global commodity price — not the company — often decides the profit.

---

## 💡 CONCEPT: "Why Commodity Businesses Are Cyclical"

Commodities — steel, aluminium, cement, crude oil — are largely undifferentiated products.

When demand is high, prices spike, and producers earn fat profits.

Those profits attract new capacity, which eventually creates a glut.

The glut crashes prices, profits collapse, and weak players struggle.

This boom-bust rhythm is the eternal commodity cycle.

🧠 **Remember:** Commodities are undifferentiated, so prices boom and bust with demand and capacity — driving brutal cycles.

---

## 🔬 DEEP DIVE: "Analysing the Commodity Cyclicals"

These heavy, capital-intensive sectors share a common analytical lens.

- **Commodity prices:** The single biggest driver — global steel, aluminium, crude, or cement prices set the margin.
- **Cost-curve position:** The lowest-cost producer survives downturns and thrives in upturns — cost leadership is the moat.
- **Capacity & utilisation:** New capacity (or its absence) shapes the cycle; high utilisation signals tight markets.
- **Capital intensity & leverage:** Huge plants need huge capital and often debt — dangerous at the bottom of the cycle.
- **Sub-sectors:** Metals (global prices, China demand), cement (regional, energy costs), oil & gas (crude prices, refining margins, government control), power (regulated returns, the renewable shift).

🧠 **Remember:** Commodity cyclicals turn on commodity prices, cost-curve position, capacity cycles, and heavy capital — and the low-cost producer wins.

---

## 🔢 FORMULA: "EBITDA per Tonne"

**Formula:** EBITDA per Tonne = EBITDA ÷ Volume (tonnes sold)

| Variable | Meaning |
|----------|---------|
| EBITDA | Operating profit before depreciation, interest, tax |
| Volume | Tonnes of steel, cement, etc. sold |

**Example:** EBITDA ₹6,000 cr on 100 lakh tonnes of cement.
EBITDA per Tonne = 6,000 cr ÷ 100 lakh tonnes = **₹6,000 per tonne**.

**Signal:** A high or rising EBITDA per tonne means → strong pricing and cost control (good part of the cycle). A falling one means → margin pressure from weak prices or rising costs.

**Analyst Insight:** EBITDA per tonne is the signature metric for steel and cement — it strips out volume and reveals the true per-unit profitability that swings hugely through the cycle.

🧠 **Remember:** EBITDA per tonne is the steel and cement analyst's favourite gauge — it shows per-unit profitability, which booms and busts with the cycle.

---

## 📊 REAL WORLD: "The Steel Cycle — Riding Global Prices"

Steel is the classic commodity cyclical, and Indian majors like Tata Steel and JSW Steel ride the global cycle.

When global steel prices and demand are strong (often linked to China), their realisations and EBITDA per tonne surge, and profits boom.

When prices fall, EBITDA per tonne shrinks and the same companies can swing toward losses — especially the higher-cost, more leveraged ones.

The lowest-cost producers fare best through the cycle, because they stay profitable even when prices drop.

It's a vivid reminder that for commodities, the cycle and the cost curve matter as much as the company.

🧠 **Remember:** Steelmakers boom and bleed with global prices — and the lowest-cost producer survives the downturns best.

---

## ⚠️ MISTAKE: "Valuing a cyclical on its peak-cycle earnings"

**The error:** A student values a steel or cement company on its bumper peak-cycle profits, applying a normal multiple.

**Why they make it:** Peak earnings make the stock look cheap on a trailing P/E.

**The correct understanding:** Peak commodity earnings are temporary — they normalise (or crash) as the cycle turns; you must use mid-cycle or normalised earnings (the Module 10 link).

**The consequence:** Paying up for peak earnings, right before the commodity cycle rolls over, is the single most common way to lose money in cyclicals.

🧠 **Remember:** Never value a commodity cyclical on peak earnings — use mid-cycle, normalised numbers, or the low P/E becomes a trap.

## 🔬 DEEP DIVE: "The Signature Metrics of the New-Economy Sectors"

Each of these sectors has its own KPIs and dynamics.

- **Telecom:** ARPU, subscriber base, data usage, and heavy 5G/spectrum capex; consolidated to a few players (TRAI/DoT regulated).

🧠 **Remember:** ARPU for telecom, China+1 for chemicals, SSSG for retail, and unit economics for new-age — each sector, its own scorecard.

---

## 🔢 FORMULA: "ARPU (Average Revenue Per User)"

**Formula:** ARPU = Total Revenue ÷ Number of Subscribers (per period)

| Variable | Meaning |
|----------|---------|
| Total Revenue | Revenue from the subscriber base in the period |
| Number of Subscribers | Total active users |

**Example:** A telecom operator earns ₹6,000 cr in a month from 30 crore subscribers.
ARPU = ₹6,000 cr ÷ 30 cr = **₹200 per user per month**.

**Signal:** Rising ARPU means → customers are paying more (tariff hikes, data upgrades) — a key profitability driver. Falling ARPU means → price wars or downgrading.

**Analyst Insight:** Telecom is so capital-heavy that small ARPU changes swing profits massively — which is why analysts watch ARPU and tariff moves above almost everything else.

🧠 **Remember:** ARPU is telecom's lifeblood — in a high-fixed-cost business, even a small rise per user flows powerfully to profit.

---

## 📊 REAL WORLD: "Telecom's ARPU War and Recovery"

India's telecom sector was upended by an intense price war that crushed ARPUs for years.

Aggressive low pricing forced the industry to consolidate down to a few large players, like Bharti Airtel and Reliance Jio.

Once competition rationalised, operators began raising tariffs, lifting ARPU and improving profitability.

Because telecom is so capital-intensive, these ARPU improvements flowed powerfully to the bottom line.

It's the clearest example of why ARPU — not just subscriber numbers — is the metric that decides telecom profits.

🧠 **Remember:** Telecom's brutal price war crushed ARPU, then consolidation and tariff hikes revived it — ARPU is the whole story.

---

**The error:** A student sees a new-age company posting losses and concludes it has no value.

**Why they make it:** They apply a profit-based lens (like P/E) that simply doesn't fit an early-stage platform.

**The correct understanding:** Many platforms invest heavily upfront to capture a market; the question is *unit economics* and the credible path to profitability, not current losses.

**The consequence:** Writing off a strong platform purely for current losses — or, conversely, blindly cheering one with broken unit economics — both come from using the wrong lens.

🧠 **Remember:** Judge a new-age company by its unit economics and path to profit — not by whether it's profitable *today*.

## INTERVIEW QUESTIONS

### 🎙️ IQ1: "How would you value a loss-making new-age technology company?"
*New-age / valuation round — Kotak Securities, Nuvama, Axis Capital.*

**Answer Framework:**
- I wouldn't use P/E, since there are no profits yet to value.
- I'd focus on unit economics — contribution margin per order or per user.
- I'd assess GMV, take rate, and the trend toward profitability.
- I'd use metrics like Price-to-Sales and look at the size of the addressable market.
- The key question is whether there's a credible, improving path to profitability.

**🔑 Power Phrase:** "I value a new-age company on its unit economics and path to profit, not its current losses — the question is whether each transaction is getting more profitable."

**⚠️ Trap:** Applying P/E or dismissing it for being loss-making. Unit economics and the path to profit are the right lens.

---

### 🎙️ IQ2: "Why is ARPU so critical to a telecom company?"
*Telecom / sector round — DSP, ICICI Securities, IIFL.*

**Answer Framework:**
- Telecom is extremely capital-intensive, with huge fixed costs in networks and spectrum.
- Once the network is built, additional revenue per user flows heavily to profit.
- So small changes in ARPU swing profitability dramatically.
- ARPU rises with tariff hikes and customers upgrading to higher data plans.
- It matters more than raw subscriber numbers, because low-paying users add little profit.

**🔑 Power Phrase:** "In telecom, the network's already paid for — so every extra rupee of ARPU drops almost straight to profit; it's the metric that decides everything."

**⚠️ Trap:** Focusing on subscriber count alone. A large but low-ARPU base can be far less profitable than a smaller, high-ARPU one.

---

### 🎙️ IQ3: "What's driving the structural growth in India's specialty chemicals sector?"
*Chemicals / sector round — Edelweiss, Systematix, Motilal Oswal.*

**Answer Framework:**
- The global "China+1" trend is a key driver, as buyers diversify supply chains.
- India is positioned to win some of that shifting demand, boosting exports.
- Strong R&D and process capabilities let players move into higher-value chemicals.
- Companies are adding capacity to capture the growing export opportunity.
- So I'd assess a company's export mix, R&D, capacity expansion, and customer relationships.

**🔑 Power Phrase:** "China+1 is a structural tailwind for Indian chemicals — the winners are those with the R&D and capacity to capture the demand shifting their way."

**⚠️ Trap:** Treating chemicals as just another cyclical. The China+1 structural growth story is the distinctive driver here.
