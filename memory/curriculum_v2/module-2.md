# SESSION 2.1: The Primary Market — How Companies Raise Money

> "When you buy a share in an IPO, the company gets your money. Buy that same share the next day, and it gets nothing. Why does that one-day gap change everything?"

---

## 🎣 HOOK: "The Day a Company Goes Public"

A private company is owned by a handful of people — founders, family, a few investors.

An IPO changes that overnight. Suddenly, anyone with a demat account can own a slice.

In return, the company gets a flood of fresh cash to grow.

That single event — going from private to public — is the primary market in action.

🧠 Remember: The primary market is where a company sells its shares to the public for the very first time.

---

## 💡 CONCEPT: "The Primary Market"

The primary market is where new shares are created and sold directly by the company to investors. The money goes straight to the company.

This is how businesses raise capital to grow — building factories, repaying debt, or funding expansion.

Think of buying a brand-new car straight from the showroom. Your money goes to the manufacturer. That's the primary market. (Reselling that car later is the secondary market — next session.)

🧠 Remember: In the primary market, the company itself sells the shares and pockets the cash.

---

## 🔬 DEEP DIVE: "How an IPO Actually Works"

An IPO (Initial Public Offering) follows a clear sequence.

- The company files a DRHP (Draft Red Herring Prospectus) with SEBI — a giant disclosure document.
- SEBI reviews it for completeness and the company answers queries.
- A **price band** is set (e.g., ₹900–₹950 per share) for investors to bid within.
- Investors apply during the open window through their broker, blocking funds via ASBA/UPI.
- Shares are allotted, and the stock **lists** on the NSE and BSE a few days later.
- From listing day onward, all trading happens in the secondary market — the company is done collecting.

🧠 Remember: DRHP → SEBI review → price band → bidding → allotment → listing. That's the IPO journey.

---

## 📊 REAL WORLD: "LIC — India's Biggest IPO"

In May 2022, the Life Insurance Corporation of India raised around ₹21,000 crore — the largest IPO in Indian history at the time.

The government sold a small slice of its stake to the public, and that money flowed to the government as the selling shareholder.

But the stock listed at a discount to its issue price, disappointing many retail investors.

The lesson: a huge, hyped IPO is not the same thing as a guaranteed profit.

🧠 Remember: Even the biggest IPOs can list below their issue price — size and hype guarantee nothing.

---

## 🔢 FORMULA: "Subscription (Oversubscription) Ratio"

Formula: **Subscription Ratio = Total Shares Bid For / Total Shares Offered**

| Variable | Meaning |
|----------|---------|
| Shares Bid For | Total shares all investors applied for |
| Shares Offered | Total shares the company put up in the IPO |

Example: A company offers 1 crore shares. Investors collectively bid for 38 crore shares. Subscription = 38 / 1 = **38x** (38 times oversubscribed).

Signal: A high ratio → strong demand and likely a strong listing. A ratio below 1 → undersubscribed, a weak signal.

Analyst Insight: Heavy oversubscription means most retail applicants won't get a full allotment — shares are allotted by lottery. But high demand doesn't guarantee long-term value; it often reflects short-term hype, not fundamentals.

🧠 Remember: Oversubscription shows demand, not worth — a 38x IPO can still be a bad investment.

---

## ⚠️ MISTAKE: "Thinking the Company Gets Money Every Time a Share Trades"

The error: beginners assume that whenever a share is bought, the company receives the cash.

Why: it feels logical — buying a company's share should fund the company.

The truth: the company only gets money in the primary market (the IPO/fresh issue). After listing, shares trade between investors, and the company gets nothing from those trades.

Confuse this in an interview and you reveal you don't understand the most basic market structure.

🧠 Remember: The company is paid only once — at issue. Everything after that is investors trading with each other.

## 🎙️ INTERVIEW QUESTIONS

### 🎙️ IQ1: "What's the difference between the primary and secondary market?"
*Entry-level technical — a guaranteed opener at ICICI Securities, Kotak, and HDFC Securities.*

**Answer Framework:**
- The primary market is where a company issues new shares directly to investors, as in an IPO.
- The money raised flows straight to the company to fund growth or repay debt.
- The secondary market is where those existing shares trade between investors afterwards.
- In the secondary market, the company receives nothing — investors simply trade with each other.
- So the primary market raises capital; the secondary market provides liquidity.

**🔑 Power Phrase:** "The primary market funds the company; the secondary market funds the exit."

**⚠️ Trap:** Don't say the company keeps earning from every trade. It's paid only once — at issue.

### 🎙️ IQ2: "Walk me through what happens in an IPO from start to listing."
*Associate level — a process question at Nuvama, Axis Capital, and investment banking-linked desks.*

**Answer Framework:**
- The company files a DRHP with SEBI, disclosing financials, risks, and use of funds.
- After SEBI's review and any clarifications, a price band is set for the offer.
- Investors apply during the open window through their brokers, blocking funds via UPI or ASBA.
- Shares are then allotted — often by lottery if the issue is oversubscribed.
- Finally, the stock lists on the NSE and BSE, and all further trading is in the secondary market.

**🔑 Power Phrase:** "An IPO is the bridge a company crosses from private ownership to public accountability."

**⚠️ Trap:** Don't forget SEBI's review step or the allotment stage — skipping them makes the answer look incomplete.

### 🎙️ IQ3: "An IPO is oversubscribed 40 times. What does that tell you, and what doesn't it tell you?"
*Associate or lateral level — a judgement question at DSP and Edelweiss.*

**Answer Framework:**
- It tells me there's very strong short-term demand for the shares on offer.
- It also signals a likely strong listing pop, at least on day one.
- What it doesn't tell me is whether the company is fundamentally sound or fairly priced.
- Oversubscription is often driven by hype, grey-market premiums, and momentum, not analysis.
- I'd still value the business on its own merits before forming any view.

**🔑 Power Phrase:** "Oversubscription measures appetite, not quality — and I invest in quality."

**⚠️ Trap:** Don't equate demand with value. The interviewer is checking if you can separate hype from fundamentals.

# SESSION 2.2: The Secondary Market — Where Shares Change Hands

> "More than 99% of all share trading in India happens in a market where the company earns nothing. So why does it matter so much?"

---

## 🎣 HOOK: "The Market That Never Touches the Company"

Once a share lists, the company steps back. From then on, the share belongs to whoever buys it.

You buy from another investor. You sell to another investor. The company watches from the sidelines.

This is the secondary market — and it's where almost all the action is.

The NSE and BSE exist to make this trading fast, fair, and instant.

🧠 Remember: The secondary market is where investors trade existing shares with each other — the company isn't involved.

---

## 💡 CONCEPT: "The Secondary Market"

The secondary market is where shares that already exist are bought and sold between investors. India's two big stock exchanges — the NSE and BSE — host this trading.

Its real job is **liquidity**: the ability to turn your shares into cash quickly, whenever you want.

Think of OLX or a used-car market. The original maker isn't involved. Buyers and sellers meet and agree a price. The exchange is just the trusted marketplace.

🧠 Remember: The secondary market gives shares liquidity — you can sell whenever you want, to whoever's buying.

---

## 🔬 DEEP DIVE: "How a Stock Exchange Works"

A stock exchange is a high-speed matching engine.

- Buyers place buy orders; sellers place sell orders, all routed through brokers.
- The exchange matches a buyer to a seller at an agreed price, in milliseconds.
- Shares are held electronically in your **demat account** (via NSDL or CDSL), not as paper.
- A **broker** (like Zerodha or Groww) is your licensed gateway to the exchange.
- The exchange publishes live prices so everyone sees the same number at the same time.
- Without this system, you'd have to find a buyer yourself and verify they actually pay.

🧠 Remember: The exchange matches buyers and sellers instantly, and your shares live in a demat account.

---

## 📊 REAL WORLD: "The NSE — A Global Heavyweight"

India's National Stock Exchange has, for several years running, been the world's largest derivatives exchange by the number of contracts traded.

Millions of trades flow through it every single day, matched in fractions of a second.

This deep liquidity is why a large investor can buy or sell crores of rupees of Reliance or HDFC Bank shares without much difficulty.

A liquid market is a healthy market — it's what lets capital move freely.

🧠 Remember: Deep liquidity on exchanges like the NSE lets big money move in and out smoothly.

---

## 🔢 FORMULA: "Market Capitalisation"

Formula: **Market Cap = Share Price × Number of Shares Outstanding**

| Variable | Meaning |
|----------|---------|
| Share Price | Current price of one share in the secondary market |
| Shares Outstanding | Total number of shares the company has issued |

Example: A company has 100 crore shares trading at ₹500 each. Market Cap = 500 × 100 crore = **₹50,000 crore**.

Signal: A higher market cap → a larger company by market value. Companies are grouped as large-cap, mid-cap, or small-cap based on this.

Analyst Insight: Market cap — not share price — tells you a company's true size. A ₹100 share can belong to a far bigger company than a ₹5,000 share, depending on the number of shares. Beginners obsess over price; analysts look at market cap.

🧠 Remember: A company's real size is its market cap, not its share price.

---

## ⚠️ MISTAKE: "Believing a Low Share Price Means a Cheap Company"

The error: beginners think a ₹50 stock is "cheaper" than a ₹5,000 stock.

Why: in everyday life, a lower price tag means cheaper.

The truth: share price alone is meaningless without the number of shares. A ₹50 stock can belong to a far larger, more expensive company than a ₹5,000 stock.

In an interview, calling a stock "cheap" based only on its price marks you as a complete beginner. Value is about market cap and earnings, not the sticker price.

🧠 Remember: A low price doesn't mean cheap — always think in market cap, not share price.

### 🎙️ IQ1: "What is market capitalisation, and why is it more useful than share price?"
*Entry-level technical — extremely common at ICICI Securities, Kotak, and Motilal Oswal.*

**Answer Framework:**
- Market cap is the share price multiplied by the total number of shares outstanding.
- It measures the total market value of the entire company, not just one share.
- Share price alone is misleading because companies have very different share counts.
- A ₹100 stock with billions of shares can be larger than a ₹5,000 stock with very few.
- That's why companies are classified as large, mid, or small cap by market cap, not price.

**🔑 Power Phrase:** "Share price tells you the cost of one slice; market cap tells you the size of the whole pizza."

**⚠️ Trap:** Don't call a stock cheap or expensive based on price alone — it instantly signals a beginner.

### 🎙️ IQ2: "Why does liquidity matter in the secondary market?"
*Associate level — a market-structure question at Nuvama and DSP.*

**Answer Framework:**
- Liquidity is the ease of buying or selling a share without moving its price much.
- High liquidity lets large investors enter and exit positions smoothly.
- It tightens the gap between buy and sell prices, lowering trading costs.
- Illiquid stocks are risky — you may be stuck unable to sell at a fair price.
- That's why liquidity itself is a factor I weigh before recommending a stock.

**🔑 Power Phrase:** "Liquidity is the freedom to change your mind without paying a heavy price."

**⚠️ Trap:** Don't ignore liquidity when discussing small-cap ideas — it's often their biggest hidden risk.

### 🎙️ IQ3: "If a company's share price doubles overnight on a stock split, has the company become more valuable?"
*Associate or lateral level — a conceptual trap question at Edelweiss and Axis Capital.*

**Answer Framework:**
- A stock split changes the number of shares, not the company's total value.
- In a split, the price falls proportionally as share count rises, so market cap is unchanged.
- The opposite — a price rise with the same share count — would change market cap.
- Splits are usually done to make shares more affordable and improve liquidity.
- So the company's underlying value is exactly the same the morning after a split.

**🔑 Power Phrase:** "A stock split slices the pizza into more pieces — it doesn't make the pizza bigger."

**⚠️ Trap:** Don't confuse a higher number of shares with higher value. Market cap is what actually matters.

# SESSION 2.3: Who's Who in the Market — The Players

> "On any given day, a foreign investor in New York and a college student in Delhi are both moving the same Nifty. Who actually holds the power?"

---

## 🎣 HOOK: "The Players at the Table"

The stock market isn't one crowd. It's different players with very different sizes, goals, and time horizons.

A retail investor buys a few shares. A foreign fund moves thousands of crores in a single click.

When these giants buy or sell together, the whole market shifts.

Knowing who's playing helps you read why prices move.

🧠 Remember: The market is many different players — and the biggest ones can move prices on their own.

---

## 💡 CONCEPT: "The Market Participants"

The market is made up of several types of players, each with different firepower.

- **Retail investors:** individuals like you, trading small amounts.
- **FIIs (Foreign Institutional Investors):** big global funds investing in India.
- **DIIs (Domestic Institutional Investors):** Indian mutual funds, insurers, pension funds.
- **Promoters:** the founders/owners who hold large stakes.

Think of a cricket stadium. Retail investors are individual fans. FIIs and DIIs are the big sponsors who can change the game. Promoters own the team itself.

🧠 Remember: Retail, FIIs, DIIs, and promoters — four players, four very different levels of power.

---

## 🔬 DEEP DIVE: "How the Big Players Differ"

Each player behaves differently, and that shapes the market.

- FIIs bring huge capital but can exit fast when global conditions sour — making them volatile.
- DIIs (mutual funds, LIC) often act as a counterweight, buying when FIIs sell.
- Retail investors are growing fast in India, increasingly via SIPs into mutual funds.
- Promoters' actions are watched closely — buying signals confidence, pledging shares signals risk.
- Institutions move markets with size; retail moves it through sheer collective numbers.
- Tracking who is buying and selling reveals the mood and direction of the market.

🧠 Remember: FIIs bring volatile global money; DIIs steady the ship; promoters' moves signal insider confidence.

---

## 📊 REAL WORLD: "When FIIs Sell, DIIs Catch the Ball"

In several recent market corrections, foreign investors (FIIs) pulled large sums out of Indian equities, spooked by global rate hikes or geopolitical risk.

Yet the Indian market didn't crash as hard as expected.

Why? Domestic investors (DIIs) — powered by relentless monthly SIP inflows from millions of Indians — kept buying.

This rising domestic firepower has made the Indian market far more resilient than a decade ago.

🧠 Remember: Strong domestic SIP money now cushions the market when foreign investors flee.

---

## 🔢 FORMULA: "Free Float"

Formula: **Free-Float Market Cap = Total Market Cap × Free-Float Factor**

| Variable | Meaning |
|----------|---------|
| Total Market Cap | Price × all shares outstanding |
| Free-Float Factor | The % of shares actually available for public trading (not held by promoters or locked in) |

Example: A company has a market cap of ₹1,00,000 crore. Promoters hold 60%, so the free float is 40%. Free-Float Market Cap = 1,00,000 × 0.40 = **₹40,000 crore**.

Signal: A higher free float → more shares available to trade → usually more liquid. A low free float → fewer tradable shares → prices can swing sharply.

Analyst Insight: Indices like the Nifty and Sensex weight companies by free-float market cap, not total market cap. So a company where promoters hold a huge stake carries less index weight than its full size suggests.

🧠 Remember: Free float is the slice of a company actually available for the public to trade.

---

## ⚠️ MISTAKE: "Blindly Following FII Activity"

The error: beginners treat "FIIs are buying" as an automatic signal to buy.

Why: foreign funds are seen as smart, well-resourced money.

The truth: FIIs are driven by global factors — US rates, currency, their own redemptions — that may have nothing to do with a specific Indian company's value.

Following FII flows blindly, without your own analysis, means you're reacting to forces you don't understand. Use flows as context, never as a command.

🧠 Remember: FII activity is useful context, not a buy or sell instruction — always do your own analysis.

## 🎙️ INTERVIEW QUESTIONS

### 🎙️ IQ1: "What's the difference between FIIs and DIIs, and how do they affect the market?"
*Entry-level technical — common at ICICI Securities, Kotak, and macro-aware desks.*

**Answer Framework:**
- FIIs are foreign institutional investors — large global funds investing in Indian equities.
- DIIs are domestic institutions — Indian mutual funds, insurers, and pension funds.
- FIIs bring big capital but can exit quickly on global cues, adding volatility.
- DIIs, powered by steady SIP inflows, often buy when FIIs sell, providing a counterbalance.
- Tracking both flows gives a read on the market's mood and likely direction.

**🔑 Power Phrase:** "FIIs bring the speed, DIIs bring the stability — and watching both tells you the market's mood."

**⚠️ Trap:** Don't treat FII selling as automatically bearish — DII buying frequently absorbs it.

### 🎙️ IQ2: "Why do indices use free-float market cap instead of total market cap?"
*Associate level — an index-construction question at Nuvama, DSP, and ETF-linked desks.*

**Answer Framework:**
- Free float counts only the shares actually available for public trading.
- Promoter and government holdings are locked away and don't trade freely.
- Weighting by free float reflects the shares investors can genuinely buy and sell.
- It prevents a promoter-heavy company from dominating an index beyond its tradable size.
- This makes indices like the Nifty and Sensex more investable and representative.

# SESSION 2.4: Indices — How We Measure the Market

> "The news says 'Sensex up 500 points today.' But you can't buy the Sensex. So what actually went up?"

---

## 🎣 HOOK: "The Number Everyone Quotes"

Every evening, the news announces the Nifty and Sensex levels. They're treated like the market's heartbeat.

But you can't actually buy "the Sensex." It's not a company. It's not a share.

It's a single number that summarises how a basket of top companies did.

Understand how it's built, and the daily market headline finally makes sense.

🧠 Remember: An index is a single number that tracks how a chosen basket of stocks performed.

---

## 💡 CONCEPT: "What an Index Is"

A stock market index is a basket of selected stocks, tracked as one number, to represent the market or a part of it.

The Sensex tracks 30 large companies on the BSE. The Nifty 50 tracks 50 large companies on the NSE.

Think of a school's average marks. You don't read every student's report card. One average number tells you how the whole class did. An index is the market's average.

🧠 Remember: An index is the market's report-card average — one number for a whole basket of stocks.

---

## 🔬 DEEP DIVE: "How an Index Is Built"

Indices follow clear construction rules.

- A set number of stocks is chosen — 50 for the Nifty, 30 for the Sensex.
- Stocks are selected for size, liquidity, and how well they represent the market.
- Each stock is weighted by its **free-float market cap** — bigger free float, bigger weight.
- So a giant like Reliance or HDFC Bank influences the index far more than a smaller member.
- The basket is reviewed periodically; weak stocks are dropped and stronger ones added.
- The index level moves as its member stocks' prices move, blended by their weights.

🧠 Remember: An index weights its members by free-float market cap, so the biggest companies move it most.

---

## 📊 REAL WORLD: "The Sensex's Long Climb"

The BSE Sensex started in 1978–79 with a base value of just 100 points.

Over the decades, as Indian companies grew and the economy expanded, it crossed thousands, then tens of thousands of points.

Each milestone reflected the rising value of India's biggest listed companies.

The Sensex's journey is, in effect, the story of corporate India's growth told in a single number.

🧠 Remember: A long-term rising index reflects the long-term growth of a country's biggest companies.

---

## 🔢 FORMULA: "Weight of a Stock in an Index"

Formula: **Stock Weight (%) = (Stock's Free-Float Market Cap / Total Free-Float Market Cap of Index) × 100**

| Variable | Meaning |
|----------|---------|
| Stock's Free-Float Market Cap | Free-float value of that one stock |
| Total Free-Float Market Cap | Combined free float of all stocks in the index |

Example: A stock has a free-float market cap of ₹5 lakh crore in an index whose total free-float market cap is ₹100 lakh crore. Weight = (5 / 100) × 100 = **5%**.

Signal: A higher weight → that stock's price moves swing the index more. A lower weight → less impact.

Analyst Insight: A handful of heavyweight stocks often dominate the Nifty. So on some days the index can rise even when most stocks fall — simply because the few large-weight names went up. The "market" and "most stocks" aren't always the same thing.

🧠 Remember: A stock's index weight decides how much its movement sways the whole index.

---

## ⚠️ MISTAKE: "Thinking a Rising Index Means All Stocks Rose"

The error: beginners assume that if the Nifty is up, their stocks must be up too.

Why: the index is treated as "the whole market."

The truth: the index is weighted. A few heavyweight stocks can push it up while the majority of stocks actually fall that day.

Saying "the market was green so everything rose" in an interview shows you don't understand weighting. Always check market breadth, not just the index level.

🧠 Remember: A green index doesn't mean every stock rose — heavyweights can carry it alone.

# SESSION 2.5: How a Trade Actually Happens — Orders, Settlement & SEBI

> "You tap 'Buy' on your phone and the share appears seconds later. But the share doesn't truly become yours that instant. So what's really happening behind the screen?"

---

## 🎣 HOOK: "The Two-Second Illusion"

You tap 'Buy.' The app says 'Order executed.' It feels instant and complete.

But behind that tap, a whole machine springs into action — matching, clearing, settling, verifying.

The share isn't legally yours the very second you tap. There's a settlement process.

Understanding it is what separates a trader from someone who just clicks buttons.

🧠 Remember: A trade looks instant, but a clearing-and-settlement machine works behind every tap.

---

## 💡 CONCEPT: "Orders and Settlement"

When you trade, you place an **order**. The exchange matches it, then the trade is **settled** — shares and money actually change hands.

The two most common order types:
- **Market order:** buy/sell immediately at the best available price.
- **Limit order:** buy/sell only at a price you set, or better.

Think of ordering food. A market order is "give me whatever's ready, now." A limit order is "I'll buy a samosa only if it's ₹15 or less."

🧠 Remember: A market order prioritises speed; a limit order prioritises price.

---

## 🔬 DEEP DIVE: "What Happens After You Hit Buy"

The journey from tap to ownership has clear stages.

- Your **order** goes from your broker's app to the exchange's matching engine.
- The exchange **matches** it with a counterparty and confirms the trade.
- A **clearing corporation** steps in to guarantee both sides will honour the deal.
- **Settlement** then transfers shares to your demat account and money to the seller.
- India settles on a **T+1** basis — one working day after the trade date.
- This guarantee system means you never have to worry whether the stranger on the other side will pay.

🧠 Remember: Order → match → clearing → settlement, completed one working day later under T+1.

---

## 📊 REAL WORLD: "India's Leap to Faster Settlement"

For years, Indian markets settled trades on a T+2 basis — two days after the trade.

In phases through to early 2023, India moved fully to **T+1 settlement**, becoming one of the world's fastest major markets.

SEBI then began piloting an even faster optional **T+0** (same-day) settlement.

Faster settlement means you get your shares or money sooner, and risk in the system is reduced.

🧠 Remember: India has moved to T+1 settlement and is pioneering even faster same-day settlement.

---

## 🔢 FORMULA: "Bid-Ask Spread"

Formula: **Bid-Ask Spread = Ask Price − Bid Price**

| Variable | Meaning |
|----------|---------|
| Bid Price | The highest price a buyer is willing to pay |
| Ask Price | The lowest price a seller is willing to accept |

Example: For a stock, the bid is ₹100.00 and the ask is ₹100.50. Spread = 100.50 − 100.00 = **₹0.50** (0.5% of price).

Signal: A narrow spread → high liquidity and low trading cost. A wide spread → low liquidity and a costlier trade.

Analyst Insight: The spread is a hidden cost of trading. In highly liquid stocks like Reliance the spread is tiny, but in illiquid small-caps it can be large — quietly eating into your returns every time you trade.

🧠 Remember: The bid-ask spread is a hidden cost — wide spreads silently eat your returns.

---

## ⚠️ MISTAKE: "Always Using Market Orders in Illiquid Stocks"

The error: beginners always use market orders for the speed and convenience.

Why: it executes instantly, which feels efficient.

The truth: in an illiquid stock with a wide spread, a market order can fill at a far worse price than expected — you might buy much higher than the last traded price.

This mistake can quietly cost real money. In thinly traded stocks, a limit order protects you from nasty price surprises.

🧠 Remember: In illiquid stocks, use limit orders — a market order can fill at a shockingly bad price.

# SESSION 2.6: What Moves Stock Prices

> "Two companies report the exact same profit growth on the same day. One stock soars 10%, the other crashes 8%. What on earth just happened?"

---

## 🎣 HOOK: "Expectations vs Reality"

A company can report a record profit and watch its stock *fall*.

How? Because the market had already expected even more. Reality fell short of expectations.

Stock prices don't move on news alone. They move on news *versus what was expected*.

This single idea explains a thousand confusing market days.

🧠 Remember: Prices move on the gap between reality and expectations, not on the news by itself.

---

## 💡 CONCEPT: "What Drives Prices"

In the short term, a stock's price is set by supply and demand — more buyers than sellers pushes it up, and vice versa.

But what drives that buying and selling? Mainly: earnings, news, expectations, and sentiment.

Think of a cricketer's reputation. One great innings (earnings) helps. But what really moves their value is whether they beat the *expectations* set for them.

🧠 Remember: Short-term prices follow supply and demand; that demand is driven by earnings, news, and expectations.

---

## 🔬 DEEP DIVE: "The Real Price Drivers"

Several forces push prices around.

- **Earnings:** results beating or missing expectations cause the sharpest moves.
- **News:** new contracts, regulations, management changes, lawsuits — all shift demand.
- **Macro factors:** interest rates, inflation, and global cues move whole markets at once.
- **Sentiment:** fear and greed can detach prices from fundamentals for a while.
- **Flows:** big FII or DII buying/selling can move prices regardless of news.
- Over the long run, though, prices follow earnings and cash flows — the fundamentals win.

🧠 Remember: Short term, sentiment and flows rule; long term, earnings and cash flow always win.

---

## 📊 REAL WORLD: "Good Results, Falling Stock"

It happens often on Indian results days: a company posts solid profit growth, yet its stock drops sharply the same day.

The reason is usually a miss on expectations — perhaps margins disappointed, or management gave weak guidance for the year ahead.

The market is forward-looking. It prices the future, not just the quarter that's already done.

So a 'good' result can still be a 'bad' result if it falls short of the bar set for it.

🧠 Remember: The market reacts to the future outlook, not just the results already reported.

---

## 🔢 FORMULA: "Total Stock Return"

Formula: **Total Return (%) = [(Sell Price − Buy Price) + Dividend] / Buy Price × 100**

| Variable | Meaning |
|----------|---------|
| Sell Price | Price at which you sell |
| Buy Price | Price at which you bought |
| Dividend | Any dividend received while holding |

Example: You buy a stock at ₹200, sell at ₹240, and receive a ₹5 dividend. Total Return = [(240 − 200) + 5] / 200 × 100 = **22.5%**.

Signal: A higher total return → better performance. Including dividends gives the true, complete return.

Analyst Insight: Many beginners track only price gains and ignore dividends. For high-dividend stocks like ITC or many PSUs, dividends form a big chunk of total return. Always measure total return, not just price change.

🧠 Remember: Real return includes dividends, not just the change in price.

---

## ⚠️ MISTAKE: "Believing Prices Always Reflect Fundamentals"

The error: beginners assume a rising price means a great company and a falling price means a bad one.

Why: it's intuitive — price feels like a verdict on quality.

The truth: in the short term, prices are swayed by sentiment, hype, and flows that can detach from fundamentals entirely.

Treating short-term price as proof of value leads to buying hype and selling in panic. Price and value are different things — especially in the short run.

🧠 Remember: Short-term price is not proof of value — sentiment and flows distort it constantly.

**🎯 Objective:** Follow one real Indian listed company from its IPO to today, and produce a one-page "market profile" that shows you understand how it raised money, how it trades, and what moves it.

**🏢 Company Assigned:** Zomato Ltd (NSE: ZOMATO) — a well-documented recent IPO with rich, accessible public data, ideal for tracing the full journey.

## To-Do List

1. Find Zomato's IPO details (use moneycontrol, the NSE/BSE site, or its DRHP): the IPO year, the price band, the issue size in ₹, and how many times it was oversubscribed.
2. Note how much money the company actually raised in the primary market and what it said it would use the funds for.
3. Find the listing day price and compare it to the issue price — did it list at a premium or discount? Write one line on what that signalled.
4. Look up Zomato's current share price and number of shares outstanding, and calculate its current market capitalisation.
5. Classify it as large-cap, mid-cap, or small-cap based on that market cap.
6. Find the latest shareholding pattern: what percentage is held by promoters, FIIs, DIIs, and retail? Note the free-float percentage.
7. Pick one day in the last year when the stock moved sharply (up or down), and find the news or result behind it — was it an earnings surprise, news event, or macro move?
8. Using a recent one-year period, calculate the total return: price change plus any dividend (note Zomato may not pay one), divided by the starting price.
9. Write two sentences on what currently moves Zomato's stock the most — earnings, expectations, sentiment, or flows.
10. Write a 6-line "Market Profile" covering: IPO outcome, current market cap and category, ownership mix, and what drives the stock.

**📤 Deliverable:** A one-page market profile of Zomato — your 6-line summary plus the supporting numbers — written clearly enough for a friend with zero finance background to follow the share's full journey.

**⏱️ Time Estimate:** 4–6 hours.

**🏆 What Great Looks Like:**
1. Every figure (issue size, oversubscription, market cap, shareholding) is pulled from a real source, with the number and date clearly stated.
2. The sharp-move day is correctly linked to a real cause, showing you understand the difference between news and the expectations gap.
3. The final profile connects the primary market (IPO), the secondary market (trading and market cap), the players (ownership mix), and the price drivers into one clear story.
