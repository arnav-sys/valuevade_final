# SESSION 1.1: What Is Finance, Really?

> "Why would anyone pay ₹3 lakh crore for Reliance when its refineries, towers, and stores are 'only' worth a fraction of that on paper?"

---

## 🎣 HOOK: "The ₹100 That Shrinks"

Offer a friend ₹100 today or ₹100 next Diwali. Everyone grabs today's.

Why? Money has a time value.

A rupee today can be invested. It can earn. It can grow.

A rupee next year is just a promise.

Finance, stripped to its core, is the science of moving money across time and risk.

🧠 Remember: Finance is about the *future* value of money, not just the amount.

---

## 💡 CONCEPT: "What Finance Actually Is"

Finance is the management of money across time — raising it, investing it, and growing it.

Every company is a machine. Money goes in. The machine turns it into more money. Then someone decides what to do with the extra.

Your whole job as an analyst is judging whether that machine is worth buying.

Think of a juice shop. You put in ₹100 for oranges and a stall. You sell juice for ₹150. You keep ₹30 after costs. Finance asks one question: is this shop worth your money?

🧠 Remember: Finance = raising money, investing it, and judging whether it grows.

---

## 🔬 DEEP DIVE: "The Three Big Questions of Finance"

Every single finance decision answers one of three questions.

- **Investment:** Where should the money go — which projects, assets, or stocks?
- **Financing:** Where does the money come from — debt, equity, or retained profit?
- **Returns:** What do we do with what we earn — reinvest, pay dividends, or repay debt?
- Equity research lives inside Question 1 — judging where money *should* go.
- Every annual report is a company answering these three questions in public.
- Master these three lenses and any financial statement becomes readable.

🧠 Remember: All of finance reduces to three questions — invest, finance, return.

---

## 📊 REAL WORLD: "Zomato — Losing Money, Worth a Fortune"

Zomato listed on the NSE and BSE in July 2021.

It had never posted an annual profit. Yet investors valued it at over ₹1 lakh crore on listing day.

Why? They weren't paying for past profits. They were paying for future cash — millions of Indians ordering food on one app for the next twenty years.

Finance values the future, not the past. By FY24, Zomato turned profitable, partly proving the bet right.

🧠 Remember: Markets price the future a company can create, not the profit it has already made.

---

## 🔢 FORMULA: "Present Value — The Most Important Formula in Finance"

Formula: **PV = FV / (1 + r)^n**

| Variable | Meaning |
|----------|---------|
| PV | Value today |
| FV | The future amount of money |
| r | Discount rate (per year) |
| n | Number of years away |

Example: ₹100 received one year from now, at a 10% rate. PV = 100 / 1.10 = **₹90.9**. So ₹100 next year is worth about ₹91 today.

Signal: A higher r or higher n means → lower present value. A lower r means → future money is worth more today.

Analyst Insight: When the RBI raises interest rates, the present value of future profits drops. That's exactly why high-growth stocks like Zomato and Nykaa fall hardest when rates rise.

🧠 Remember: Every valuation is just future money pulled back to today's value.

---

## ⚠️ MISTAKE: "Confusing Profit With Cash"

The error: beginners assume a profitable company always has cash.

Why they make it: school teaches that profit equals success.

The truth: profit is an accounting opinion; cash is a fact. A company can show ₹100 crore profit on paper while its bank account runs dry — because customers haven't paid yet.

Say "profitable means safe" in an interview and you're finished. Plenty of profitable companies have gone bankrupt. They ran out of cash, not profit.

🧠 Remember: Profit is an opinion; cash is a fact.

## ❓ QUIZ

### Q1. ₹100 received one year from now, discounted at 10%, is worth approximately how much today?

a) ₹110
b) ₹100
c) ₹91
d) 90 paise

✅ Answer: c) ₹91
📝 Why: PV = 100 / 1.10 = 90.9 ≈ ₹91. Option (a) adds interest instead of removing it, (b) ignores time value entirely, and (d) misplaces the decimal.

### Q2. Why was Zomato valued at over ₹1 lakh crore despite never posting an annual profit at listing?

a) SEBI mandated the valuation
b) Investors were pricing its expected future cash flows
c) It had large current profits hidden in reserves
d) Its factories were worth that much

✅ Answer: b) Investors were pricing its expected future cash flows
📝 Why: Finance values the future, not the past. Zomato had no factories (c, d wrong), and SEBI does not set company valuations (a wrong).

### Q3. Which of these is NOT one of the three core questions of finance?

a) Where should the money be invested?
b) Where does the money come from?
c) What do we do with the returns?
d) Who is the company's auditor?

✅ Answer: d) Who is the company's auditor?
📝 Why: Investment, financing, and returns are the three core questions. The auditor matters for trust and verification, but it isn't one of finance's core decision questions.

### Q4. True or False: A company that reports a net profit is guaranteed to have cash in its bank account.

✅ Answer: False
📝 Why: Profit can include sales that haven't been collected in cash yet. Profitable companies can — and do — run out of cash and fail.

### Q5. Fill in the blank: The formula PV = FV / (1 + r)^n shows that as the discount rate r rises, the present value of future money ________.

✅ Answer: falls (decreases)
📝 Why: A larger denominator shrinks PV. This is precisely why rate hikes hurt high-growth stocks the most.

## 🎙️ INTERVIEW QUESTIONS

### 🎙️ IQ1: "Walk me through why a rupee today is worth more than a rupee a year from now."
*Entry-level analyst — first technical screen at firms like ICICI Securities and Motilal Oswal.*

**Answer Framework:**
- A rupee today can be invested immediately to earn a return — say 7% in a fixed deposit.
- That rupee becomes ₹1.07 in a year, so future money must be discounted to compare it fairly.
- This is the time value of money, captured by PV = FV / (1 + r)^n.
- At a 10% rate, ₹100 a year out is worth only about ₹91 today.
- This single idea underpins all of valuation — DCF, bonds, even your home-loan EMI.

**🔑 Power Phrase:** "Money has a time value — and every valuation I build is just future cash pulled back to today."

**⚠️ Trap:** Don't explain it only through inflation. Time value exists even with zero inflation, because of opportunity cost.

### 🎙️ IQ2: "How can a loss-making company be worth more than a profitable one? Give me an example."
*Research associate level — common at growth and tech-focused desks like Nuvama and Axis Capital.*

**Answer Framework:**
- Markets value future cash flows, not the current profit-and-loss line.
- A loss today, funded by investment in growth, can create far larger profits tomorrow.
- Zomato listed at over ₹1 lakh crore with no annual profit, pricing in two decades of food-delivery demand.
- A stable but no-growth firm can stay cheap because its future looks just like its present.
- The analyst's job is to judge whether that future is realistic — not to reject losses on sight.

**🔑 Power Phrase:** "I don't buy last year's profit — I buy the next decade's cash flows."

**⚠️ Trap:** Don't sound like you endorse every loss-making firm. Separate investment-driven losses from a structurally broken business.

### 🎙️ IQ3: "What's the difference between profit and cash, and why should I care as an analyst?"
*Entry to associate level — an extremely common opening technical at almost every Indian research firm.*

**Answer Framework:**
- Profit is an accounting figure; it can record a sale before the customer has actually paid.
- Cash is the real money in the bank — it pays salaries, debt, and dividends.
- A company can look profitable on paper yet be insolvent if its receivables keep piling up.
- This is exactly why the cash flow statement exists alongside the P&L.
- Many infrastructure and real-estate firms have shown profits while drowning in unpaid dues.

**🔑 Power Phrase:** "Profit is an opinion; cash is a fact — and I trust facts."

**⚠️ Trap:** Don't say they're "basically the same with a timing difference." That timing difference is the whole point, and it can be fatal.

# SESSION 1.2: Meet the Big Three — The Financial Statements

> "How can you judge whether a company is healthy, growing, and honest — using just three documents?"

---

## 🎣 HOOK: "Three Documents, One Truth"

Every listed company in India tells its entire financial story using just three documents.

Not thirty. Three.

Reliance uses three. TCS uses three. A tiny SME on NSE Emerge uses three.

The balance sheet. The income statement. The cash flow statement.

Learn to read these three, and no company can hide from you.

🧠 Remember: Three statements reveal everything that matters about a company's finances.

---

## 💡 CONCEPT: "The Big Three"

The three financial statements are the standard report card every company publishes. Each answers a different question.

- Balance Sheet → What does the company own and owe right now?
- Income Statement → Did it make money over the year?
- Cash Flow Statement → Where did the actual cash come from and go?

Think of checking a cricketer's fitness. The balance sheet is the body scan (current state). The income statement is the season's run tally (performance). The cash flow statement is the heart-rate monitor (real energy in and out).

🧠 Remember: Three statements, three questions — what you have, what you earned, where cash moved.

---

## 🔬 DEEP DIVE: "How the Three Statements Connect"

They are not three islands. They lock together.

- Net profit from the income statement flows into the balance sheet as retained earnings.
- That same net profit is also the starting line of the cash flow statement.
- Cash generated in the cash flow statement updates the cash figure on the balance sheet.
- Assets bought (capex) appear in the cash flow statement, then sit on the balance sheet.
- Change any one statement and the other two must change too.
- This single linkage is the foundation of every 3-statement model you'll build in Module 4.

🧠 Remember: The three statements are one connected system, not three separate files.

---

## 📊 REAL WORLD: "Infosys — Reading the Report Card"

Open Infosys's FY24 annual report on its investor relations page.

The balance sheet shows huge cash reserves and almost no debt. The income statement shows steady revenue with strong margins. The cash flow statement shows healthy operating cash — real money, not just paper profit.

Put the three together and the verdict is instant: Infosys is a cash-rich, profitable, low-risk machine.

No single statement could have told you that on its own.

🧠 Remember: Only all three statements together give the full picture of a company.

---

## 🔢 FORMULA: "The Accounting Equation — The Spine of the Balance Sheet"

Formula: **Assets = Liabilities + Equity**

| Variable | Meaning |
|----------|---------|
| Assets | Everything the company owns |
| Liabilities | Everything the company owes to others |
| Equity | What is left over for shareholders |

Example: A company owns ₹500 crore of assets and owes ₹200 crore to lenders. Equity = 500 − 200 = **₹300 crore** belongs to shareholders.

Signal: If liabilities grow faster than assets → equity shrinks → rising risk. Growing equity → wealth is being built.

Analyst Insight: This equation must *always* balance. If your model doesn't balance, you've made an error somewhere — and it's the very first thing a senior will check.

🧠 Remember: Assets = Liabilities + Equity — and it must always balance.

---

## ⚠️ MISTAKE: "Reading Only the Profit Number"

The error: beginners glance at net profit and stop right there.

Why: it's the headline in every news report — "Company X profit up 20%!"

The truth: profit alone is meaningless. Is the company drowning in debt (balance sheet)? Is that profit even real cash (cash flow)? You can't know from one number.

Judge a company on a single figure in an interview and you've marked yourself as a novice. Real analysts triangulate across all three statements.

🧠 Remember: One statement never tells the whole story — always read all three.

## 🎙️ INTERVIEW QUESTIONS

### 🎙️ IQ1: "Walk me through the three financial statements and how they connect."
*The single most common opening technical — asked at virtually every firm, from ICICI Securities to Kotak to Motilal Oswal.*

**Answer Framework:**
- The income statement shows revenue minus expenses to arrive at net profit over a period.
- The balance sheet is a point-in-time snapshot: assets equal liabilities plus equity.
- The cash flow statement reconciles profit to actual cash across operating, investing, and financing activities.
- They link: net profit flows to retained earnings on the balance sheet and also starts the cash flow statement; the ending cash then updates the balance sheet's cash line.
- Change one and all three move — which is exactly why a 3-statement model must always balance.

**🔑 Power Phrase:** "The three statements aren't three documents — they're one connected system."

**⚠️ Trap:** Don't describe them as independent. The interviewer is testing whether you understand the *linkages*, not the definitions.

### 🎙️ IQ2: "If you could pick only one of the three statements to assess a company, which would you choose and why?"
*Associate level — a judgement question at firms like DSP and Nuvama.*

**Answer Framework:**
- I'd choose the cash flow statement, because cash is the hardest number to fake.
- It reveals whether reported profit is actually converting into real money.
- Many frauds and failures show healthy profit but weak or negative operating cash.
- The trade-off is that I lose the snapshot of debt (balance sheet) and margins (income statement).
- In practice I'd never rely on one alone — but the cash flow statement is the truth serum.

**🔑 Power Phrase:** "If I'm allowed only one lie detector, I pick the cash flow statement."

**⚠️ Trap:** Don't pick the income statement just because profit feels familiar — that signals junior thinking.

### 🎙️ IQ3: "What does it mean if a company is profitable but has negative operating cash flow?"
*Associate or lateral level — a red-flag diagnostic question at Edelweiss and Axis Capital.*

**Answer Framework:**
- It means profit is being booked but cash isn't being collected.
- Common causes are customers not paying (rising receivables) or inventory piling up.
- It can be temporary in a fast-growing firm, or a serious red flag from aggressive revenue recognition.
- I'd check the trend over three to five years and compare receivable days against peers.
- Sustained profit with negative operating cash is a classic warning sign of trouble.

**🔑 Power Phrase:** "Profit you can't collect isn't profit — it's a promise."

**⚠️ Trap:** Don't immediately shout fraud. First separate growth-driven cash gaps from manipulation-driven ones.

# SESSION 1.3: The Balance Sheet — What a Company Owns and Owes

> "If a company vanished tomorrow, what would be left — and who would get it?"

---

## 🎣 HOOK: "A Photograph, Not a Movie"

The income statement is a movie. It shows everything that happened over a whole year.

The balance sheet is a photograph. It freezes a company at one single instant — usually 31 March in India.

It says: right now, this is everything we own, everything we owe, and what's left for the owners.

🧠 Remember: A balance sheet is a snapshot of one moment, not a story over time.

---

## 💡 CONCEPT: "The Two Sides"

The balance sheet has two sides, and they must always match.

One side: assets — everything the company controls (cash, factories, inventory, money owed to it).

The other side: how those assets were paid for — liabilities (borrowed) plus equity (the owners' money).

Picture buying a ₹50 lakh flat. ₹40 lakh is a home loan (liability). ₹10 lakh is your own savings (equity). The flat (asset, ₹50 lakh) = loan + your money. A company works exactly the same way, just much bigger.

🧠 Remember: Everything a company owns was paid for by either lenders or owners.

---

## 🔬 DEEP DIVE: "Inside the Balance Sheet"

The balance sheet is built from a few clear blocks.

- **Current assets:** turn into cash within a year — cash, inventory, receivables (money customers owe).
- **Non-current assets:** long-term — factories, machinery, land, patents.
- **Current liabilities:** due within a year — supplier dues (payables), short-term loans.
- **Non-current liabilities:** long-term debt and bonds repayable after a year.
- **Equity:** share capital plus accumulated profits (reserves).
- The key liquidity question: can current assets cover current liabilities? (You'll test this in Module 9.)

🧠 Remember: Split everything into current versus non-current — that's how analysts scan a balance sheet.

---

## 📊 REAL WORLD: "Reliance — Reading the Asset Side"

Look at Reliance Industries' balance sheet for FY24.

It shows enormous non-current assets — refineries, Jio's telecom towers, retail stores. These are capital-heavy businesses.

On the funding side, it carries significant debt (non-current liabilities) but also a massive equity base built from decades of retained profit.

An analyst reading it sees the verdict instantly: this is an asset-heavy empire funded by both lenders and shareholders.

🧠 Remember: The balance sheet reveals whether a business is asset-heavy or asset-light at a glance.

---

## 🔢 FORMULA: "Net Worth — The Book Value of Equity"

Formula: **Net Worth = Total Assets − Total Liabilities**

| Variable | Meaning |
|----------|---------|
| Total Assets | Everything the company owns |
| Total Liabilities | Everything the company owes |
| Net Worth | What truly belongs to shareholders |

Example: A company has assets of ₹1,000 crore and liabilities of ₹700 crore. Net Worth = 1,000 − 700 = **₹300 crore**.

Signal: Rising net worth year on year → the company is building shareholder wealth. Falling net worth → value is being eroded.

Analyst Insight: Net worth is the *book* value. Market value (share price × number of shares) is usually very different. That gap reveals what investors think of the future — and it's the foundation of the price-to-book (P/B) ratio.

🧠 Remember: Net worth is what shareholders would own if every debt were paid off today.

---

## ⚠️ MISTAKE: "Thinking Assets = Good, Liabilities = Bad"

The error: beginners assume more assets is always good and all liabilities are dangerous.

Why: the words simply *sound* positive and negative.

The truth: idle, unproductive assets quietly destroy value. And smart debt can multiply returns.

A candidate who fears all debt completely misses how a firm like Reliance uses cheap loans to fund high-return projects. The *quality* of assets and the *cost* of debt matter far more than the raw amounts.

🧠 Remember: It's not how much you own or owe — it's how productive and how cheap it is.

This session in 60 seconds:

1. The balance sheet is a snapshot of one instant — what's owned, what's owed, and what's left for owners.
2. Assets = Liabilities + Equity, and the two sides always balance.

## 🎙️ INTERVIEW QUESTIONS

### 🎙️ IQ1: "Walk me through the major line items on a balance sheet."
*Entry-level technical — extremely common at ICICI Securities, Kotak, and HDFC Securities.*

**Answer Framework:**
- The asset side splits into current assets (cash, receivables, inventory) and non-current assets (property, plant, equipment, and intangibles).
- The other side splits into liabilities — current (payables, short-term debt) and non-current (long-term debt).
- Equity sits below the liabilities: share capital plus reserves (accumulated profits).
- The fundamental check is that total assets must equal total liabilities plus equity.
- I'd then read the mix — asset-heavy versus asset-light, and debt versus equity funding.

**🔑 Power Phrase:** "I read a balance sheet in two cuts — current versus non-current, and owed versus owned."

**⚠️ Trap:** Don't list items at random. Show structure (current/non-current) — it signals that you actually analyse, not just memorise.

### 🎙️ IQ2: "What's the difference between book value and market value of equity?"
*Associate level — a valuation-linkage question at Motilal Oswal and Nuvama.*

**Answer Framework:**
- Book value is net worth on the balance sheet: assets minus liabilities.
- Market value is share price times the number of shares — what investors will pay today.
- The gap between them reflects expectations: market value usually exceeds book value for high-growth firms.
- An IT or FMCG firm trades far above book; a struggling PSU may trade below it.
- The price-to-book ratio captures this gap and is especially key for banks and asset-heavy firms.

**🔑 Power Phrase:** "Book value is what accountants recorded; market value is what the future is worth."

**⚠️ Trap:** Don't say they should be equal. A persistent gap is normal and informative — not an error.

### 🎙️ IQ3: "A company has a lot of debt on its balance sheet. Is that automatically a concern?"
*Associate or lateral level — a judgement and risk question at DSP, Axis Capital, and Edelweiss.*

**Answer Framework:**
- Not automatically — debt is only a problem relative to the company's ability to service it.
- I'd compare debt to equity and check interest coverage (can profits cover the interest?).
- Stable, cash-generative businesses like utilities and telecom can safely carry more debt.
- Cheap debt funding high-return projects actually boosts shareholder returns through leverage.
- The concern arises when debt is rising, costly, and earnings are volatile all at once.

**🔑 Power Phrase:** "Debt isn't dangerous — unserviceable debt is."

**⚠️ Trap:** Don't reflexively call debt bad. The interviewer is testing nuance, not caution.

# SESSION 1.4: The Income Statement — The Story of Profit

> "A company can grow its sales 40% in a year and still be heading straight for the rocks. How?"

---

## 🎣 HOOK: "The Top Line and the Bottom Line"

A company can announce ₹10,000 crore in sales and still be in deep trouble.

Sales — the "top line" — tells you how much money came in.

Profit — the "bottom line" — tells you how much you actually kept.

The entire income statement is the journey from one to the other. And a lot can go wrong on the way down.

🧠 Remember: Sales is what came in; profit is what you kept. The gap between them is the whole story.

---

## 💡 CONCEPT: "The Income Statement (P&L)"

The income statement, also called the Profit & Loss account (P&L), shows how much a company earned and spent over a period — a quarter or a year.

It starts with revenue and subtracts costs, layer by layer, until you reach net profit.

Think of your monthly salary slip. Gross salary sits at the top (revenue). Then the deductions — rent, food, EMIs, taxes (costs). What finally lands in your account is your take-home (net profit).

🧠 Remember: The income statement = revenue minus costs, step by step, ending in net profit.

---

## 🔬 DEEP DIVE: "Walking Down the P&L"

The income statement flows from top to bottom in clear stages.

- Revenue: total sales of goods or services.
- Minus cost of goods sold (COGS) → **Gross Profit**.
- Minus operating expenses (salaries, rent, marketing) → **Operating Profit (EBIT)**.
- Minus interest on debt → **Profit Before Tax (PBT)**.
- Minus tax → **Net Profit** (the bottom line).
- Each stage strips out a different cost — so each profit number tells you something different.

🧠 Remember: Profit comes in layers — gross, operating, pre-tax, and net. Always know which one you're reading.

---

## 📊 REAL WORLD: "Asian Paints — Where the Margin Lives"

Asian Paints sells paint, but its real strength shows up in the space between revenue and profit.

It buys crude-linked raw materials, yet it holds strong operating margins year after year.

Why? Pricing power and brand. When crude prices rise, it can pass the cost on to customers without losing them.

An analyst reading its P&L sees a business that defends its margins — the holy grail of FMCG.

🧠 Remember: A great business defends its margins even when input costs rise.

---

## 🔢 FORMULA: "Net Profit Margin"

Formula: **Net Profit Margin (%) = (Net Profit / Revenue) × 100**

| Variable | Meaning |
|----------|---------|
| Net Profit | The bottom line, after all costs and tax |
| Revenue | Total sales for the period |

Example: A company earns ₹120 crore net profit on ₹1,000 crore of revenue. Margin = (120 / 1,000) × 100 = **12%**.

Signal: A higher margin → more profit kept from every rupee of sales. A lower or falling margin → costs are rising or pricing power is weakening.

Analyst Insight: Always compare margins to peers, never in isolation. A 5% margin is great for a grocery retailer like D-Mart but poor for a software firm like Infosys. Context is everything.

🧠 Remember: Margin shows how many paise of profit a company keeps from every rupee of sales.

---

## ⚠️ MISTAKE: "Chasing Revenue Growth, Ignoring Profit"

The error: beginners get excited by fast revenue growth and stop looking.

Why: "growing 40%!" sounds unstoppable.

The truth: revenue growth funded by selling at a loss is a trap. Many startups grew their top line fast by burning cash on discounts.

A company can grow its revenue straight into bankruptcy. Always ask the real question — is this growth profitable, or simply bought?

🧠 Remember: Revenue growth without profit is just expensive noise.

# SESSION 1.5: The Cash Flow Statement — Why Profit Isn't Cash

> "A company reported record profits the year before it collapsed. The cash flow statement saw it coming."

---

## 🎣 HOOK: "The Statement That Doesn't Lie"

You can dress up profit. You can delay costs, book sales early, and play with estimates.

But cash is brutal. Either the money hit the bank account, or it didn't.

The cash flow statement tracks real money in and out.

That's exactly why seasoned analysts read it first, not last.

🧠 Remember: Profit can be styled; cash cannot. The cash flow statement is the truth.

---

## 💡 CONCEPT: "The Cash Flow Statement"

This statement tracks every rupee of actual cash that moved in and out over a period.

It answers one blunt question: did the company generate real money, or just paper profit?

Think of it as your bank passbook, not your salary slip. Your slip says you earned ₹50,000. Your passbook shows what actually landed and what actually left.

The cash flow statement is the company's passbook.

🧠 Remember: The cash flow statement is a company's bank passbook — real money in, real money out.

---

## 🔬 DEEP DIVE: "The Three Buckets of Cash"

All cash flow splits into three activities.

- **Operating (CFO):** cash from the core business — selling products, collecting from customers.
- **Investing (CFI):** cash spent on or earned from assets — buying machinery, selling a factory.
- **Financing (CFF):** cash from raising or repaying capital — loans, share issues, dividends.
- Add the three together and you get the net change in cash for the period.
- The gold standard is strong positive operating cash flow — the business funds itself.
- Negative operating cash propped up by financing cash = surviving on borrowed money.

🧠 Remember: Cash flows through three buckets — operating, investing, financing. Operating is king.

---

## 📊 REAL WORLD: "The Warning Profit Never Showed"

Several Indian firms — across infrastructure and finance — reported healthy profits right up until their collapse.

But their cash flow statements told a darker story: weak or negative operating cash for years, propped up by constant fresh borrowing.

The profit looked fine. The cash never came.

The analysts who actually read the cash flow statement got out early.

🧠 Remember: When profit rises but operating cash doesn't, trust the cash.

---

## 🔢 FORMULA: "Free Cash Flow (FCF)"

Formula: **Free Cash Flow = Operating Cash Flow − Capital Expenditure**

| Variable | Meaning |
|----------|---------|
| Operating Cash Flow (CFO) | Cash generated by the core business |
| Capital Expenditure (Capex) | Cash spent on long-term assets |

Example: A company generates ₹500 crore of operating cash and spends ₹200 crore on new plants. FCF = 500 − 200 = **₹300 crore**.

Signal: Positive FCF → real cash left over for dividends, debt repayment, or growth. Negative FCF → the business needs outside money just to keep going.

Analyst Insight: FCF is the cash a company could hand to shareholders without harming the business. It's the number a DCF valuation is built on — you'll use it heavily in Module 12.

🧠 Remember: Free cash flow is the real cash left after keeping the business running.

---

## ⚠️ MISTAKE: "Treating Net Profit as Cash in the Bank"

The error: beginners assume net profit equals the cash a company has.

Why: in their minds, both are just "the bottom line."

The truth: profit includes non-cash items (like depreciation) and sales not yet collected. The cash flow statement exists to adjust profit back into actual cash.

Value a company on profit alone and you'll badly misjudge any firm that reports profit but generates no cash.

🧠 Remember: Net profit is not a pile of cash — always check the cash flow statement.

# SESSION 1.6: The Rules of the Game — Accounting Principles & Ind AS

> "Two honest companies can report two different profits for the exact same year — and both can be right. How?"

---

## 🎣 HOOK: "Same Reality, Different Profit"

Two companies. Same sales. Same costs. Yet they report different profits — completely legally.

How? Because accounting involves *choices*: how fast to depreciate a machine, when to book a sale, how to value inventory.

Accounting rules narrow these choices, but they never erase them.

Knowing the rules is how you compare two companies fairly.

🧠 Remember: Accounting is rules plus judgement — and the judgement is where companies differ.

---

## 💡 CONCEPT: "Accounting Standards (Ind AS)"

Accounting standards are the rulebook every Indian company follows to prepare its financial statements. India uses Ind AS (Indian Accounting Standards), largely aligned with global IFRS.

Why it matters: standards make companies comparable. Without them, every firm would report differently and analysis would be impossible.

Think of cricket's laws. Without one rulebook, India and Australia couldn't even play the same match. Ind AS is the rulebook for financial reporting.

🧠 Remember: Ind AS is the common rulebook that makes Indian companies comparable.

---

## 🔬 DEEP DIVE: "The Core Accounting Principles"

A handful of principles shape every statement.

- **Accrual:** record revenue and costs when they happen, not when cash moves.
- **Matching:** match costs to the revenue they helped generate, in the same period.
- **Going concern:** assume the company will keep operating, not shut down tomorrow.
- **Prudence:** don't overstate profits or assets; stay cautious with optimism.
- **Consistency:** use the same methods year to year, so trends stay comparable.
- Break any of these and the numbers stop meaning what you think they mean.

🧠 Remember: Accrual, matching, going concern, prudence, consistency — the five pillars of honest accounts.

---

## 📊 REAL WORLD: "Why Ind AS Changed the Game"

When India moved to Ind AS (phased in from 2016), reported numbers shifted for many companies — not because the business changed, but because the rules did.

Revenue recognition, lease accounting, and financial instruments were all treated differently.

Analysts had to re-learn how to read statements and restate old years to compare them fairly.

It was a sharp reminder: the rulebook itself shapes the numbers.

🧠 Remember: When accounting rules change, the numbers change — even if the business doesn't.

---

## 🔢 FORMULA: "Straight-Line Depreciation"

Formula: **Annual Depreciation = (Cost of Asset − Salvage Value) / Useful Life**

| Variable | Meaning |
|----------|---------|
| Cost of Asset | The purchase price |
| Salvage Value | Expected resale value at the end of its life |
| Useful Life | Number of years the asset is used |

Example: A machine costs ₹50 lakh, has a salvage value of ₹5 lakh, and a useful life of 9 years. Annual depreciation = (50 − 5) / 9 = **₹5 lakh per year**.

Signal: A longer useful life → lower yearly depreciation → higher reported profit. A shorter life → the opposite.

Analyst Insight: Two firms with identical machines can report different profits just by choosing different useful lives. Always check the depreciation policy before comparing margins — it's a favourite spot for window-dressing.

🧠 Remember: Depreciation spreads an asset's cost over its life — and the chosen life shapes reported profit.

---

## ⚠️ MISTAKE: "Comparing Two Companies Without Checking Their Policies"

The error: beginners line up two companies' profits side by side and declare a winner.

Why: the numbers look directly comparable.

The truth: different depreciation methods, inventory valuation, or revenue timing can make those profits non-comparable.

In an interview, comparing two firms without adjusting for accounting differences shows you don't truly understand the statements. Always read the notes to accounts first.

🧠 Remember: Never compare two companies' numbers until you've checked they used the same accounting choices.

# SESSION 1.7: Economics for Analysts — The Forces Behind the Numbers

> "Why can the RBI move every stock on the NSE without buying a single share?"

---

## 🎣 HOOK: "The Invisible Hand on Every Stock"

The RBI doesn't own Reliance or Infosys. Yet when it changes one number — the interest rate — every stock on the NSE can move.

How? Because economics is the weather that every company operates inside.

Rates, inflation, growth, and currency shape demand, costs, and valuations — for everyone at once.

🧠 Remember: A company doesn't operate in a vacuum — the economy is the weather it lives in.

---

## 💡 CONCEPT: "Macro vs Micro Economics"

Microeconomics studies single players — one company, one customer, one price. Macroeconomics studies the whole economy — inflation, GDP growth, interest rates, employment.

An analyst needs both. Micro tells you if the company is good; macro tells you if the timing is good.

Micro is studying one player's form. Macro is reading the pitch, the weather, and the crowd. You need both to predict the match.

🧠 Remember: Micro judges the company; macro judges the environment it plays in.

---

## 🔬 DEEP DIVE: "Supply, Demand, and Price"

The most basic engine in all of economics.

- Demand: how much buyers want at a given price. A lower price usually means more demand.
- Supply: how much sellers offer at a given price. A higher price usually means more supply.
- Price settles where supply meets demand — the equilibrium.
- More demand than supply → prices rise (think onions just before a festival).
- More supply than demand → prices fall (think mangoes at peak season).
- Every product, share, and currency is priced by this same tug-of-war.

🧠 Remember: Price is simply where what buyers want meets what sellers offer.

---

## 📊 REAL WORLD: "When the RBI Hikes, Markets Listen"

When the RBI raises the repo rate to fight inflation, borrowing gets costlier for everyone.

Loans cost more, so consumers buy fewer cars and homes — hitting auto and real-estate stocks. Companies pay more interest, squeezing their profits.

And future profits get discounted harder, so high-growth stocks fall the most.

One rate decision ripples through the entire market.

🧠 Remember: Interest rates set by the RBI flow into demand, profit, and valuation across all stocks.

## 🔢 FORMULA: "Real Interest Rate"

Formula: **Real Interest Rate ≈ Nominal Interest Rate − Inflation Rate**

| Variable | Meaning |
|----------|---------|
| Nominal Rate | The stated interest rate (e.g., on a fixed deposit) |
| Inflation Rate | How fast prices are rising |
| Real Rate | Your true gain in purchasing power |

Example: A fixed deposit pays 7% (nominal) while inflation is 5%. Real rate = 7 − 5 = **2%**. Your money truly grows only 2%.

Signal: A positive real rate → savers gain purchasing power. A negative real rate → inflation eats your returns despite the interest.

Analyst Insight: Markets care about real rates, not just headline rates. A "high" 7% rate means little if inflation is also 7% — the real return is zero, and that shapes where money flows.

🧠 Remember: It's not the interest rate that matters — it's the rate *after* inflation.

---

## ⚠️ MISTAKE: "Ignoring the Macro When Picking a Stock"

The error: beginners fall in love with a great company and ignore the economy.

Why: a strong business *feels* like it should always win.

The truth: even a great company struggles against rising rates, a slowing economy, or a weak rupee.

Buy a brilliant exporter right before the rupee strengthens, or a lender right before a rate shock, and returns can sink. Great company, wrong weather, bad outcome.

🧠 Remember: A great company in a bad macro environment can still be a bad investment.

## ❓ QUIZ

### Q1. When the RBI raises interest rates, which is the most likely effect on high-growth stocks?

a) Their valuations rise
b) Their valuations fall, as future profits are discounted harder
c) They become immune to rates
d) Their revenue automatically doubles

✅ Answer: b) Their valuations fall, as future profits are discounted harder
📝 Why: Higher rates raise the discount rate, lowering the present value of distant future profits — which hurts high-growth stocks the most.

### Q2. A fixed deposit offers 8% interest while inflation is 6%. The real interest rate is approximately:

a) 14%
b) 8%
c) 2%
d) 6%

✅ Answer: c) 2%
📝 Why: Real rate ≈ Nominal − Inflation = 8 − 6 = 2%. Your true gain in purchasing power is only 2%.

### Q3. Which of these is a macroeconomic factor rather than a microeconomic one?

a) A single company's pricing
b) One customer's buying decision
c) The national inflation rate
d) A firm's marketing budget

✅ Answer: c) The national inflation rate
📝 Why: Inflation affects the whole economy. The others concern single players, which is microeconomics.

### Q4. True or False: A high-quality company is always a good investment regardless of the macro environment.

✅ Answer: False
📝 Why: Even an excellent company can deliver poor returns in a hostile macro environment — rising rates, slowing growth, or an unfavourable currency move.

### Q5. Fill in the blank: The point where supply meets demand and sets the market price is called the ________.

✅ Answer: equilibrium
📝 Why: At equilibrium, the quantity buyers want equals the quantity sellers offer, which settles the price.

## 🎙️ INTERVIEW QUESTIONS

### 🎙️ IQ1: "How does a change in interest rates affect the stock market?"
*Entry-level technical — common at ICICI Securities, Kotak, and across macro-aware desks.*

**Answer Framework:**
- Higher rates make borrowing costlier, so consumers spend less on big-ticket items like cars and homes.
- Companies pay more interest, which squeezes their profits.
- Future profits get discounted at a higher rate, lowering present valuations.
- Rate-sensitive sectors — autos, real estate, NBFCs — react most sharply.
- So a single RBI decision ripples through demand, profit, and valuation together.

**🔑 Power Phrase:** "An interest rate isn't just a borrowing cost — it's the discount rate the whole market is valued against."

**⚠️ Trap:** Don't stop at "borrowing gets costlier." The valuation channel — discounting future profits — is what separates a sharp answer from a basic one.

### 🎙️ IQ2: "What's the difference between nominal and real interest rates, and why should an investor care?"
*Associate level — a macro literacy question at Nuvama, DSP, and Axis Capital.*

**Answer Framework:**
- The nominal rate is the stated rate, like 7% on a fixed deposit.
- The real rate subtracts inflation to show the true gain in purchasing power.
- If inflation matches the nominal rate, the real return is zero despite the interest.
- Investors care because real returns, not headline rates, decide whether wealth actually grows.
- It also shapes where money flows — into equities, gold, or fixed income.

**🔑 Power Phrase:** "Investors don't get richer from interest — they get richer from interest *after* inflation."

**⚠️ Trap:** Don't quote only the headline rate. Showing you adjust for inflation signals real analytical maturity.

### 🎙️ IQ3: "Walk me through how India's GDP growth slowing down would affect a consumer-facing company you cover."
*Associate or lateral level — an applied macro-to-micro question at Edelweiss and larger research desks.*

**Answer Framework:**
- Slowing GDP usually means weaker income growth and more cautious consumers.
- A consumer-facing firm sees softer demand, especially for discretionary products.
- Volume growth slows, and the company may resort to discounts, hurting margins.
- Staples hold up better than discretionary items, so the product mix matters a lot.
- I'd revisit my volume and margin assumptions and most likely trim my growth forecast.

**🔑 Power Phrase:** "When the economy slows, discretionary demand is the first thing consumers cut."

**⚠️ Trap:** Don't treat all consumer firms the same — staples and discretionary names behave very differently in a slowdown.
