## Session 1 · The FMVA Exam, Decoded — Format, Strategy & the Two Gates

*Orientation to the FMVA exam itself: the two pass-gates (80% course assessments, 70% final), the exact final-exam format, and the triage time-strategy that separates the third who fail from the two-thirds who pass.*

**By the end of this session you will be able to:**

### Hook — One in Three Fail — and Almost None for the Reason You'd Think

Here's the uncomfortable truth nobody tells you about the FMVA: roughly one in three candidates fail the final exam on their first attempt — and almost none of them fail because they didn't know the material. They fail because they treated a 3-hour, open-book, 50-question exam like a memory test instead of a performance under time pressure. You've spent this entire program building real analyst skill — DCFs, ratios, three-statement models. This module is not here to re-teach any of that; you already have it. It exists to make sure that on exam day, that knowledge actually converts into a score above 70%. We start by decoding the exam itself: what it really tests, how it's structured, and the single strategy that separates the third who fail from the two-thirds who pass.

### Concept — The Two-Gate Structure: Where the Real Risk Lives

The FMVA is not a single test — it's a multi-stage program with two distinct pass-gates. Stage 1: optional prep courses to refresh basics. Stage 2: 14 required core courses, each ending in a graded assessment. Stage 3: 3 elective courses chosen from 27. Stage 4: the final exam. The first gate is that every end-of-course assessment requires 80% to pass, with unlimited free retakes. The second gate is the final exam itself, which requires 70%. You cannot sit the final until all course gates are cleared. Understanding this structure tells you exactly where the risk lives: the course assessments are low-stakes (infinite retakes), but the final is high-stakes (a 30-day wait between attempts). Your preparation energy should therefore be weighted toward the final, not the course quizzes.

> **Analogy.** Think of it like earning a driving licence. The course assessments are the practice tests you can retake until you're ready — no penalty for failing. The final exam is the actual road test: you get one shot, and if you fail you wait a month before trying again. Smart candidates treat the practice tests as learning tools and save their peak preparation for the road test.

**Key terms**

- **Core courses:** The 14 required courses spanning accounting, modeling, valuation, Excel, FP&A and presentations that form the FMVA backbone.
- **End-of-course assessment:** A graded quiz at the end of each course requiring 80% to pass; unlimited free retakes.
- **Final exam:** The capstone 50-question assessment requiring 70% to earn the credential.
- **Two-gate structure:** The requirement to clear every 80% course assessment before being allowed to attempt the 70% final.

### Concept — The Final Exam, Precisely: 50 / 3 Hours / Open-Book / 70%

Know the shape of the final cold before you walk in. It is 50 multiple-choice questions, 3 hours long, and open-book. The questions fall into three buckets: basic conceptual/knowledge questions, financial-ratio and calculation questions, and Excel-based modeling case studies. The pass mark is 70%, meaning at least 35 of 50 correct. There is no per-question timer, but a hard 3-hour total cap. Fail, and there is a 30-day waiting period before a retake. 'Open-book' is the most misunderstood feature: it does NOT mean you can look up every answer — there isn't time. It means the exam tests application, not memorisation, and your reference materials are a safety net for the rare formula you blank on, not a primary strategy.

> **Analogy.** Open-book here works like a cockpit checklist for a pilot. The checklist exists for the rare moment you need to confirm a step — but a pilot who reads the checklist for every single action will never land the plane in time. You fly mostly from trained instinct and use the reference only at the edges.

**Key terms**

- **Open-book:** You may reference materials during the exam, but time pressure makes it a safety net, not a lookup strategy.
- **Excel case study:** A question requiring you to build or interpret a model/calculation and answer based on the result.
- **Pass mark (70%):** 35 of 50 questions correct — the threshold to become FMVA certified.
- **Retake window:** A 30-day mandatory wait between final-exam attempts.

### Deep Dive — Winning the 3-Hour Clock

**The 3-hour clock is your real constraint**

50 questions in 180 minutes averages 3.6 minutes per question — but that average is a trap. Conceptual questions take 30–60 seconds; ratio calculations take 1–3 minutes; Excel case studies can eat 8–12 minutes each. If even 8 of your 50 are Excel cases, they alone could consume 80+ minutes, leaving roughly 100 minutes for the other 42. The winning approach is **triage**: a first pass answering every quick conceptual and calculation question, flagging and skipping the heavy Excel cases, then returning to them with the time that remains. Never let one stubborn case-study question burn 15 minutes while ten easy marks sit unanswered.

**How to actually use 'open-book'**

Build a one-page exam cheat sheet before the exam — not to read during it, but because the act of building it cements recall. On it: the core formulas (WACC, FCFF, FCFE, terminal value, key ratios, NPV/IRR), the enterprise-to-equity bridge, and the order of operations for a DCF. During the exam, glance at it only when you genuinely blank. Treat any question where you reach for the sheet as a signal to review that topic afterward — not as normal practice.

**Read the question stem precisely**

CFI's most reliable trap is the enterprise-value-versus-equity-value distinction, closely followed by cash-flow-versus-net-income. A question may give you everything needed to compute enterprise value, then ask for equity value per share — and the wrong answer (enterprise value) will be sitting right there as an option. Train yourself to underline what the question actually asks for before you compute. The difference between a 68% and a 72% is usually four questions you rushed, not four you didn't know.

### Common Mistakes — The Four Exam-Day Mistakes That Sink Otherwise-Ready Candidates

1. **Treating the open-book exam as a lookup exam.** Why it happens: Candidates assume 'open-book' means low pressure and plan to research answers in real time. *Fix:* Prepare as if it were closed-book. Use references only as an occasional safety net — there is no time to look up 50 answers.

2. **Working strictly in question order.** Why it happens: It feels orderly and 'fair' to do questions 1 through 50 in sequence. *Fix:* Triage. First pass: answer every quick conceptual and calculation question. Second pass: return to flagged Excel case studies with remaining time.

3. **Letting one Excel case study consume 12–15 minutes.** Why it happens: Perfectionism plus the sunk-cost feeling of 'I've nearly got it.' *Fix:* Cap each Excel case at a strict time budget. If you exceed it, lock in your best answer, flag it, and move on. Easy marks elsewhere are worth more.

4. **Answering enterprise value when the question asks for equity value (or vice versa).** Why it happens: The question provides enterprise-value inputs and the EV figure is offered as a distractor option. *Fix:* Underline exactly what is asked. If equity value per share is wanted, finish the enterprise-to-equity bridge and divide by share count — don't stop at EV.

## Session 2 · Accounting Fundamentals — The Three Statements & Accrual Logic

*The accounting bedrock the exam tests relentlessly: the three statements as one connected system, accrual vs cash, and the linkages that drive every model and valuation.*

**By the end of this session you will be able to:**

- Describe how the income statement, balance sheet and cash flow statement connect
- Distinguish accrual profit (net income) from cash flow and reconcile the two
- Apply the accounting equation, retained-earnings roll-forward and net working capital
- Avoid the depreciation and working-capital traps in cash-flow questions

### Hook — The Foundation Under Every Model You'll Ever Build

Every valuation you will ever build, every model, every ratio — all of it sits on a single foundation: the three financial statements and the accrual logic that governs them. CFI knows this, which is why a meaningful chunk of the final exam tests pure accounting fundamentals: classifying items, distinguishing net income from cash flow, and tracing how the statements connect. The good news? This is the most learnable, most predictable part of the exam — the rules don't change and the traps are well known. Nail this session and you've locked in some of the easiest, most reliable marks on the entire paper.

### Concept — Three Statements, One Connected System

The income statement (P&L) measures performance over a period — revenue minus expenses equals net income. The balance sheet is a snapshot at a point in time — assets equal liabilities plus equity. The cash flow statement reconciles net income to actual cash, sorted into operating, investing and financing activities. The idea CFI tests relentlessly is accrual accounting: revenue and expenses are recognised when earned or incurred, not when cash moves. A company can report healthy net income while burning cash, or vice versa — because the income statement runs on accruals and the cash flow statement runs on cash. They are reconciled, never identical.

> **Analogy.** Think of accrual accounting like a restaurant taking a reservation deposit. A customer pays Rs 10,000 today for an event next month — the cash arrives now, but under accrual accounting the restaurant hasn't earned the revenue yet, so it sits as a liability (deferred revenue) until the event happens. Cash in the door and revenue earned are two different timelines, and accounting tracks both separately.

**Key terms**

- **Accrual accounting:** Recognising revenue when earned and expenses when incurred, regardless of when cash changes hands.
- **Net income vs cash flow:** Net income is an accrual measure of profit; cash flow is the actual movement of cash. They are reconciled on the cash flow statement, not equal.
- **Deferred revenue:** Cash received before the good or service is delivered; a liability until earned.
- **Matching principle:** Expenses are recorded in the same period as the revenues they help generate.

### Formulas — The Three Accounting Identities You Must Memorise

**The Accounting Equation**

```
Assets = Liabilities + Shareholders' Equity
```

- **Assets** — Everything the company owns or controls (cash, receivables, inventory, PP&E).
- **Liabilities** — Everything the company owes (payables, debt, deferred revenue).
- **Equity** — Owners' residual claim (share capital + retained earnings).

Every rupee of assets is financed either by money owed to others (liabilities) or money belonging to owners (equity). The balance sheet must always balance.

> Example: If a firm has Rs 500 Cr of assets and Rs 300 Cr of liabilities, equity = 500 - 300 = Rs 200 Cr.

> Note: A company buys Rs 50 Cr of machinery using Rs 50 Cr of new debt. Assets rise Rs 50 Cr (machinery) and liabilities rise Rs 50 Cr (debt). The equation stays balanced; equity is unchanged.

**Retained Earnings Roll-Forward**

```
Ending RE = Beginning RE + Net Income - Dividends
```

- **Beginning RE** — Retained earnings at the start of the period.
- **Net Income** — Profit for the period, taken from the income statement.
- **Dividends** — Profits distributed to shareholders during the period.

Retained earnings is the running total of all profits the company has kept rather than paid out. This single line links the income statement to the balance sheet.

> Example: Beginning RE Rs 120 Cr + Net Income Rs 40 Cr - Dividends Rs 10 Cr = Ending RE Rs 150 Cr.

> Note: This is how net income flows from the P&L into equity on the balance sheet — a connection CFI loves to test.

**Net Working Capital**

```
NWC = Current Assets - Current Liabilities
```

- **Current Assets** — Assets expected to convert to cash within one year (receivables, inventory, cash).
- **Current Liabilities** — Obligations due within one year (payables, short-term debt).

Working capital measures short-term liquidity. An increase in NWC consumes cash; a decrease releases it.

> Example: Current Assets Rs 80 Cr - Current Liabilities Rs 50 Cr = NWC Rs 30 Cr.

> Note: In a cash flow statement, if NWC rises from Rs 30 Cr to Rs 45 Cr, that Rs 15 Cr increase is a cash outflow in operating activities.

### Visual — How the Three Statements Connect

A linkage diagram showing: Net Income flows from the Income Statement (1) to the top of the Cash Flow Statement under Operating Activities, and (2) to Retained Earnings in Shareholders' Equity on the Balance Sheet. The Cash Flow Statement ends with Net Change in Cash, which updates the Cash line on the Balance Sheet. The balance sheet must always balance: Assets = Liabilities + Equity.

> **Key insight:** Net income hits the balance sheet twice — once via retained earnings, once via cash — and the statement still balances. This is the heart of three-statement modeling.

### Deep Dive — The Accrual Traps the Exam Loves

**Why depreciation is the exam's favourite trick**

Depreciation is a non-cash expense: it reduces net income on the income statement, but no cash leaves the business in that period (the cash left when the asset was bought). On the cash flow statement, depreciation is therefore added back to net income in operating activities. CFI tests this constantly — a question may ask for operating cash flow given net income and depreciation, and the wrong answer subtracts depreciation instead of adding it. Remember: net income already had depreciation subtracted, so to get back to cash you add it back.

**Deferred revenue and the cash-vs-revenue gap**

When a SaaS company collects an annual subscription upfront, cash arrives immediately but revenue is recognised monthly as the service is delivered. The unearned portion sits as deferred revenue (a liability). This is why a fast-growing subscription business can show strong operating cash flow while reporting comparatively modest revenue — and why the exam uses such scenarios to test whether you understand accrual recognition versus cash receipt.

**The order the statements are built**

In a model, you build the income statement down to net income, then the cash flow statement (to derive the ending cash balance), then complete the balance sheet (cash from the CF statement, retained earnings from net income). The balance sheet is built last because it depends on outputs from the other two. If your balance sheet doesn't balance, the error is almost always a missing link — a cash flow item not flowing through, or retained earnings not updated.

### Example — Why TCS's Profit and Cash Differ

**Tata Consultancy Services (TCS) — illustrative accrual lens**

**Situation:** An analyst is shown TCS reporting strong net income for a quarter and is asked why reported profit and the change in cash differ so much.

**Data:** Suppose (illustratively) TCS reports Net Income of Rs 11,000 Cr. Add back non-cash Depreciation & Amortisation of Rs 600 Cr. Then adjust for working capital: receivables rise Rs 1,500 Cr (a cash outflow, because revenue was booked but not yet collected) while payables rise Rs 400 Cr (a cash inflow). Operating cash flow is approximately 11,000 + 600 - 1,500 + 400 = Rs 10,500 Cr.

**Analysis:** Net income (Rs 11,000 Cr) and operating cash flow (Rs 10,500 Cr) differ purely because of accrual timing: D&A is non-cash (added back), and the rise in receivables means some booked revenue hasn't been collected yet (subtracted). None of this implies a problem — it is exactly how accrual accounting and the cash flow reconciliation work.

**Analyst takeaway:** Profit is an opinion shaped by accrual rules; cash is a fact. The cash flow statement exists to translate between them — and the exam rewards anyone who can trace that translation step by step.

### Common Mistakes — The Four Accounting Errors the Exam Punishes

1. **Subtracting depreciation when calculating operating cash flow.** Why it happens: Depreciation 'feels' like a cost, so candidates instinctively subtract it. *Fix:* Add depreciation back. It was already subtracted to reach net income, but it is non-cash, so reversing it returns you toward actual cash.

2. **Treating net income and cash flow as the same number.** Why it happens: Both sound like 'how much the company made,' so they blur together. *Fix:* Net income is accrual profit; operating cash flow adjusts it for non-cash items and working-capital changes. They reconcile, never equal.

3. **Forgetting that an increase in working capital uses cash.** Why it happens: More receivables or inventory looks like growth, which feels positive. *Fix:* An increase in a current asset (receivables, inventory) is a cash outflow; an increase in a current liability (payables) is a cash inflow.

4. **Recording cash received upfront as revenue immediately.** Why it happens: Cash in the bank feels like earned revenue. *Fix:* Under accrual accounting, unearned cash is a liability (deferred revenue) until the good or service is delivered.

### Summary — Session 2 — What to Lock In

- The three statements form one connected system: net income links the income statement to both retained earnings and the cash flow statement, and cash links back to the balance sheet.
- Accrual accounting recognises revenue/expenses when earned/incurred, not when cash moves — so net income and cash flow reconcile but are never equal.
- Memorise the accounting equation (A = L + E), the retained-earnings roll-forward, and net working capital — these underpin countless exam questions.
- Depreciation is non-cash: add it back to net income when deriving operating cash flow (the exam's favourite trap).
- An increase in working capital consumes cash; a decrease releases it — get the direction right and you bank the cash-flow questions.

## Session 3 · Financial Analysis & Ratios — The Calculations the Exam Tests

*The exact ratio calculations that appear as numerical questions: profitability, liquidity, solvency and efficiency, plus the DuPont decomposition.*

### Hook — Where Knowing Becomes Calculating

Up to now the exam has asked whether you understand the financial statements. Ratios are where it flips the question to: can you calculate, correctly, under time pressure? This is the single most calculation-dense topic on the FMVA final — a large share of the ratio-and-numerical questions live here — and it is also the most bankable, because every answer is a clean, checkable number. There is no interpretation to argue about: a current ratio is current assets over current liabilities, full stop. Master the formulas cold and the arithmetic discipline to apply them, and you turn this entire block into guaranteed marks. The candidates who lose points here don't misunderstand ratios — they use ending balances where they need averages, plug EBIT where net income belongs, or annualise a number the wrong way. This session locks down both the formulas and the traps.

### Concept — Four Families, Four Questions

Every ratio CFI tests belongs to one of four families, and each family answers one question. Profitability ratios (margins, ROE, ROA, ROIC) ask: how efficiently does the company turn revenue and capital into profit? Liquidity ratios (current, quick) ask: can it cover its short-term bills? Solvency or leverage ratios (debt-to-equity, interest coverage) ask: can it survive its debt load? Efficiency or activity ratios (inventory, receivables and asset turnover) ask: how well does it use its assets to generate sales? Knowing which family a question lives in tells you instantly which formula to reach for. A ratio in isolation is almost meaningless — it gains meaning only against a trend, a peer, or a benchmark.

> **Analogy.** Think of the four families like a doctor's basic vitals. Profitability is the patient's energy level, liquidity is short-term blood pressure, solvency is long-term heart health, and efficiency is metabolism. No single vital diagnoses anything alone; the doctor reads them together and against what is normal for that age and build. Ratios work exactly the same way.

**Key terms**

- **Profitability ratios:** Measure how effectively a firm converts revenue and capital into profit (margins, ROE, ROA, ROIC).
- **Liquidity ratios:** Measure the ability to meet short-term obligations (current ratio, quick ratio).
- **Solvency / leverage ratios:** Measure debt load and the ability to service it (debt-to-equity, interest coverage).
- **Efficiency / activity ratios:** Measure how well assets are used to generate sales (inventory, receivables and asset turnover).

### Formulas — The High-Yield Ratio Formulas to Memorise

**Profitability Margins**

```
Gross Margin   = (Revenue - COGS) / Revenue
Operating Margin = EBIT / Revenue
Net Margin     = Net Income / Revenue
```

> Example: Revenue Rs 500 Cr, COGS Rs 300 Cr, EBIT Rs 80 Cr, Net Income Rs 50 Cr → Gross 40%, Operating 16%, Net 10%.

> Note: Each margin must be divided by Revenue, never by each other. Dividing gross profit by COGS gives markup, not margin.

**Return on Equity (ROE)**

```
ROE = Net Income / Shareholders' Equity
```

> Example: Net Income Rs 50 Cr / Equity Rs 250 Cr = 20%.

> Note: ROE is flattered by leverage. DuPont decomposition reveals whether the ROE is quality-driven or leverage-driven.

**Return on Assets & Return on Invested Capital**

```
ROA  = Net Income / Total Assets
ROIC = NOPAT / Invested Capital    (NOPAT = EBIT × (1 - tax rate))
```

> Example: EBIT Rs 80 Cr, tax 25% → NOPAT = Rs 60 Cr. Invested Capital Rs 400 Cr → ROIC = 15%.

> Note: ROIC above WACC = value creation; below WACC = value destruction.

**Liquidity Ratios**

```
Current Ratio = Current Assets / Current Liabilities
Quick Ratio   = (Current Assets - Inventory) / Current Liabilities
```

> Example: Current Assets Rs 120 Cr (inventory Rs 40 Cr), Current Liabilities Rs 60 Cr → Current 2.0x, Quick 1.33x.

> Note: Quick ratio is the stricter test, preferred for inventory-heavy businesses.

**Leverage & Coverage**

```
Debt-to-Equity    = Total Debt / Shareholders' Equity
Interest Coverage = EBIT / Interest Expense
```

> Example: Total Debt Rs 300 Cr / Equity Rs 250 Cr = 1.2x. EBIT Rs 90 Cr / Interest Rs 15 Cr = 6.0x coverage.

> Note: Coverage uses EBIT (pre-interest), never net income.

**Efficiency / Activity Ratios**

```
Inventory Turnover    = COGS / Average Inventory
Receivables Turnover  = Revenue / Average Receivables
DSO                   = 365 / Receivables Turnover
```

> Example: COGS Rs 600 Cr / Avg Inventory Rs 100 Cr = 6.0x (~61 days). Receivables turnover 10x → DSO = 36.5 days.

> Note: Inventory turnover uses COGS; receivables turnover uses Revenue. Use average balances, not ending.

**DuPont Decomposition (3-Step)**

```
ROE = Net Margin × Asset Turnover × Equity Multiplier
    = (Net Income/Revenue) × (Revenue/Total Assets) × (Total Assets/Equity)
```

> Example: Net Margin 5% × Asset Turnover 2.0x × Equity Multiplier 1.5x = 15% ROE.

> Note: A high equity multiplier (leverage) is the risky way to inflate ROE. DuPont reveals the source.

**Cash Conversion Cycle**

```
CCC = DIO + DSO - DPO
    where DIO = 365/inventory turnover, DPO = 365/payables turnover
```

A short or negative CCC signals operational strength. DPO is subtracted because longer payable days reduces cash tied up.

### Deep Dive — Beyond the Formula: What the Exam Really Probes

**DuPont: why a high ROE can be a warning sign**

Two companies report 18% ROE. Company A: net margin 12%, asset turnover 1.0x, equity multiplier 1.5x. Company B: net margin 4%, asset turnover 1.0x, equity multiplier 4.5x. Identical headline ROE, completely different stories. A earns its return from genuine profitability; B manufactures it with leverage (equity multiplier of 4.5x means assets are 4.5 times equity — very high debt). In a downturn, B's interest burden could wipe out equity while A coasts. CFI loves this exact setup: given the three DuPont components, identify which firm's ROE is higher-quality. The answer is always the one relying less on the equity multiplier.

**Average vs ending balances, and matching numerators**

Two precision points the exam rewards. First, ratios that pair an income-statement flow with a balance-sheet stock should use the average of opening and closing balances. Second, match the numerator to the asset: inventory turnover uses COGS (inventory is carried at cost), while receivables turnover uses Revenue (receivables arise from sales). Swapping these is a frequent wrong answer.

**References**

- CFI Financial Analysis Fundamentals — the four ratio families and the DuPont framework.
- Standard treatment of the cash conversion cycle in working-capital analysis.

### Example — Reading Avenue Supermarts (DMart) Through Ratios

**Situation:** Illustrative figures — Revenue Rs 50,000 Cr, COGS Rs 42,500 Cr, Net Income Rs 2,500 Cr, Total Assets Rs 25,000 Cr, Shareholders' Equity Rs 20,000 Cr, Average Inventory Rs 4,250 Cr.

**Computed ratios:** Gross margin 15%, Net margin 5%, Asset turnover 2.0x, Inventory turnover 10x (~36 days), ROE 12.5%, Equity multiplier 1.25x. DuPont: 5% × 2.0 × 1.25 = 12.5%.

**Analysis:** Thin margins but rapid asset use — the classic high-volume, low-margin retail model. The low equity multiplier (1.25x) confirms the ROE is quality-driven, not leverage-driven.

**Analyst takeaway:** Low margin plus high turnover = volume retailer. A low equity multiplier confirms the ROE is real rather than leverage-driven. Always run the DuPont check before judging whether an ROE is good.

### Common Mistakes — The Ratio Errors That Cost Easy Marks

1. **Using ending balances when the formula needs average balances.** *Fix:* Use (beginning + ending) / 2 when both are given for turnover ratios, ROA and ROE.
2. **Calculating interest coverage with net income instead of EBIT.** *Fix:* Interest coverage = EBIT / Interest Expense. Net income is already after interest, which double-counts the deduction.
3. **Confusing margin with markup (dividing gross profit by COGS).** *Fix:* Margin divides by Revenue; markup divides by cost.
4. **Using Revenue for inventory turnover (or COGS for receivables turnover).** *Fix:* Inventory turnover uses COGS; receivables turnover uses Revenue.

### Summary — Session 3 — What to Lock In

- Every ratio belongs to one of four families — profitability, liquidity, solvency/leverage, efficiency — and each answers one specific question.
- Memorise: the three margins, ROE/ROA/ROIC, current and quick ratios, debt-to-equity and interest coverage, the turnover ratios, and DuPont.
- DuPont (ROE = net margin × asset turnover × equity multiplier) reveals whether an ROE is driven by quality (margin/efficiency) or by leverage — high ROE from a high equity multiplier is lower quality.
- Use EBIT (not net income) for interest coverage, divide margins by Revenue (not cost), and match turnover numerators (COGS for inventory, Revenue for receivables).
- Pair period flows with average balances, and remember a ratio is meaningful only against trend, peer or benchmark.

## Session 4 · Building the 3-Statement Model — Linking, Balancing & Circularity

*How the three statements are wired together in a model: the build order, the cash and retained-earnings links, circular references from interest, and why the balance sheet must balance.*

**By the end of this session you will be able to:**

- Build a three-statement model in the correct order
- Trace the cash and retained-earnings links between statements
- Resolve circularity from interest-on-debt with iterative calculation
- Diagnose why a balance sheet fails to balance

### Hook — The Model That Has to Balance

The three-statement model is the rite of passage of finance — the thing that separates someone who has read about accounting from someone who can actually build. It is also the conceptual spine of the FMVA exam: questions on linking, the balancing plug, and circular references all trace back to whether you truly understand how the income statement, balance sheet and cash flow statement are wired into a single living system. If you change one assumption — say, depreciation rises by Rs 10 Cr — does the change ripple correctly through all three statements and does the balance sheet still balance? In a well-built model it does, automatically.

### Concept — One System, Built in a Fixed Order

A three-statement model is one integrated system where outputs of one statement become inputs to another. Build it in a fixed order: first the income statement, then the cash flow statement, then the balance sheet (last, because almost every line depends on outputs from the other two). The golden rule: net income flows to two places — the top of the cash flow statement and retained earnings on the balance sheet — and the model only works if every such link is live, never hardcoded.

> **Analogy.** Think of it like plumbing in a house. The income statement is the water tank, the cash flow statement is the pipework, and the balance sheet is every tap and fixture the water finally reaches. Install the tank first, then the pipes, then connect the fixtures — if a single joint is hardcoded shut instead of connected, the system springs a leak (the balance sheet won't balance).

**Key terms**

- **Integrated model:** A model where the three statements are dynamically linked, so a change in one assumption ripples through all three.
- **Build order:** Income statement → cash flow statement → balance sheet.
- **Live link:** A formula reference (not a typed-in number) connecting one statement's output to another's input.
- **Supporting schedules:** The PP&E, debt and equity roll-forwards that feed the balance sheet.

### Formulas — The Roll-Forwards and the Balance Check

**The Balance Check**

```
Total Assets = Total Liabilities + Total Equity
Balance Check = Assets - Liabilities - Equity = 0
```

> Example: Assets Rs 800 Cr; Liabilities Rs 500 Cr; Equity Rs 300 Cr → 800 - 500 - 300 = 0. Balanced.

**PP&E Roll-Forward**

```
Ending PP&E = Beginning PP&E + CapEx - Depreciation
```

> Example: Beginning PP&E Rs 200 Cr + CapEx Rs 50 Cr - Depreciation Rs 30 Cr = Ending PP&E Rs 220 Cr.

> Note: Depreciation appears three times — income-statement expense, CF add-back, and PP&E reduction.

**Retained Earnings Roll-Forward**

```
Ending RE = Beginning RE + Net Income - Dividends
```

> Example: Beginning RE Rs 300 Cr + Net Income Rs 100 Cr - Dividends Rs 20 Cr = Ending RE Rs 380 Cr.

> Note: If the balance sheet is off by exactly net income, this link is almost always missing.

**Cash & Debt Roll-Forwards**

```
Ending Cash = Beginning Cash + Net Change in Cash
Ending Debt  = Beginning Debt + Drawdowns - Repayments
```

> Example: Beginning Cash Rs 50 Cr + Net Change Rs 10 Cr = Rs 60 Cr.

**Interest Expense (the source of circularity)**

```
Interest Expense = Interest Rate × Average Debt
                 = Rate × (Beginning Debt + Ending Debt) / 2
```

> Note: Interest on average debt creates a loop: interest → net income → cash → debt balance → average debt → interest. Resolve with iterative calculation in Excel.

Depreciation flows to both a CF add-back and a PP&E reduction. Net Change in Cash flows to the Balance Sheet cash line. A dashed feedback loop from the Balance Sheet Debt line back to Interest Expense is labelled in red: **CIRCULARITY: interest → debt → cash → net income → interest.** Balance check row: Assets - Liabilities - Equity = 0.

### Deep Dive — Balancing, the Plug, and Taming Circularity

**What 'the plug' actually is**

The 'plug' is the line that absorbs leftover cash. In most models this is cash and a revolver (revolving credit facility): surplus cash builds the cash balance; a shortfall draws the revolver. This cash-and-revolver sweep makes the balance sheet self-balance. Never hardcode a number to force a balance — that hides a real error.

**Why interest creates a circular reference, and how to resolve it**

Three accepted fixes: (1) enable iterative calculation in Excel (File > Options > Formulas > Enable iterative calculation); (2) add a 'circularity switch' — a toggle cell that breaks the loop to zero when clearing errors; (3) compute interest on beginning debt instead of average debt, removing the loop entirely at a small cost in precision. CFI expects you to name iterative calculation as the standard remedy.

**Debugging a balance sheet that won't balance**

Work a fixed checklist: Is net income linked into retained earnings? Is ending cash from the CF statement linked to the cash line? Is depreciation consistent across all three places? Does every working-capital change have a matching CF line? Is capex flowing to both PP&E and the investing section? The size of the imbalance is a powerful clue: an imbalance equal to net income → missing retained-earnings link; equal to depreciation → missing add-back or PP&E entry.

**References**

- CFI Building a 3-Statement Model — linking logic, supporting schedules and the balancing mechanism.
- Standard treatment of cash-and-revolver as the balance-sheet plug.
- Excel iterative-calculation setting as the conventional fix for interest-driven circularity.

### Example — Tracing One Year Through the Model

**Data:** Net Income Rs 100 Cr (after Rs 40 Cr depreciation and Rs 10.8 Cr interest). CFO = 100 + 40 - 20 = Rs 120 Cr. CFI = -Rs 60 Cr (CapEx). CFF = -Rs 50 Cr (debt repayment Rs 30 Cr + dividends Rs 20 Cr). Net change in cash = Rs 10 Cr. Beginning cash Rs 50 Cr → ending cash Rs 60 Cr. PP&E = 200 + 60 - 40 = Rs 220 Cr. RE = 300 + 100 - 20 = Rs 380 Cr. Debt = 150 - 30 = Rs 120 Cr.

**Analysis:** Net income does its double job: drives the Rs 10 Cr cash build (CF statement) and lifts RE from Rs 300 Cr to Rs 380 Cr (balance sheet). Depreciation appears three times — IS expense, CF add-back, PP&E reduction. Every link is live → balance check reads zero.

**Analyst takeaway:** A balanced model is the inevitable result of wiring every connection correctly. Break any one link and the check row tells you instantly.

### Common Mistakes — The Linking Errors That Break the Model

1. **Not flowing net income into retained earnings.** *Fix:* Link RE as Beginning RE + Net Income - Dividends.
2. **Forgetting to add depreciation back in the cash flow statement.** *Fix:* Add it back in operating cash flow — it must appear in IS, CF add-backs, and PP&E schedule, all consistently.
3. **Breaking the cash link — not feeding ending cash to the balance sheet.** *Fix:* Set balance-sheet cash = beginning cash + net change in cash from CF statement.
4. **Hardcoding a number to force the balance sheet to balance.** *Fix:* Never hardcode the plug. Use a proper cash-and-revolver sweep.

### Summary — Session 4 — What to Lock In

- A three-statement model is one integrated system; build it: income statement → cash flow statement → balance sheet (last, because it depends on the other two).
- Net income flows to two places: the top of the cash flow statement and retained earnings. Ending cash from the CF feeds the balance-sheet cash line.
- Master the roll-forwards (PP&E = begin + capex - depreciation; RE = begin + net income - dividends) and keep a balance check row = 0.
- Interest on average debt creates a circular reference; standard fix is enabling iterative calculation in Excel.
- When the balance sheet won't balance, debug by the imbalance amount. Never hardcode the plug.

## Session 5 · DCF Valuation — FCFF, WACC, Terminal Value & the Equity Bridge

*The full DCF mechanics CFI expects: unlevered free cash flow, discounting at WACC, terminal value by Gordon-growth and exit-multiple, and the enterprise-to-equity bridge.*

**By the end of this session you will be able to:**

- Build unlevered free cash flow (FCFF) from operating inputs
- Calculate WACC and discount cash flows correctly
- Compute terminal value using both Gordon-growth and exit-multiple methods
- Bridge from enterprise value to equity value per share

### Hook — Value Is Just Discounted Cash Flow

Strip away every buzzword and intrinsic valuation reduces to one idea: a business is worth the cash it will generate in the future, discounted back to today. The DCF model is the single most heavily tested valuation method on the FMVA final. Almost every DCF question probes one of four things: can you build free cash flow correctly, can you compute WACC, can you calculate terminal value the right way, and can you bridge from enterprise value to equity value per share.

### Concept — The Logic: Forecast, Discount, Bridge

A DCF has three movements: (1) forecast free cash flow for an explicit period (usually 5–10 years); (2) capture everything beyond that horizon in a terminal value, and discount both back to today at the appropriate rate; (3) bridge from the result to value per share. The crucial fork: FCFF is cash to all capital providers and is discounted at WACC → enterprise value. FCFE is cash to equity holders only and is discounted at the cost of equity → equity value directly. Always pair FCFF with WACC.

> **Analogy.** FCFF is the rent the property earns before deciding how it is financed — it belongs to the bank (the mortgage) and to you (the owner) together, so you value it at a blended cost of capital. FCFE is what is left for you personally after the mortgage payment, so you value it at your own required return. Mixing them up produces a meaningless answer.

**Key terms**

- **FCFF (unlevered FCF):** Cash available to all capital providers; discounted at WACC to give enterprise value.
- **FCFE (levered FCF):** Cash available to equity holders only; discounted at cost of equity to give equity value directly.
- **Terminal value:** The value of all cash flows beyond the explicit forecast, captured in a single figure.
- **Enterprise vs equity value:** Enterprise value is the whole firm; equity value subtracts net debt.

### Formulas — The DCF Formula Stack

**Unlevered Free Cash Flow (FCFF)**

```
FCFF = EBIT × (1 - tax rate) + D&A - CapEx - Increase in NWC
```

> Example: EBIT Rs 200 Cr × 0.75 = 150; + D&A 50 - CapEx 70 - NWC increase 20 = FCFF Rs 110 Cr.

> Note: FCFF uses EBIT × (1 - tax), NOT net income, because it excludes the financing (interest) decision.

**Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC)**

```
WACC = (E/V) × Re + (D/V) × Rd × (1 - tax rate)
```

- E/V, D/V — Market-value weights of equity and debt.
- Re — Cost of equity (from CAPM).
- Rd × (1 - tax) — After-tax cost of debt.

> Example: E Rs 600 Cr, D Rs 400 Cr, Re 12%, Rd 8%, tax 25% → (0.6 × 12) + (0.4 × 8 × 0.75) = 7.2 + 2.4 = 9.6%.

> Note: Use market values for weights; apply the tax shield only to the cost of debt.

**Cost of Equity (CAPM)**

```
Re = Rf + Beta × (Rm - Rf)
```

> Example: Rf 7%, Beta 1.2, equity risk premium 6% → Re = 7 + 1.2 × 6 = 14.2%.

**Terminal Value — Gordon (Perpetuity) Growth**

```
TV(n) = FCFF(n) × (1 + g) / (WACC - g)
```

> Example: FCFF(5) Rs 250 Cr, g 4%, WACC 10% → TV = 250 × 1.04 / 0.06 = Rs 4,333 Cr.

> Note: Forgetting the (1 + g) is a classic exam error. g must never equal or exceed WACC.

**Terminal Value — Exit Multiple**

```
TV(n) = EBITDA(n) × Exit Multiple
```

> Example: EBITDA(5) Rs 500 Cr × 9.0x = TV Rs 4,500 Cr.

**Enterprise Value (discounting)**

```
EV = Σ [FCFF(t) / (1 + WACC)^t] + TV(n) / (1 + WACC)^n
```

> Example: PV of explicit FCFF Rs 1,800 Cr + PV of TV (4,333 / 1.10^5 = Rs 2,690 Cr) = EV Rs 4,490 Cr.

> Note: Terminal value is typically 60–80% of total enterprise value.

**Enterprise-to-Equity Bridge**

```
Equity Value     = Enterprise Value - Net Debt
Value per Share  = Equity Value / Shares Outstanding
```

> Example: EV Rs 4,490 Cr - Net Debt Rs 490 Cr = Equity Rs 4,000 Cr; / 100 Cr shares = Rs 40 per share.

> Note: Skipping the net-debt subtraction is the single most common DCF mistake on the exam.

### Visual — The DCF Bridge

Left-to-right waterfall: PV of Explicit FCFF (Years 1–5) ~Rs 1,800 Cr → + PV of Terminal Value ~Rs 2,690 Cr → **ENTERPRISE VALUE = Rs 4,490 Cr** (terminal value ~60% of total) → less Net Debt Rs 490 Cr → **EQUITY VALUE = Rs 4,000 Cr** → ÷ 100 Cr shares → **INTRINSIC VALUE = Rs 40 per share.** Red annotation: *'Stop at enterprise value and you've valued the whole firm, not the equity — always subtract net debt.'*

### Deep Dive — Inside WACC, Terminal Value, and the Consistency Rule

**Building WACC correctly**

WACC blends the after-tax cost of debt and the cost of equity (from CAPM), weighted by market values. The tax shield is why debt is cheaper than equity after tax — apply it only to the cost of debt. Use market capitalisation for equity weights, not book equity.

**Two ways to terminal value, and why it dominates**

The perpetuity-growth (Gordon) method assumes FCF grows forever at rate g (must be below WACC, anchored to long-run inflation/GDP). The exit-multiple method assumes the firm is sold for a market multiple of final-year EBITDA. Because terminal value is typically 60–80% of total enterprise value, small changes in g or the exit multiple swing the valuation heavily. Always compute both and cross-check.

**The consistency rule: match the cash flow to the rate**

FCFF discounted at WACC → enterprise value. FCFE discounted at cost of equity → equity value directly. Discounting FCFF at the cost of equity, or FCFE at WACC, double-counts or omits the financing effect. Whenever a question gives you a cash flow and a rate, check they are paired correctly first.

**References**

- CFI DCF Modeling — FCFF construction, WACC, terminal value methods and the equity bridge.
- CAPM as the standard cost-of-equity model and the after-tax treatment of the cost of debt.

### Example — Valuing an IT Services Firm by DCF

**Data:** WACC 10%, PV of five explicit years of FCFF = Rs 1,800 Cr, final-year FCFF = Rs 250 Cr, g = 4%. TV = 250 × 1.04 / 0.06 = Rs 4,333 Cr. PV of TV = 4,333 / 1.10^5 = Rs 2,690 Cr. EV = Rs 4,490 Cr. Debt Rs 700 Cr, cash Rs 210 Cr (net debt Rs 490 Cr), 100 Cr shares.

**Analysis:** Equity value = 4,490 - 490 = Rs 4,000 Cr. Per share = Rs 40. Terminal value (Rs 2,690 Cr) is ~60% of EV. Had g been 5% instead of 4%, TV would jump to Rs 5,250 Cr, lifting the per-share value well above Rs 40 — illustrating how sensitive DCF is to terminal assumptions.

**Analyst takeaway:** Always discount FCFF at WACC, subtract net debt, and divide by the share count — in that order.

### Common Mistakes — The DCF Errors That Cost the Most

1. **Discounting FCFF at the cost of equity (or FCFE at WACC).** *Fix:* Pair them: FCFF → WACC (enterprise value); FCFE → cost of equity (equity value directly).
2. **Reporting enterprise value per share without subtracting net debt.** *Fix:* Equity Value = Enterprise Value - Net Debt, then divide by shares.
3. **Setting the perpetual growth rate equal to or above WACC.** *Fix:* g must be strictly less than WACC (realistically near long-run inflation/GDP).
4. **Forgetting the (1 + g) in the terminal value numerator.** *Fix:* TV = FCFF(n) × (1 + g) / (WACC - g). The (1 + g) grows the final year's flow into the first perpetuity year.

### Summary — Session 5 — What to Lock In

- A DCF forecasts free cash flow, discounts it (plus a terminal value) to today, and bridges to value per share.
- FCFF = EBIT × (1 - tax) + D&A - CapEx - increase in NWC, discounted at WACC → enterprise value. FCFE discounted at cost of equity → equity value directly. Always match the cash flow to the rate.
- WACC = (E/V)Re + (D/V)Rd(1 - tax), with market-value weights and the tax shield applied only to debt; cost of equity from CAPM.
- Terminal value (Gordon: FCFF(n)(1 + g)/(WACC - g), g < WACC; or exit multiple: EBITDA(n) × multiple) is usually 60–80% of total value.
- Equity Value = Enterprise Value - Net Debt, then ÷ shares. Skipping the net-debt step is the most common DCF error.

## Session 6 · Comparable Company & Precedent Transaction Analysis

*Relative valuation as the exam frames it: trading comps, precedent-transaction comps, the key multiples, and when each multiple is appropriate.*

**By the end of this session you will be able to:**

- Build a trading-comps set and apply the right multiple
- Distinguish trading comps from precedent-transaction comps
- Explain the control premium in transaction multiples
- Choose between EV/EBITDA, P/E and EV/Revenue for a given case

### Hook — What Would the Market Actually Pay?

A DCF tells you what a business is worth in theory. Relative valuation answers a different, blunter question: what would the market actually pay for it right now? Comparable company analysis looks at how similar public companies trade today; precedent transaction analysis looks at what acquirers actually paid for similar companies in past M&A deals. The FMVA exam tests: which multiple to use, the iron rule of pairing EV multiples with the right metric, and the control premium that makes acquisitions cost more than the market price.

### Concept — Two Methods, One Logic

Relative valuation values a company by comparison rather than by forecasting its own cash flows. **Trading comps** (comparable company analysis): similar listed companies, compute their multiples, take the median or mean, apply to the target. **Precedent transaction comps** (M&A comps): multiples paid in completed acquisitions of similar companies. The crucial distinction: transaction multiples embed a **control premium** — acquirers pay above the trading price to gain control and capture synergies — so precedent transactions typically yield higher valuations. Both methods reflect current market sentiment, which is their strength and weakness. Quality lives or dies on peer selection: peers must resemble the target in industry, size, geography, growth and margins.

> **Analogy.** Trading comps are like checking the asking and selling prices of similar flats currently on the market. Precedent transactions are like the prices similar flats actually sold for recently, including any premium a buyer paid in a bidding war. The second number is usually higher.

**Key terms**

- **Comparable company analysis:** Valuing a target using the trading multiples of similar listed peers.
- **Precedent transaction analysis:** Valuing a target using multiples paid in past acquisitions of similar companies.
- **Control premium:** The amount above the unaffected market price an acquirer pays to gain control.
- **Peer set:** The group of comparable companies chosen to match the target on industry, size, geography, growth and margin.

### Formulas — Multiples, the EV Bridge, and the Control Premium

**Applying a Valuation Multiple**

```
Implied Value = Target Metric × Peer Multiple (median or mean)
```

> Example: Target EBITDA Rs 400 Cr × median EV/EBITDA 15x = implied enterprise value Rs 6,000 Cr.

> Note: An EV multiple gives enterprise value (then bridge to equity); an equity multiple like P/E gives share price or equity value directly.

**Enterprise Value Multiples**

```
EV/EBITDA  (capital-structure neutral, the workhorse)
EV/EBIT
EV/Revenue  (for loss-making or early-stage firms)
```

> Note: EV multiples pair with pre-interest metrics available to all capital providers.

**Equity Value Multiples**

```
P/E = Price / EPS
P/B = Price / Book Value per Share
```

> Note: Equity multiples pair with post-interest metrics that belong only to equity holders.

**Enterprise Value Bridge**

```
Enterprise Value = Market Cap + Total Debt - Cash (+ Minority Interest + Preferred)
```

> Example: Market cap Rs 6,200 Cr + net debt (-Rs 200 Cr) = EV Rs 6,000 Cr.

**Control Premium**

```
Control Premium % = (Offer Price - Unaffected Price) / Unaffected Price
```

> Example: Offer Rs 600 vs unaffected Rs 480 → (600 - 480) / 480 = 25%.

### Deep Dive — The Matching Rule, the Workhorse Multiple, and Why M&A Costs More

**The iron rule: match the multiple to the metric**

EV multiples (EV/EBITDA, EV/EBIT, EV/Revenue) must pair with pre-interest metrics because EV represents the whole business. Equity multiples (P/E, P/B) must pair with post-interest metrics because the share price represents only equity after debt holders are paid. Therefore EV/EBITDA and P/E are valid, but EV/Net income and P/EBITDA are nonsensical mismatches. When a question offers EV/Net income as an option, it is testing exactly this.

**Why EV/EBITDA is the workhorse**

EV/EBITDA dominates cross-company comparison for two reasons: (1) it is capital-structure neutral — two otherwise identical companies with different debt loads should have similar EV/EBITDA; (2) it strips out differences in depreciation policy. Its weakness is that it ignores capital intensity.

**Precedent transactions and the control premium**

Transaction multiples are almost always higher than trading multiples for two reasons: (1) the control premium — acquirers pay above the prevailing share price to secure control; (2) synergies — a strategic buyer expects to extract cost or revenue synergies. Precedent data can also be stale. Analysts run both methods alongside a DCF and present results as a football field.

**References**

- CFI Comparable Valuation Analysis — trading comps, transaction comps and multiple selection.
- The football-field chart used to present valuation ranges across DCF, trading comps and precedent transactions.

### Example — Valuing a Mid-Cap IT Firm by Comps

**Data:** Peer EV/EBITDA (illustrative): A 18.0x, B 16.0x, C 14.0x, D 12.0x → median 15.0x. Target EBITDA Rs 400 Cr. Trading comps implied EV = Rs 6,000 Cr. Target has net cash Rs 200 Cr → equity value Rs 6,200 Cr. Precedent transaction: similar firm acquired at 18.0x → implied EV Rs 7,200 Cr (25% control premium).

**Analysis:** Trading comps: EV Rs 6,000 Cr (equity Rs 6,200 Cr). Precedent: EV Rs 7,200 Cr (~20% higher, reflecting control premium). Used together with a DCF, these form a football field. Note: because the firm holds net cash, equity value exceeds enterprise value — the bridge runs in reverse.

**Analyst takeaway:** Expect precedent transactions to sit above trading comps because of the control premium, apply the median multiple, and always bridge EV multiples to equity using net debt.

### Common Mistakes — The Comps Errors That Cost Marks

1. **Pairing an EV multiple with an equity metric (e.g. EV/Net income or P/EBITDA).** *Fix:* EV multiples → pre-interest metrics; equity multiples → post-interest metrics.
2. **Forgetting the control premium in precedent transaction analysis.** *Fix:* Transaction multiples embed a control premium and often synergies, so they sit above trading comps.
3. **Choosing a poor peer set.** *Fix:* Select peers matching the target on industry, size, geography, growth and margin profile.
4. **Comparing P/E across companies with very different leverage.** *Fix:* Use EV/EBITDA for cross-company comparison when leverage differs.

### Summary — Session 6 — What to Lock In

- Relative valuation prices a company by comparison: trading comps (live peer trading multiples) and precedent transactions (acquisition multiples).
- Precedent transactions usually value companies higher than trading comps because they embed a control premium and expected synergies.
- Iron matching rule: EV multiples → pre-interest metrics; equity multiples → post-interest metrics. EV/Net income and P/EBITDA are invalid.
- EV/EBITDA is the workhorse (neutral to capital structure and depreciation policy); apply the peer median to the target metric, then bridge EV to equity using net debt.
- Control premium % = (offer - unaffected) / unaffected; present all methods together as a football field.

## Session 7 · Excel for Financial Modeling — Functions, Shortcuts & Case Studies

**By the end of this session you will be able to:**

### Hook — The Analyst's Instrument

Every concept in this program ultimately lives inside one tool: Excel. The FMVA exam takes this seriously: roughly a sixth of the final is Excel-based case studies, and they do not merely ask what a function does — they hand you data and make you compute. You can know every formula in finance and still lose marks if you reach for the wrong function, mishandle Excel's NPV timing quirk, or build so messily that you can't trace your own answer.

### Concept — Four Function Families and One Way to Build

The functions that matter fall into four families: **Lookup functions** (XLOOKUP, INDEX/MATCH, VLOOKUP) pull a value from a table by a key. **Logical functions** (IF, IFS, AND, OR, IFERROR) make a model respond to conditions. **Conditional aggregation** (SUMIFS, COUNTIFS, AVERAGEIFS) totals data meeting criteria. **Financial functions** (NPV, IRR, XNPV, XIRR, PMT) handle time value of money. Beyond functions, build models in clear zones (inputs, calculations, outputs), colour-code (blue for hardcoded inputs, black for formulas), never bury a hardcoded number inside a formula, and keep formulas consistent across a row.

> **Analogy.** Think of Excel like a professional kitchen. The four function families are your core stations, and clean modeling is the mise en place: everything labelled and in its place so that under the pressure of a dinner rush you move fast and never poison the dish with a hidden mistake.

**Key terms**

- **Lookup functions:** Retrieve a value from a table by a key — XLOOKUP, INDEX/MATCH, VLOOKUP.
- **Conditional aggregation:** Total or count data meeting criteria — SUMIFS, COUNTIFS.
- **Financial functions:** Time-value-of-money calculations — NPV, IRR, XNPV, XIRR, PMT.
- **Colour coding:** Blue for hardcoded inputs, black for formulas.

### Formulas — The High-Yield Excel Functions

**XLOOKUP / INDEX-MATCH (lookups)**

```excel
=XLOOKUP(lookup_value, lookup_array, return_array)
=INDEX(return_array, MATCH(lookup_value, lookup_array, 0))
```

> Example: `=XLOOKUP("Infosys", A2:A10, C2:C10)` returns the value in column C on the Infosys row.

> Note: Unlike VLOOKUP, they can look left and don't break when columns are inserted. Always use exact match (0).

**IF / IFERROR (logical)**

```excel
=IF(logical_test, value_if_true, value_if_false)
=IFERROR(value, value_if_error)
```

> Example: `=IF(EBIT>0, "Profit", "Loss")` ; `=IFERROR(Revenue/Units, 0)`

> Note: Wrap division and lookups in IFERROR so a single #N/A doesn't cascade through the model.

**SUMIFS (conditional aggregation)**

```excel
=SUMIFS(sum_range, criteria_range1, criteria1, criteria_range2, criteria2, ...)
```

> Example: `=SUMIFS(Sales, Region, "North", Product, "A")` — totals sales where region is North and product is A.

> Note: SUMIFS (plural) takes multiple conditions; SUMIF (singular) takes only one.

**NPV / XNPV (present value)**

```excel
=NPV(rate, value1, value2, ...)
=XNPV(rate, values, dates)
```

> Example: Time-0 outflow Rs 1,000 + inflows Rs 600, Rs 600 at 10%: `=-1000 + NPV(10%, 600, 600)` = Rs 41.3 Cr.

> **Critical trap:** A cash flow at time 0 must sit OUTSIDE the NPV function, because Excel discounts NPV's first argument by one full period. Or use XNPV with real dates.

**IRR / XIRR (rate of return)**

```excel
=IRR(values)
=XIRR(values, dates)
```

> Example: `=IRR({-1000, 600, 600})` ≈ 13.1%. Since 13.1% > the 10% hurdle, the project adds value.

> Note: Use IRR for equal annual periods; use XIRR the moment cash flows fall on irregular dates.

**PMT & Cell Referencing**

```excel
=PMT(rate, nper, pv)
```

> Example: `=PMT(8%/12, 60, -500000)` — monthly payment on a Rs 5,00,000 loan over 5 years at 8% annual.

> Note: Lock assumption cells with $ (press F4 to toggle) so copying a formula always points back to them.

### Deep Dive — The NPV Trap, Robust Lookups, and Clean Models

**Excel's NPV timing trap (the most tested Excel pitfall)**

Excel's NPV function does NOT behave the way the textbook NPV concept does. It assumes the first value in its arguments occurs one period from now, not today. If you have an initial investment at time 0 followed by future inflows, you must NOT put the time-0 outflow inside NPV — you add it separately: `=initial_outflow + NPV(rate, future_flows)`, with the outflow as a negative. The clean alternative is XNPV, which takes actual dates of every cash flow and discounts each by its true elapsed time. The same logic applies to IRR versus XIRR.

**Why INDEX-MATCH and XLOOKUP beat VLOOKUP**

VLOOKUP can only look to the right of the lookup column, and it references the return column by position number — so inserting a column silently returns wrong data. INDEX-MATCH separates 'where to look' (MATCH) from 'what to return' (INDEX), can look in any direction, and survives column insertions. XLOOKUP is the modern single function that does all of this directly.

**Modeling best practice: the habits that prevent errors**

Separate inputs from calculations from outputs. Colour-code: blue for hardcoded inputs, black for formulas. Never embed a hardcoded number inside a formula (write `=Revenue*0.18` is bad — put 0.18 in a labelled input cell). Keep each formula identical across a row so it copies cleanly. Lock assumption cells with absolute references ($).

**References**

- CFI Excel Fundamentals and Excel Modeling courses — functions, shortcuts and best-practice formatting.
- Excel's documented NPV assumption that the first cash flow occurs one period in the future.

### Example — An Excel Case Study, Worked

**Data:** Cash flows — time 0: -Rs 1,000 Cr; Year 1: +Rs 600 Cr; Year 2: +Rs 600 Cr. Discount rate 10% (pulled by XLOOKUP from a risk-tier table).

**NPV (built correctly):** `=-1000 + NPV(10%, 600, 600)` = 545.5 + 495.9 - 1,000 = **Rs 41.3 Cr.**

**IRR:** `=IRR({-1000, 600, 600})` ≈ **13.1%.**

**Analysis:** Two decisions made the answer correct. (1) The time-0 outflow sat OUTSIDE the NPV function. (2) The discount rate was pulled by lookup rather than typed in. Both metrics agree: positive NPV and IRR above the 10% hurdle.

**Analyst takeaway:** In Excel case studies, the marks turn on Excel mechanics: keep time-0 cash flows outside NPV (or use XNPV), pull inputs by lookup, and let NPV and IRR cross-check each other.

### Common Mistakes — The Excel Errors That Cost Case-Study Marks

1. **Putting a time-0 cash flow inside Excel's NPV function.** *Fix:* Add the time-0 cash flow outside: `=CF0 + NPV(rate, future_flows)`, or use XNPV with actual dates.
2. **Relying on VLOOKUP in a model that will change.** *Fix:* Use INDEX-MATCH or XLOOKUP. Always force an exact match.
3. **Hardcoding a number inside a formula (e.g. `=Revenue*0.18`).** *Fix:* Put assumptions in labelled input cells (blue) and reference them.
4. **Forgetting absolute references ($) when copying a formula.** *Fix:* Lock assumption cells with $ (press F4). Otherwise a copied formula shifts its reference and silently returns wrong numbers.

## Session 8 · Budgeting, Forecasting & FP&A

*Financial planning & analysis as tested: forecasting methods, revenue and cost drivers, the budgeting cycle, and variance analysis.*

**By the end of this session you will be able to:**

- Apply common forecasting methods (straight-line, driver-based, regression)
- Build revenue and cost forecasts from operational drivers
- Compute and interpret budget-vs-actual variances
- Explain the rolling-forecast and budgeting cycle

### Hook — Where Finance Stops Looking Back and Starts Looking Forward

Accounting records what happened. FP&A asks what will happen next — and what the business should do about it. The FMVA exam tests three things here: how companies forecast (the methods and their trade-offs), how they budget (incremental versus zero-based, static versus flexible), and how they analyse variances when actuals diverge from the plan — including the sign discipline that trips up so many candidates.

### Concept — Forecast, Budget, Analyse

FP&A runs a continuous loop: forecast the future, set a budget as the financial plan, track actual results, then analyse the variances and re-forecast. **Forecasting methods:** straight-line (constant growth rate — quick but crude), driver-based (units × price — defensible, ties to real business levers), regression/moving averages (patterns from history), top-down (market to share to company) vs bottom-up (individual products/salespeople aggregated). **Budgeting styles:** incremental (adjust last year) vs zero-based (rebuild from nothing, justifying every rupee). A static budget is fixed at one planned activity level; a flexible budget adjusts to the actual level of activity, making it far fairer for judging variable-cost performance.

> **Analogy.** FP&A is like planning and running a road trip. The forecast is your estimated arrival time based on distance and speed. The budget is the fuel and food money you set aside. Variance analysis is comparing actual progress against the plan. A rolling forecast is checking the GPS continuously and updating your ETA, rather than trusting a single guess made before you left the driveway.

**Key terms**

- **Driver-based forecasting:** Building a forecast from operational drivers (e.g. units × price) rather than a blanket growth rate.
- **Zero-based budgeting (ZBB):** Rebuilding the budget from zero each period, justifying every expense.
- **Flexible budget:** A budget recalculated at the actual level of activity, so variable costs are judged fairly.
- **Variance analysis:** Comparing actual results to budget to explain and act on the difference (favourable or unfavourable).

### Formulas — Forecasting and Variance Formulas

**Straight-Line (Growth) Forecast**

```
Forecast = Prior Period × (1 + growth rate)
```

> Example: Revenue Rs 200 Cr × 1.08 = Rs 216 Cr next year.

**Compound Annual Growth Rate (CAGR)**

```
CAGR = (Ending Value / Beginning Value)^(1/n) - 1
```

> Example: Rs 100 Cr to Rs 146.4 Cr over 3 years → (146.4/100)^(1/3) - 1 ≈ 13.5%.

**Driver-Based Revenue**

```
Revenue = Units Sold × Price per Unit
```

> Example: 10,00,000 units × Rs 500 = Rs 500 Cr budgeted revenue.

**Variance and Variance %**

```
Variance   = Actual - Budgeted
Variance % = Variance / Budgeted
```

> Example: Costs: Actual Rs 540 Cr - Budget Rs 500 Cr = Rs 40 Cr unfavourable (more was spent than planned).

> Note: Sign discipline is everything. A +Rs 40 Cr is favourable on a revenue line but unfavourable on a cost line.

**Price and Volume Variance (flexible-budget decomposition)**

```
Price Variance  = (Actual Price - Budget Price) × Actual Units
Volume Variance = (Actual Units - Budget Units) × Budget Price
```

> Example: Price: (480 - 500) × 11,00,000 = -Rs 22 Cr ; Volume: (11,00,000 - 10,00,000) × 500 = +Rs 50 Cr ; net = +Rs 28 Cr.

> Note: The two parts always add back to the total variance.

### Visual — The FP&A Cycle and the Variance Bridge

**Top panel — FP&A Loop:** Circular flow: PLAN → BUDGET → FORECAST → ACTUALS → VARIANCE & RE-FORECAST → (back to FORECAST). Captioned: *'continuous, not once a year.'*

**Bottom panel — Revenue Variance Bridge:** Waterfall from Budgeted Revenue Rs 500 Cr → +Volume variance Rs 50 Cr → -Price variance Rs 22 Cr → Actual Revenue Rs 528 Cr (net +Rs 28 Cr favourable). Callout: *'The favourable total hides a price problem offset by strong volume — which is exactly why you decompose.'*

### Deep Dive — Methods, Budgets, and the Discipline of Variance

**Choosing a forecasting method**

No single method is best. Straight-line growth is fine for stable, mature revenue but misleading for fast-changing lines. Driver-based forecasting is the gold standard when you understand the operational levers — it ties the forecast to units, price, capacity or customers. Moving averages and regression suit data with discernible patterns or seasonality. Top-down is quick and good for a high-level view; bottom-up is more granular and accountable. Strong FP&A teams often build both and reconcile them.

**Budgeting approaches and static versus flexible**

Incremental budgeting starts from last year and adjusts — easy and stable, but it bakes in past inefficiencies. Zero-based budgeting rebuilds from zero and forces every expense to justify itself. The static-versus-flexible distinction matters enormously for variance analysis: a static budget is fixed at one planned volume; a flexible budget recalculates budgeted costs at the actual activity level, isolating true efficiency from volume effects.

**Variance sign discipline and rolling forecasts**

A variance is favourable only if it helps profit: for revenue, actual above budget is favourable; for costs, actual below budget is favourable. Calling a cost overrun 'favourable' because the number is positive is a classic slip. Decomposing variances (price vs volume for revenue; rate vs efficiency for costs) turns a number into an explanation management can act on. Rolling forecasts — continuously updated projections that always look a fixed number of periods ahead — keep the plan honest as conditions change.

**References**

- CFI FP&A and Budgeting & Forecasting courses.

### Example — Forecast, Budget, and the Variance Story

**Data:** Budget (driver-based): 10,00,000 units × Rs 500 = Rs 500 Cr. Actual: 11,00,000 units × Rs 480 = Rs 528 Cr. Total variance = +Rs 28 Cr (favourable). Decompose: Volume variance = +Rs 50 Cr favourable; Price variance = -Rs 22 Cr unfavourable. Check: 50 - 22 = +Rs 28 Cr.

**Analysis:** Revenue beat budget by Rs 28 Cr — a clean win on the surface. But the company sold 10% more units (Rs 50 Cr tailwind) yet had to drop price by 4% (Rs 22 Cr headwind). The favourable total masks margin erosion from discounting — a very different action plan from 'everything is fine.'

**Analyst takeaway:** Always decompose a revenue variance into price and volume. The components add back to the total but reveal what the single number conceals.

### Common Mistakes — The FP&A Errors That Cost Marks

1. **Getting the variance sign wrong (calling a cost overrun 'favourable').** *Fix:* Favourable only if it helps profit: actual above budget is favourable for revenue, unfavourable for costs.
2. **Judging variable-cost performance against a static budget.** *Fix:* Use a flexible budget — recalculate budgeted costs at the actual activity level.
3. **Confusing a budget with a forecast.** *Fix:* A budget is the agreed plan/target; a forecast is the latest best estimate. They serve different purposes and often differ.
4. **Straight-lining every line item with one growth rate.** *Fix:* Use driver-based forecasting where you understand the levers. Never assume one rate fits everything.

### Summary — Session 8 — What to Lock In

- FP&A is the forward-looking loop: forecast, budget, track actuals, analyse variances, re-forecast — turning numbers into decisions.
- Forecasting methods: straight-line (stable lines), driver-based (units × price, the defensible default), regression/moving average (patterns), top-down vs bottom-up.
- Budgeting: incremental (adjust last year) vs zero-based (rebuild from zero); static (fixed volume) vs flexible (adjusted to actual activity, fairer for variable costs).
- Variance = Actual - Budget, but sign discipline rules: actual above budget is favourable for revenue, unfavourable for costs. Decompose revenue variances into price and volume.
- Use CAGR = (ending/beginning)^(1/n) - 1 for multi-year growth; prefer rolling forecasts over a static annual budget.

## Session 9 · Capital Budgeting & Corporate Finance — NPV, IRR & Decisions

*Investment-decision tools and corporate-finance fundamentals: NPV, IRR, MIRR, payback, and the cost-of-capital logic behind accept/reject decisions.*

**By the end of this session you will be able to:**

- Compute NPV, IRR, MIRR and payback for a project
- Apply the correct accept/reject decision rule
- Explain the limitations of IRR versus NPV
- Connect cost of capital to capital-allocation decisions

### Hook — How Companies Decide Where to Bet Billions

Every major corporate decision — build the new plant, launch the product, acquire the rival — is ultimately a capital budgeting decision: should we commit money now in exchange for cash flows later? The FMVA exam tests the toolkit precisely: NPV, IRR, payback, the profitability index, and the decision rule attached to each. It also tests the conflicts — the situations where IRR and NPV disagree and choosing the wrong one destroys value.

### Concept — The Toolkit and Its Decision Rules

**Capital budgeting tools and accept rules:**

- **NPV:** Discount all cash flows to today and subtract the initial investment. **Accept if NPV > 0.**
- **IRR:** The discount rate that makes NPV exactly zero. **Accept if IRR > hurdle rate.**
- **Payback period:** Time to recover the initial outlay. **Accept if payback < target.**
- **Profitability index (PI):** PV of future cash flows / investment. **Accept if PI > 1.**

For **independent projects**: accept all with positive NPV. For **mutually exclusive projects**: rank by NPV, not IRR — NPV measures absolute value created. The hurdle rate is usually WACC.

> **Analogy.** NPV is the rupees of value a deal adds after accounting for what the money could have earned elsewhere — the number that actually grows the fund. IRR is the deal's annualised return rate, useful but blind to size. Payback is simply how fast you get your stake back. A disciplined investor lets NPV drive the final choice.

**Key terms**

- **NPV:** PV of all cash flows minus the initial investment; accept if positive. The theoretically superior rule.
- **IRR:** The discount rate that makes NPV zero; accept if it exceeds the hurdle.
- **Profitability index (PI):** PV of future cash flows / initial investment; accept if > 1.
- **Mutually exclusive projects:** Projects where choosing one rules out the others, so you rank by NPV rather than IRR.

### Formulas — The Capital Budgeting Formulas and Rules

**Net Present Value (NPV)**

```
NPV = Σ [CF_t / (1 + r)^t] - Initial Investment    (accept if NPV > 0)
```

> Example: Inflows Rs 400/450/500 Cr over 3 years at 10%, investment Rs 1,000 Cr → PV = 1,111 Cr; NPV = Rs 111 Cr.

> Note: NPV is in currency, so it captures absolute value created — which is why it is the decisive rule.

**Internal Rate of Return (IRR)**

```
IRR: the rate r where NPV = 0    (accept if IRR > hurdle rate)
```

> Example: For the above cash flows, IRR ≈ 16%; since 16% > 10% hurdle, accept.

> Note: IRR is intuitive but blind to scale and assumes interim cash flows are reinvested at the IRR itself.

**Payback & Discounted Payback**

```
Payback Period = time for cumulative cash flow to recover the initial investment
```

> Example: Investment Rs 1,000 Cr; inflows Rs 400/450/500 Cr → payback about 2.3 years.

> Note: Simple payback ignores the time value of money and anything after payback. Discounted payback fixes the time-value flaw but still ignores post-payback cash flows.

**Profitability Index (PI)**

```
PI = PV of Future Cash Flows / Initial Investment    (accept if PI > 1)
```

> Example: PV of inflows Rs 1,200 Cr / investment Rs 1,000 Cr = PI 1.2 (NPV = Rs 200 Cr).

> Note: PI > 1 is equivalent to NPV > 0. PI helps rank projects under a capital constraint.

**Modified IRR (MIRR)**

```
MIRR = (FV of inflows at the cost of capital / PV of outflows)^(1/n) - 1
```

MIRR fixes IRR's unrealistic reinvestment assumption by reinvesting interim cash flows at the cost of capital instead of the IRR. It also avoids the multiple-IRR problem.

|---|---|---|
| NPV | Value added in currency | NPV > 0 |
| IRR | Project's own return rate | IRR > hurdle rate |

### Deep Dive — Why NPV Wins, and Which Cash Flows Count

**NPV versus IRR: the conflict that destroys value if you get it wrong**

NPV and IRR usually agree for a single, conventional project. They can disagree sharply for mutually exclusive projects, and then NPV is right. IRR has three weaknesses: (1) **Scale blindness** — IRR is a percentage, so a tiny project with a huge return rate can out-rank a large project that creates far more absolute value. (2) **Reinvestment assumption** — IRR implicitly assumes interim cash flows are reinvested at the IRR itself, which is unrealistic when the IRR is very high; MIRR fixes this. (3) **Multiple IRRs** — when cash flows change sign more than once, there can be several IRRs or none. For all three reasons, when NPV and IRR conflict, follow NPV.

**Relevant cash flows: include the right things, ignore the rest**

Use **incremental cash flows** — only the cash flows that change because of the project. **Ignore sunk costs** (money already spent is gone and irrelevant). **Include opportunity costs** (if the project uses land the company already owns, the foregone value is a real cost). Account for changes in NWC and any terminal/salvage value at the end.

**References**

- CFI Corporate Finance Fundamentals — NPV, IRR, payback, PI and the accept/reject rules.
- The standard result that mutually exclusive projects should be ranked by NPV rather than IRR.

### Example — Evaluating a Project, and When IRR Misleads

**Single project:** Invest Rs 1,000 Cr; inflows Rs 400/450/500 Cr; hurdle 10%. NPV = Rs 111 Cr (positive). IRR ≈ 16% (> 10% hurdle). Payback ≈ 2.3 years. PI = 1.11 (> 1). **All four agree: accept.**

**Mutually exclusive choice:** Project A: Rs 100 Cr investment, returns Rs 130 Cr in one year → NPV Rs 18.2 Cr, IRR 30%. Project B: Rs 1,000 Cr investment, returns Rs 1,200 Cr in one year → NPV Rs 90.9 Cr, IRR 20%.

**Analysis:** An IRR-driven manager would pick A (30% > 20%). But B creates far more absolute value (NPV Rs 90.9 Cr vs Rs 18.2 Cr) because it deploys much more capital at a still-attractive return. Since projects are mutually exclusive, choose B. This is IRR's scale blindness in action.

**Analyst takeaway:** Run all the metrics, but let NPV decide. When IRR and NPV disagree on mutually exclusive projects, the higher NPV wins.

### Common Mistakes — The Capital Budgeting Errors the Exam Baits

1. **Choosing the higher-IRR project over the higher-NPV project when mutually exclusive.** *Fix:* Rank by NPV. IRR is scale-blind.
2. **Including sunk costs in the analysis.** *Fix:* Exclude sunk costs entirely. Money already spent is irrelevant.
3. **Ignoring opportunity costs.** *Fix:* Include the value foregone by using an owned resource for this project rather than its next best use.
4. **Relying on payback period alone.** *Fix:* Use NPV as the arbiter. Payback ignores the time value of money and all cash flows after the payback point.

### Summary — Session 9 — What to Lock In

- Capital budgeting evaluates long-term investments with NPV (accept if > 0), IRR (accept if > the hurdle rate), payback (accept if < target) and PI (accept if > 1); the hurdle rate is WACC.
- NPV is the theoretically superior rule because it measures absolute value created; for mutually exclusive projects, rank by NPV, not IRR.
- IRR has three weaknesses: scale blindness, unrealistic reinvestment-at-IRR assumption (fixed by MIRR), and possible multiple IRRs with non-conventional cash flows.
- Use incremental cash flows: exclude sunk costs (already spent, irrelevant) and include opportunity costs (value foregone from owned resources).
- Payback is a useful liquidity/risk lens but a poor sole criterion; let NPV decide.

## Session 10 · Sensitivity, Scenarios, Dashboards & Presentations

*Turning models into decisions and decisions into communication: data tables and scenario manager, sensitivity analysis, dashboards, and CFI's presentation principles.*

**By the end of this session you will be able to:**

- Run one- and two-variable sensitivity analyses in Excel
- Build and switch scenarios with the scenario manager
- Design a clean analyst dashboard
- Apply CFI presentation and data-visualisation principles

### Hook — One Number Is Never the Whole Answer

A model produces a single number — a share price, an NPV, a return. But that number is built on assumptions about an uncertain future. The best analysts don't hand decision-makers one answer; they show the range of outcomes and what drives it, then communicate it so clearly that the decision becomes obvious.

### Concept — Stress-Test It, Then Communicate It

**Sensitivity analysis** changes one input at a time to see how much the output moves — isolating the effect of each driver. **Scenario analysis** changes a coherent set of inputs together to describe distinct futures — a base, a bull and a bear case, each with its own internally consistent combination of assumptions. The distinction: sensitivity moves one variable; scenarios move several at once. Excel provides purpose-built what-if tools — data tables, Goal Seek and Scenario Manager. Once you have the range, the second half of the job is communication: dashboards, the right chart type, and always delivering the 'so what.'

> **Analogy.** Sensitivity analysis is asking 'if the temperature alone drops 5 degrees, how does comfort change?' — one variable. Scenario analysis is painting three whole days: a sunny best case, a typical base case, and a stormy worst case.

**Key terms**

- **Sensitivity analysis:** Varying a single input to measure how much the output changes, isolating each driver's impact.
- **Scenario analysis:** Changing a coherent set of inputs together to model distinct futures (base / bull / bear).
- **What-if tools:** Excel features — data tables, Goal Seek, Scenario Manager.
- **The 'so what':** The insight or recommendation a presentation must deliver, translating analysis into a decision.

### Formulas — The Excel What-If Toolkit

**One-Variable Data Table**

`Data > What-If Analysis > Data Table` — vary ONE input, read one or more outputs.

> Example: Input values 10%, 11%, 12% for WACC → outputs Rs 30.0, Rs 25.4, Rs 21.9 per share.

**Two-Variable Data Table**

`Data > What-If Analysis > Data Table` — vary TWO inputs, read ONE output.

> Example: Rows = WACC (10/11/12%), columns = growth (2/3/4%); each cell shows the resulting value per share.

> Note: Only one output can sit in a two-variable table, placed in the corner.

**Goal Seek**

`Data > What-If Analysis > Goal Seek` — Set cell = target, By changing cell.

> Example: Set NPV cell to 0 by changing the WACC cell → Excel returns the breakeven discount rate (the IRR).

> Note: Goal Seek changes exactly one input; for multiple inputs use Solver instead.

**Scenario Manager**

`Data > What-If Analysis > Scenario Manager` — define named scenarios of input sets.

> Example: Define Bear (WACC 12%, g 2%), Base (11%, 3%), Bull (10%, 4%) → Rs 19.4 / Rs 25.4 / Rs 35.6 per share.

> Note: Scenario Manager is the tool of choice when several inputs change together (true scenario analysis).

**Tornado chart:** Horizontal bars sorted longest-to-shortest showing how far the output swings when each input moves across its range. Terminal growth and WACC are the longest bars (most impactful).

**Analyst dashboard:** Clean one-screen layout with a few KPI tiles (valuation, upside %, IRR), one trend line chart, one bar chart. *'Key outputs only — built for a decision, not a data dump.'*

### Deep Dive — Tools, Charts, and Delivering the Insight

**Choosing the right what-if tool**

Match the tool to the question. One-variable data table: how outputs respond to a single input across a range. Two-variable data table: the classic valuation grid where one output responds to two inputs (remembering only one output is allowed). Goal Seek: you know the answer you want and need the single input that delivers it (the breakeven WACC that zeroes NPV is literally the IRR). Scenario Manager: several inputs must move together as a coherent story. A tornado chart ranks the inputs by how much each swings the output.

**Data visualisation: the right chart for the job**

- **Column or bar chart:** Compare values across categories
- **Line chart:** Show a trend over time
- **Waterfall (bridge) chart:** Show how a starting value reaches an ending value through sequential increases and decreases (revenue bridges, NPV builds)
- **Scatter plot:** Show the relationship between two variables
- **Pie/doughnut:** Use sparingly, only for a few parts of a whole

Design rules: remove clutter, label directly, use colour to highlight not decorate, make the single most important comparison the most visually prominent thing.

**Presentations: lead with the 'so what'**

Structure for a busy reader: executive summary up front with the recommendation, then supporting analysis, then detail in an appendix. Every slide should make one clear point, ideally stated in the title as a takeaway ('Valuation supports a buy at the current price') rather than a label ('Valuation'). The cardinal sin: presenting data without the 'so what' — pages of tables and charts with no clear conclusion.

**References**

- CFI Sensitivity Analysis, Dashboards, Data Visualisation and Presentations courses.
- Excel What-If Analysis tools: Data Table (one- and two-variable), Goal Seek and Scenario Manager.

### Example — Stress-Testing the DCF and Presenting the Range

**Data:** Base case value = Rs 25.4 per share. Sensitivity grid (see Visual above) shows range Rs 19.4 to Rs 35.6 — a spread of more than 80% from bottom to top. Scenarios via Scenario Manager: Bear Rs 19.4, Base Rs 25.4, Bull Rs 35.6. Tornado chart: terminal growth and WACC are the dominant drivers.

**Analysis:** The single base number is far less informative than the range. The grid and tornado make the source of uncertainty explicit — the valuation is dominated by terminal assumptions, so those are exactly where due diligence should focus. Presented well: executive-summary slide titled with the recommendation, scenario range as the headline chart, tornado showing which assumptions to challenge.

**Analyst takeaway:** Never present a point estimate alone. Show the range, rank the drivers with a tornado, visualise cleanly, and lead with the 'so what.'

### Common Mistakes — The Errors That Weaken Analysis and Its Delivery

1. **Confusing sensitivity analysis with scenario analysis.** *Fix:* Sensitivity varies ONE input at a time; scenario analysis changes a coherent SET of inputs together.
2. **Trying to read two outputs from a two-variable data table.** *Fix:* A two-variable data table supports exactly one output. Build separate tables for multiple outputs.
3. **Choosing the wrong chart type for the message.** *Fix:* Column/bar for comparison, line for trend, waterfall for bridges, scatter for relationships.
4. **Presenting data without the 'so what'.** *Fix:* Lead with the recommendation, title each slide with its takeaway, and state the insight explicitly.

### Summary — Session 10 — What to Lock In

- Sensitivity analysis varies ONE input to isolate its effect; scenario analysis changes a coherent SET of inputs together (base / bull / bear).
- Match the Excel what-if tool: one-variable data table (one input), two-variable data table (two inputs, ONE output), Goal Seek (solve one input for a target output), Scenario Manager (switch named input sets).
- A tornado chart ranks which inputs move the output most, focusing due diligence on assumptions that matter.
- Chart choice by message: column/bar for comparison, line for trend, waterfall for bridges, scatter for relationships.
- Always deliver the 'so what': lead with the recommendation, title slides with takeaways, present a range rather than a lone point estimate.

## Session 11 · Full Mock Exam Drill — 50-Question Simulation with Excel Cases

### Hook — The Dress Rehearsal That Predicts Your Result

Everything in this module has built to this: a full-length simulation of the FMVA final. Treat it as the real thing. The point isn't just the score; it's the diagnosis. A mock surfaces exactly which topics are still shaky and how your time management holds up under pressure, while there is still time to fix both. Candidates who sit two or three honest mocks before the real exam pass at far higher rates than those who only read.

### Concept — How to Sit This Mock

Recreate exam conditions precisely. Set a hard three-hour timer for all 50 questions and don't pause it. Keep your reference sheet to the side but treat the mock as effectively closed-book. Apply the triage strategy from Session 1: a first pass answering every quick conceptual and calculation question, flagging the heavier multi-step items, then a second pass to clear the flagged ones with your remaining time. ### Concept — How to Score and Diagnose

Score against the real threshold: 35 of 50 correct is 70% — the pass mark. Tag every missed question by topic (accounting, ratios, 3-statement modeling, DCF, comps, Excel, FP&A, capital budgeting, presentations). Patterns will emerge — those clusters are your study plan. - **Timed conditions:** A hard three-hour clock for all 50 questions, run without pausing.
- **Triage pass:** Answer quick questions first, flag multi-step ones, return in a second pass.
- **70% threshold:** 35 of 50 correct — the pass mark on the real final.
- **Error log:** A running note of the precise mistake behind each miss, used to target review.

### Deep Dive — Running the Simulation and Using the Result

**Recreating exam-day conditions**

Sit it in one uninterrupted three-hour block, at a desk, with only the reference sheet you intend to use on the day. Don't look up answers as you go, don't take breaks that stop the clock, and don't let a hard question break your pacing. If you routinely run out of time on the mock, that is the single most important thing to fix before the real exam. Concentration in hour three is a skill you train, not a given.

**Turning misses into a targeted plan**

Cluster wrong answers by topic and rank the clusters by size — the biggest cluster is where the most marks are hiding. A sensible rhythm: sit this mock, spend a focused block fixing the weak clusters it reveals, then re-sit to confirm the lift before the real exam. Aim to be clearing the mock comfortably above 70% — with margin, not by a single question — before you book the final. ### Formulas — The Complete Formula Vault

**1. Accounting Identities**

```
Assets = Liabilities + Equity
Ending RE = Beginning RE + Net Income - Dividends
NWC = Current Assets - Current Liabilities
```

> Note: An increase in NWC consumes cash.

**2. Profitability Ratios**

```
Gross Margin    = (Rev - COGS) / Rev
Operating Margin = EBIT / Rev
Net Margin      = NI / Rev
ROE             = NI / Equity
ROA             = NI / Assets
ROIC            = NOPAT / Invested Capital    (NOPAT = EBIT × (1 - tax))
```

> Note: ROIC above WACC = value creation. Divide margins by Revenue, never by cost.

**3. Liquidity & Leverage Ratios**

```
Current Ratio    = CA / CL
Quick Ratio      = (CA - Inventory) / CL
Debt-to-Equity   = Total Debt / Equity
Interest Coverage = EBIT / Interest Expense
```

> Note: Interest coverage uses EBIT (pre-interest), never net income.

**4. Efficiency Ratios & DuPont**

```
Inventory Turnover   = COGS / Avg Inventory
Receivables Turnover = Rev / Avg Receivables
DSO                  = 365 / Receivables Turnover
DuPont ROE           = Net Margin × Asset Turnover × Equity Multiplier
Equity Multiplier    = Total Assets / Equity
```

> Note: High ROE driven by a high equity multiplier is lower quality. Inventory turnover uses COGS; receivables turnover uses Revenue.

**5. 3-Statement Modeling Mechanics**

```
Balance Check  = Assets - Liabilities - Equity = 0
Ending PP&E    = Beginning PP&E + CapEx - Depreciation
Interest       = Rate × Average Debt
```

> Note: Balance off by exactly net income → missing retained-earnings link. Fix circularity with iterative calculation.

**6. DCF Core**

```
FCFF = EBIT × (1 - tax) + D&A - CapEx - increase in NWC
CAPM: Re = Rf + Beta × (Rm - Rf)
WACC = (E/V)Re + (D/V)Rd(1 - tax)
```

> Note: Match the pair: FCFF → WACC → enterprise value. Never discount FCFF at the cost of equity.

**7. Terminal Value & Equity Bridge**

```
Gordon TV      = FCF_n × (1 + g) / (WACC - g)    [g MUST be < WACC]
Exit TV        = EBITDA_n × multiple
Equity Value   = Enterprise Value - Net Debt
Value per Share = Equity Value / Shares
```

> Note: The terminal value MUST be discounted back to today. g ≥ WACC breaks the formula.

**8. Relative Valuation**

```
Implied Value     = Target Metric × Peer Multiple (median)
Enterprise Value  = Market Cap + Total Debt - Cash
Control Premium   = (Offer - Unaffected) / Unaffected
```

> Note: EV multiples pair with pre-interest metrics; P/E with net income. EV/Net income is invalid.

**9. Capital Budgeting**

```
NPV = Σ[CF_t/(1+r)^t] - Investment    (accept if > 0)
IRR: rate where NPV = 0                (accept if > hurdle)
PI  = PV of inflows / Investment       (accept if > 1)
```

> Note: For mutually exclusive projects, rank by NPV, not IRR. Exclude sunk costs; include opportunity costs.

**10. FP&A & Forecasting**

```
Straight-line Forecast = Prior × (1 + g)
CAGR                   = (Ending/Beginning)^(1/n) - 1
Variance               = Actual - Budget
Price Variance         = (Actual Price - Budget Price) × Actual Units
Volume Variance        = (Actual Units - Budget Units) × Budget Price
```

> Note: Watch the variance sign — a cost overrun is unfavourable even though the number is positive.

**11. Excel Financial Rules**

```excel
Time-0 cash flow goes OUTSIDE NPV:
=CF0 + NPV(rate, future_flows)

Use XNPV/XIRR for dated cash flows.
Lock inputs with $ (F4).
```

> Note: Prefer INDEX-MATCH or XLOOKUP over VLOOKUP; force exact matches.

**Q1. The correct discount rate for FCFF is: [medium]**
- A. Cost of equity

**Q2. A question provides EV inputs and asks for equity value per share. After computing enterprise value, the final two steps are to: [medium]**
- A. Discount and sum the cash flows
- B. Subtract net debt and divide by shares outstanding
- C. Add back depreciation and tax
- D. Multiply by the P/E ratio

> Answer: B. Equity bridge: equity value = enterprise value - net debt; value per share = equity value / shares.

**Q3. Which of the following statements is TRUE? [hard]**
- A. In the Gordon model, g may exceed WACC
- B. Excel's NPV assumes its first cash flow occurs one period from now
- C. Interest coverage is calculated using net income
- D. EV/Net income is a valid multiple

> Answer: B. Excel's NPV discounts the first argument by one period. g must be below WACC; interest coverage uses EBIT; EV/Net income is a mismatched multiple.

**Q4. For two mutually exclusive projects, the choice should be made by ranking on: [medium]**
- A. IRR
- B. NPV
- C. Payback period
- D. Profitability index

> Answer: B. Mutually exclusive projects are ranked by NPV, which measures absolute value created.

**Q5. When deriving operating cash flow from net income, depreciation is: [easy]**
- A. Subtracted
- B. Added back
- C. Ignored
- D. Doubled

> Answer: B. Depreciation is a non-cash expense already deducted to reach net income, so it is added back in operating cash flow.

**Q6. A three-statement model's balance sheet is out of balance by exactly the period's net income. The fix is to: [hard]**
- A. Link the cash line
- B. Link net income into retained earnings
- C. Add capital expenditure
- D. Remove depreciation

> Answer: B. An imbalance equal to net income points to the missing retained-earnings link.

**Q7. What is the minimum score required to pass the FMVA final exam? [easy]**

**Q8. An increase in accounts receivable during the period affects operating cash flow by: [medium]**
- A. Increasing it
- B. Decreasing it
- C. Having no effect
- D. Increasing financing cash flow

> Answer: B. An increase in a current asset such as receivables is a use of cash, so it reduces operating cash flow.

**Q9. EV/EBITDA is preferred over P/E for comparing companies primarily because it is: [medium]**
- A. Always a higher number
- B. Neutral to capital structure and depreciation policy

> Answer: B. EV/EBITDA is neutral to capital structure (EBITDA is pre-interest, EV includes debt) and to depreciation policy.
