## Session 1 — The Research Analyst Profession

*Exam weight: 1 mark*


### Hook — Two Analysts, Same Stock, Opposite Jobs

It is 8:15 AM. At a Mumbai brokerage, Aditi finishes a 28-page report on Infosys: 'Initiating
Coverage — BUY, 12-month target price attached.' By 8:30 it lands in the inbox of every
institutional and retail client the firm has. Two kilometres away, Rohan at a large mutual fund
writes a 3-page note on the very same Infosys results. Only one person will ever read it: his
fund manager. Same company. Same financials. Same quarter. Yet Aditi and Rohan have
almost nothing in common — different audiences, different paymasters, and crucially,
different rules from SEBI. Chapter 1 of the exam is worth just 1 mark, but it hands you the
map of this entire ecosystem. Every later chapter quietly assumes you already know which
seat each analyst is sitting in. Miss that, and 'classify this analyst' questions across the paper
become guesswork.


> *Hook question. If you analyse stocks all day purely for your own employer's fund and never*

publish a single word to the public, are you even a 'research analyst' as SEBI defines one?


### Concept — What a Research Analyst Actually Does

A research analyst studies companies, industries, and the economy, then produces a
research report that ends in a clear recommendation — typically Buy, Sell, or Hold — usually
with a target price and the reasoning behind it. The job has two raw materials. Primary
research is information you generate yourself: management meetings, plant visits, channel
checks with dealers, expert and customer surveys. Secondary research is information that
already exists in published form: annual reports, stock-exchange filings, regulatory
disclosures, paid databases, and credible news. A good analyst blends both — primary
research gives an edge, secondary research gives the foundation — and converts them into a
defensible view a client or fund can act on.


> **Analogy.** Think of an analyst as a doctor reaching a diagnosis. Primary research is examining

the patient yourself — checking the pulse, asking direct questions (the company visit, the
management call). Secondary research is reading the patient's existing medical file — past
prescriptions and test reports (the annual report, the filings). You would not diagnose on the
file alone, nor on a single check-up alone. The recommendation is the diagnosis; the report is
the prescription.

**Key terms**

- Research Analyst — A person who analyses securities, issuers, sectors, or the economy
and prepares research reports with recommendations.
- Research Report — A written analysis of a security or sector that contains a
recommendation and the rationale supporting it.
- Primary Research — Original information gathered first-hand by the analyst, e.g.,
management meetings, plant visits, channel checks, surveys.
- Secondary Research — Information drawn from already-published sources such as annual
reports, filings, databases, and news.
- Recommendation — The analyst's actionable opinion on a security — commonly Buy, Sell,
or Hold.
- Target Price — The price level the analyst expects a security to reach over a stated
horizon, supporting the recommendation.


### Concept — Buy-Side vs Sell-Side vs Independent — The Three Seats


Almost every Chapter 1 question rests on one taxonomy. Sell-side analysts work at
brokerages and investment banks; their research is distributed to the firm's clients and
ultimately supports the broking and banking business. Buy-side analysts work at institutions
that actually invest money — mutual funds, AIFs, portfolio managers, insurers, pension funds;
their research is for the firm's own investment decisions and is generally not published
outside. Independent research analysts are registered with SEBI in their own right and sell
research to subscribers or clients without being employed by a brokerage or a fund. The
simplest test to place anyone: ask (1) who employs them, and (2) who reads their research.


> **Analogy.** Picture three people who all taste food for a living. The sell-side analyst is the

restaurant critic whose reviews are published for everyone to read — the newspaper (the
brokerage) earns from circulation and ads (commissions). The buy-side analyst is the private
chef who tastes dishes only for the one household (the fund) that employs them — nobody
outside ever sees the verdict. The independent analyst is the consultant you personally hire
and pay for their tasting notes. Same skill, three very different employers and audiences.

**Key terms**

- Sell-Side Analyst — Works at a brokerage/investment bank; produces research distributed
to clients, supporting the firm's broking business.
- Buy-Side Analyst — Works at an investing institution (mutual fund, AIF, PMS, insurer);
produces in-house research for the firm's own portfolio, not published externally.
- Independent Research Analyst — A SEBI-registered analyst who provides research to
subscribers/clients independently of any brokerage or fund.
- Brokerage — A firm that executes trades for clients and earns commission; the typical
home of sell-side research.
- Asset Management Company (AMC) — A firm that manages pooled investment funds
such as mutual funds; a typical home of buy-side research.
- Conflict of Interest — A situation where an analyst's incentives could compromise the
objectivity of a recommendation; a recurring exam and regulatory theme.

### Deep dive — Inside the Job: Report Types, Skills & the Career Ladder


The Reports You Will Produce
Initiating coverage report — the first, most detailed report when an analyst begins covering a
stock, with the thesis, model, and target. Result/company update — a shorter note reacting
to quarterly earnings or company news. Sector/industry report — a thematic view across an
entire industry. Thematic report — built around a trend (e.g., electrification, formalisation).
Event update — a quick reaction to a budget, policy, or corporate action. Economy report —
top-down macro views. Model portfolios — a recommended basket of stocks with weights.

The Skills That Matter
Accounting and financial-statement fluency, financial modelling, valuation, deep sector
knowledge, and the ability to write tight, persuasive reports. Above all: objectivity and ethics
— the regulator judges an analyst on independence and disclosure, not on whether every call
was right.

The Career Ladder
A common path runs research associate, then analyst, then senior analyst, then head of
research. Many analysts move laterally to the buy-side or into fund management, where
strong research skills translate directly into managing money.

Why Incentives Differ
Sell-side research is distributed to clients and supports broking relationships, so it can
structurally tilt toward more 'Buy' coverage and maintaining access to management.
Buy-side research is internal and judged purely on the returns it helps the fund earn, so it is
often more sceptical and unpublished. The exam and interviewers both probe this difference.


### Real-world references

- Sell-side desks such as Motilal Oswal and Kotak Institutional Equities publish research
consumed by their clients.
- Buy-side teams at AMCs like SBI Mutual Fund and HDFC AMC run in-house research for their
own schemes.
- SEBI-registered independent research analysts provide research directly to paying
subscribers under the RA Regulations.


### Real-world example — Same Stock, Two Seats: Infosys Through Two

Analysts' Eyes
Context. Infosys (IT services, Nifty 50)

Situation. After Infosys announces Q4 results, two analysts cover it: Aditi on the sell-side at
a brokerage, and Rohan on the buy-side at a mutual fund.

Data. Aditi publishes a detailed initiating-coverage report — a Buy rating with a 12-month
price target — distributed to all the brokerage's institutional and retail clients. Rohan writes a
concise internal note recommending that the fund add to or trim its existing Infosys position;
it is seen only by his fund manager and the investment committee and is never released
publicly.

Analysis. Identical financials produce two completely different deliverables. Aditi's report is
simultaneously research and a business driver — it supports the brokerage's relationship with
clients and management. Rohan's note is pure decision support, judged only by whether it
improves the fund's returns. Note the regulatory edge too: it is the analyst who issues
research to the public (sell-side and independent) who is squarely within the registration and
disclosure machinery of the RA Regulations, not the purely in-house buy-side analyst.


### Analyst takeaway. — When a question asks you to classify an analyst, anchor on two facts

only — who employs them and who reads their research. That pair resolves the
overwhelming majority of Chapter 1 MCQs.


### Common mistakes — The 5 Chapter-1 Traps That Cost Easy Marks

1. Mistake: Reading 'sell-side' as 'the people who sell stocks to me'.
Why it happens: The label sounds like a sales role.

Fix: Sell-side means the firm SELLS research and ideas to clients through a brokerage. The buy-side are
the BUYERS of securities — the funds — who research in-house.

2. Mistake: Assuming every research analyst must be registered with SEBI.
Why it happens: Students over-generalise the certification rule.

Fix: The RA framework centres on those issuing research reports/recommendations to the public. A
purely in-house buy-side analyst sits differently — know that the registration trigger is public-facing
research.

3. Mistake: Swapping primary and secondary research.
Why it happens: The words feel interchangeable.

Fix: Primary = data YOU create first-hand (visits, surveys, channel checks). Secondary = data that
already EXISTS in published form (annual reports, filings, databases).

4. Mistake: Treating a Buy/Sell/Hold call as a promise of returns.
Why it happens: Beginners equate a recommendation with a guarantee.

Fix: A recommendation is a reasoned opinion with a target and rationale. The analyst's duty is
objectivity and disclosure, not a guarantee of being right.

5. Mistake: Believing a specific finance/commerce degree is compulsory to be an RA.
Why it happens: Old eligibility norms are widely quoted online.

Fix: The 2025 SEBI amendments relaxed the strict specific-degree requirement; the NISM Series XV
certification is now the central knowledge benchmark (revisited in Chapter 14).

## Session 2 — Introduction to the Securities Market —

Primary, Secondary, Participants
*Exam weight: 2 marks*


### Hook — The Machine Behind the 'Buy' Button

When you tap 'buy' on a share, a remarkable machine springs into action behind the screen:
a stock exchange matches your order, a broker routes it, a clearing corporation guarantees
the trade, and a depository moves the shares electronically into your account - all within a
day. Most investors never see these pipes, yet the securities market is simply the plumbing
that connects people's savings to the companies that need capital. This chapter is small - just
2 marks - but it names every venue and player the rest of the exam refers to: primary versus
secondary markets, SEBI, the exchanges, the depositories, and the intermediaries. Learn the
map once, and references to 'the depository' or 'the clearing corporation' stop being a
mystery.


> *Hook question. When you sell a share, the company whose name is on it receives nothing -*

so where does your money come from, and which market are you actually trading in?


### Concept — Primary vs Secondary Market

The securities market has two halves that do completely different jobs. The PRIMARY market
is where NEW securities are issued for the first time and the company RAISES fresh capital
directly from investors - through an IPO (initial public offering), an FPO (follow-on public
offering), a rights issue, or a private placement. The money flows from investors to the
company. The SECONDARY market is where EXISTING securities are traded among investors
after issue - the stock exchange you picture when you buy or sell a listed share. Here the
money flows between investors, and the company itself receives NOTHING from secondary
trading, because it already raised its capital in the primary market. The key insight: the
primary market performs capital FORMATION (funding companies), while the secondary
market provides LIQUIDITY (letting investors exit when they wish) and PRICE DISCOVERY
(continuously setting the market price). Both are essential - without a liquid secondary
market, few investors would commit money in the primary market in the first place.


> **Analogy.** Think of a car. The primary market is the SHOWROOM - you buy a brand-new car

directly from the manufacturer, and your money goes to the carmaker. The secondary market
is the USED-CAR market - you buy a pre-owned car from another owner, and your money goes
to that seller, not to the original manufacturer. The carmaker is paid only on the first sale;
every resale after that is simply between owners.

**Key terms**

- Primary Market — Where new securities are issued for the first time and the company
raises fresh capital.
- Secondary Market — Where existing securities are traded among investors; the company
receives nothing from these trades.
- Initial Public Offering (IPO) — A company's first issue of shares to the public, a
primary-market transaction.
- Capital Formation — The raising of fresh funds for companies, the core function of the
primary market.
- Liquidity — The ability to buy or sell a security easily, provided by the secondary market.
- Price Discovery — The continuous setting of a security's market price through
secondary-market trading.


### Concept — The Players: Exchanges, Depositories, Intermediaries and

Regulators
A web of participants makes the market work. The ISSUERS are the companies raising
capital. The INVESTORS are retail individuals and institutions (mutual funds, foreign portfolio
investors and domestic institutions). The STOCK EXCHANGES (NSE and BSE in India) provide
the electronic platform that matches buyers and sellers. The DEPOSITORIES (NSDL and CDSL)
hold securities in electronic, dematerialised form, accessed through Depository Participants -
dematerialisation replaced physical share certificates. CLEARING CORPORATIONS guarantee
and settle trades, removing counterparty risk. The INTERMEDIARIES include stockbrokers
(who route orders), merchant bankers and underwriters (who manage new issues), registrars
and transfer agents, and custodians. Sitting above all of them is the REGULATOR: SEBI, the
Securities and Exchange Board of India, which regulates the securities market and protects
investors. Know the other regulators for their separate turf: the RBI (banking and money
markets), IRDAI (insurance), and PFRDA (pensions). The market is also divided into
SEGMENTS - equity, debt, derivatives, currency and commodities - and India settles trades on
a T+1 cycle, one business day after the trade.


> **Analogy.** Picture an airport. The exchange is the air-traffic control matching flights (buyers

and sellers); brokers are the travel agents booking your ticket; the depository is the secure
baggage system that holds and moves your luggage (your shares) electronically; the clearing
corporation is the guarantor making sure every bag reaches the right owner; and SEBI is the
aviation regulator setting and enforcing the rules so the whole system stays safe.

**Key terms**

- Stock Exchange (NSE/BSE) — The electronic platform that matches buy and sell orders
for listed securities.
- Depository (NSDL/CDSL) — An institution that holds securities in electronic
(dematerialised) form.
- Dematerialisation — Holding securities electronically in a demat account rather than as
physical certificates.
- Clearing Corporation — The body that nets and guarantees settlement of trades,
removing counterparty risk.
- SEBI — The Securities and Exchange Board of India, the regulator of the securities market.
- Settlement Cycle (T+1) — The timeframe for settling a trade; India uses T+1, one
business day after the trade.

### Deep dive — From Raising Capital to Settling the Trade

Primary market routes
Companies raise capital through an IPO (a first public issue), an FPO (an additional issue by
an already-listed company), a rights issue (offered to existing shareholders), or a private
placement such as a QIP (to selected institutional investors). The primary market performs
capital formation, with merchant bankers and underwriters managing the process and
pricing.

The secondary market and the trade lifecycle
The exchange matches buy and sell orders; the broker is the member who routes them on an
investor's behalf. After a trade, the clearing corporation nets and guarantees the obligations,
and settlement then moves money and securities - in India on a T+1 cycle. Because the
depository holds shares electronically, settlement is essentially a book entry rather than a
physical transfer.

Dematerialisation and depositories
NSDL and CDSL hold securities in electronic form, and investors access them through a
Depository Participant such as a bank or broker. Dematerialisation replaced paper
certificates, eliminating forgery, loss and settlement delays. The standard investor setup is a
demat account (to hold shares), a trading account (to transact), and a linked bank account
(for funds).

The regulatory map
SEBI regulates the securities market - the exchanges, intermediaries, listed companies and
investor protection. The RBI oversees banking, money markets and government securities;
IRDAI oversees insurance; and PFRDA oversees pensions. Knowing which regulator owns
which domain is a recurring exam point, and a common trap is wrongly attributing
share-market regulation to the RBI.


### Real-world references

- NSE and BSE are India's two main stock exchanges, providing the electronic platforms on
which listed securities trade.
- NSDL and CDSL are the two depositories that hold Indian securities in dematerialised form.
- India moved its equity settlement to a T+1 cycle, so trades now settle one business day
after execution.


### Real-world example — One Share, Two Markets: From IPO to Resale

Context. An illustrative company, ABC Ltd, traced from issue to resale

Situation. ABC Ltd raises money through an IPO and is then traded by investors. Following
the share through both markets shows who gets paid and who does what.

Data. In the PRIMARY market, ABC issues new shares in its IPO; investors pay for them and
that money goes to ABC, funding its growth. Later, one of those investors sells the share on
the NSE - a SECONDARY-market trade. The buyer's money goes to the SELLING investor, not

to ABC. A broker routes the order, the exchange matches it, the clearing corporation
guarantees settlement, and the shares move through a depository (NSDL or CDSL) into the
buyer's demat account, settling on a T+1 basis.

Analysis. The contrast is the whole point. ABC is funded only once - at the IPO, in the
primary market. Every trade afterwards is investor-to-investor in the secondary market, and
the company receives nothing from those trades. What the secondary market provides
instead is liquidity (the first investor could exit) and price discovery (the market set the
price). Each intermediary plays a distinct role: the exchange matches, the broker routes, the
clearing corporation guarantees, and the depository holds and transfers.


### Analyst takeaway. — Knowing which market a transaction sits in - and which intermediary

does what - is the foundation for everything else. The company benefits from the primary
issue; the secondary market's job is liquidity and price discovery.


### Common mistakes — The Securities-Market Traps That Lose Marks

1. Mistake: Thinking a company earns money when its shares are traded on the exchange.
Why it happens: The company's name is on the share being traded.

Fix: A company raises capital only in the PRIMARY market (IPO, FPO, rights). Secondary-market trades
are between investors; the company receives nothing.

2. Mistake: Confusing the primary and secondary markets.
Why it happens: Both involve buying securities.

Fix: Primary means NEW securities are issued and capital goes to the company; secondary means
EXISTING securities are traded among investors.

3. Mistake: Confusing the depository with the exchange.
Why it happens: Both are core market institutions.

Fix: The exchange (NSE/BSE) is the trading PLATFORM that matches orders; the depository
(NSDL/CDSL) HOLDS the shares electronically. Different roles entirely.

4. Mistake: Mixing up the regulators.
Why it happens: There are several, with similar-sounding mandates.

Fix: SEBI regulates the securities market; the RBI handles banking and money markets; IRDAI handles

## Session 3 — Terminology in Equity and Debt Markets —

Speaking the Language
*Exam weight: 2 marks*


### Hook — Speak the Language, or Bleed Marks Everywhere

Imagine reading an exam line that says 'a bond's coupon is 8% but it trades at a yield of 9%'
- and not knowing that coupon and yield are two different things. You would lose that mark,
and worse, you would misread similar phrases throughout the paper. Chapter 3 is small - just
2 marks on its own - but it teaches the VOCABULARY that every other chapter silently
assumes. Face value versus market value, market cap, free float, coupon versus yield, credit
ratings: get these definitions cold and the rest of the exam reads clearly; confuse them and
you quietly bleed marks everywhere. This is the language session - short, definitional, and
pure scoring territory.


> *Hook question. A bond has a coupon of 8% but trades at a yield of 9% - what is the*

difference between those two numbers, and what does it tell you about the bond's price?


### Concept — The Language of Shares and Bonds

Two markets carry two vocabularies. In EQUITY (shares): the face value (or par value) is the
nominal value of a share, set at issue and largely an accounting figure. Book value per share
is the company's net worth divided by its shares - the accounting value. Market value (price)
is what the share trades at right now. Market capitalisation is the total market value of the
company - market price times shares outstanding - and it is how stocks are classified as
large-, mid- or small-cap. Free float is the portion of shares actually available for public
trading, excluding promoter and locked-in holdings. In DEBT (bonds): a bond is a loan to the
issuer. Its face value (par) is the amount repaid at maturity. The coupon rate is the fixed
interest rate paid on the face value, so an 8% coupon on a Rs 1,000 bond pays Rs 80 a year.
Maturity is when the principal is repaid. The yield is the actual return an investor earns given
the price they paid - and crucially, bond prices and yields move INVERSELY: when the price
rises, the yield falls, and vice versa. Credit ratings (AAA, AA and so on) signal default risk. The
single most-confused pair on the exam is coupon (fixed, on face value) versus yield (varies
with price).


> **Analogy.** Face value versus market value is like the number printed on a concert ticket versus

what it actually sells for on resale - the printed price rarely equals the market price. For bonds,
the coupon is a FIXED rent the issuer promised to pay; the yield is the actual rental RETURN
you earn based on the price you paid for the property. Pay less for the same rent and your
yield goes up - which is exactly why bond prices and yields move in opposite directions.

**Key terms**

- Face Value (Par Value) — The nominal value of a share or bond, set at issue; for a bond it
is the amount repaid at maturity.
- Market Capitalisation — Total market value of a company = market price x shares
outstanding; the basis for large/mid/small-cap classification.
- Free Float — The shares available for public trading, excluding promoter and locked-in
holdings.
- Coupon Rate — The fixed rate of interest a bond pays on its face value.
- Yield — The actual return earned on a bond given its price; it moves inversely to the price.
- Credit Rating — A grade (AAA down to
- D) signalling a bond issuer's default risk.


### Formula — The Terminology Formula Bank (With Worked Numbers)


These short formulas turn definitional questions into quick calculations. The two worth
special attention are market capitalisation (the true measure of company size) and current
yield (which reveals the bond price-yield link). Notice how the current-yield example shows a
discount bond yielding more than its coupon.

> Shared example. Threaded figures: a share trades at Rs 500 with 20 crore shares, Rs 3,000
crore of equity, Rs 600 crore of net profit and a Rs 15 dividend; and a bond has a Rs 1,000 face
value with an 8% coupon, currently trading at Rs 950.
Market Capitalisation
> Formula: Market Cap = Market Price x Shares Outstanding
> Example: Rs 500 x 20 crore shares = Rs 10,000 crore.
> Meaning: The size of a company; the basis for large-, mid- and small-cap classification - not the
per-share price.

Book Value per Share
> Formula: Book Value per Share = Shareholders' Equity / Shares Outstanding
> Example: Rs 3,000 crore equity / 20 crore shares = Rs 150.
> Meaning: The accounting net worth per share; differs from the market price, and the ratio
between them is P/B.

Dividend Yield
> Formula: Dividend Yield = (Dividend per Share / Market Price) x 100
> Example: Rs 15 / Rs 500 = 3%.
> Meaning: The cash return from dividends alone, relative to the current price.

Earnings Per Share (EPS)
> Formula: EPS = Net Profit / Shares Outstanding
> Example: Rs 600 crore / 20 crore shares = Rs 30.
> Meaning: Profit attributable to each share; the denominator in the P/E ratio.

Coupon Payment
> Formula: Coupon Payment = Coupon Rate x Face Value
> Example: 8% x Rs 1,000 = Rs 80 per year.
> Meaning: The FIXED annual interest a bond pays, regardless of its market price.

Current Yield
> Formula: Current Yield = (Annual Coupon / Current Market Price) x 100
> Example: Rs 80 coupon / Rs 950 price = 8.42%.
> Meaning: The return based on the price paid; when the price is below face value, current yield
exceeds the coupon - a discount bond.

### Deep dive — Equity Terms, Debt Terms and the Price-Yield Link

Equity terms decoded
Face value is the nominal figure set at issue; book value is the accounting net worth per
share; market value is the live traded price; market cap is price times shares (the true size
measure for large/mid/small-cap); and free float is the tradable portion excluding promoters.
The distinctions matter: a high per-share PRICE does not mean a big company - market cap
does - and book value is rarely equal to market value, the gap being what the P/B ratio
captures.

Debt terms decoded
The face (par) value is repaid at maturity; the coupon is the fixed interest on that face value;
maturity is the repayment date. The crucial measures are the YIELDS: current yield is annual
coupon divided by price, while yield to maturity (YTM) is the total return if the bond is held to
maturity, including any gain or loss to par. The coupon is fixed; the yield moves with the
price.

The price-yield inverse relationship
This is the most important bond concept. When market interest rates rise, existing bonds
with their fixed coupons become less attractive, so their PRICES fall, which RAISES their
yields to match; falling rates do the reverse. Hence price up means yield down. A bond priced
ABOVE face value is a premium bond (yield below coupon); AT face value it is a par bond
(yield equals coupon); BELOW face value it is a discount bond (yield above coupon).

Credit ratings and what they signal
Ratings run from AAA (highest safety) down through AA, A and BBB to D (default), gauging an
issuer's default risk. Higher-rated bonds are safer and pay lower yields; lower-rated
'high-yield' bonds pay more to compensate for the added risk. In India, agencies such as
CRISIL, ICRA and CARE assign these ratings.


### Real-world references

- Government securities (G-Secs) serve as the benchmark for yields in the Indian debt
market.
- CRISIL, ICRA and CARE are among the credit-rating agencies that grade bonds in India.
- When the RBI changes interest rates, the price-yield inverse relationship drives the prices of
existing bonds, linking this terminology to economic analysis.


### Real-world example — Fixed Numbers vs Market Numbers: A Bond and a

Share
Context. An illustrative bond and a share, to fix the vocabulary

Situation. An investor looks at a Rs 1,000 face-value bond paying an 8% coupon (Rs 80 a
year) and a share priced at Rs 500. The aim is to see how the FIXED printed numbers relate
to the LIVE market numbers.

Data. If interest rates rise and the bond's price falls to Rs 950, its current yield rises to
80/950 = 8.42% - it is now a DISCOUNT bond yielding more than its coupon. If instead the
price rises to Rs 1,050, the current yield falls to 80/1,050 = 7.62% - a PREMIUM bond yielding
less than its coupon. The coupon stayed fixed at Rs 80 throughout; only the price, and
therefore the yield, moved. Separately, the Rs 500 share with 20 crore shares has a market
cap of 500 x 20 = Rs 10,000 crore, a mid-cap - the Rs 500 'price' looking high tells you
nothing about size.

Analysis. The bond example shows the inverse price-yield link in action: a fixed coupon plus
a falling price equals a higher yield (discount), and a rising price equals a lower yield
(premium). The share example shows that company size is market cap, not the per-share
price. In both cases the printed figures (face value, coupon) are fixed while the market
figures (price, yield, market cap) move.


### Analyst takeaway. — Never confuse the fixed, printed numbers - face value and coupon - with

the live, market-driven ones - market price and yield. The exam tests exactly this distinction,
and so does real analysis.


### Common mistakes — The Terminology Traps That Lose Marks

1. Mistake: Confusing face value with market value.
Why it happens: Both are 'values' of the same security.

Fix: Face (par) value is the nominal figure set at issue (often Rs 10 for shares, Rs 1,000 for bonds);
market value is the live trading price. They are rarely equal.

2. Mistake: Confusing coupon and yield.
Why it happens: Both are expressed as percentages.

Fix: The coupon is the FIXED interest rate on a bond's face value; the yield is the actual return based
on the PRICE paid, which varies. Coupon is fixed; yield moves with price.

3. Mistake: Thinking a high share PRICE means a big company.
Why it happens: A large per-share number looks impressive.

Fix: Company size is MARKET CAP (price x shares), not the per-share price. A Rs 5,000 share with few
shares can be smaller than a Rs 50 share with billions.

4. Mistake: Getting the bond price-yield relationship backwards.
Why it happens: It feels intuitive that price and return move together.

Fix: Bond prices and yields move INVERSELY - when the price rises, the yield falls. A bond below face
value (a discount bond) yields MORE than its coupon.

5. Mistake: Confusing book value with market value.
Why it happens: Both are per-share values.

Fix: Book value is the accounting net worth per share; market value is the traded price. The ratio
between them is P/B, and they are usually different.

## Session 4 — Fundamentals of Research — Approaches,

Directions, Sources
*Exam weight: 5 marks*


### Hook — Two Analysts, One Stock, Two Completely Different Methods

Ask two professional analysts about the same stock and you might get two entirely different
methods. One pores over the balance sheet, the industry and the wider economy to estimate
what the business is truly WORTH - she is a fundamental analyst. The other studies only the
price chart and trading volumes to judge where the stock is headed NEXT - he is a technical
analyst. Neither is wrong; they are answering different questions with different tools.
Research is not a hunch - it is a craft with a structure, and before you analyse a single
company you make deliberate choices: fundamental or technical, top-down or bottom-up,
primary or secondary sources. This chapter is the map of HOW equity research is actually
conducted, turning raw curiosity into a defensible investment opinion. On the exam it is
worth 5 marks, and the questions test whether you can tell these methods and sources apart.


> *Hook question. Two analysts study the same stock - one reads its financial statements, the*

other only its price chart. Are they both doing legitimate research, and what is each one
actually trying to answer?


### Concept — Fundamental vs Technical, Top-Down vs Bottom-Up

Two foundational distinctions define any research approach. First, the TYPE of analysis.
Fundamental analysis studies a security's intrinsic VALUE - its financials, the economy, the
industry and the company's quality - to judge whether it is cheap or expensive; it suits
longer-term investing. Technical analysis studies PRICE and VOLUME patterns on charts to
forecast future price moves; it suits shorter-term timing. They answer different questions:
fundamental asks 'what is it worth?', technical asks 'where is the price going?'. Second, the
DIRECTION of analysis. The top-down approach starts with the big picture - the ECONOMY,
then the INDUSTRY, then the COMPANY - narrowing from macro to micro (the funnel used
across this whole module). The bottom-up approach starts with the COMPANY itself, hunting
for excellent individual businesses on their own merits, with less emphasis on the macro
backdrop. Thorough fundamental research usually blends both directions, but knowing which
is which is exam-critical.


> **Analogy.** Fundamental versus technical is like two ways to size up a used car. The

fundamental analyst lifts the bonnet to check the engine, the service history and the fair
market value - the intrinsic worth. The technical analyst simply watches how the car's resale
PRICE has been trending in the classifieds and where it seems headed - the price action.
Top-down versus bottom-up is like house-hunting: top-down picks the best CITY, then the best
NEIGHBOURHOOD, then the best house; bottom-up just hunts for one brilliant house anywhere
and worries about the area afterwards.

**Key terms**

- Fundamental Analysis — Studying a security's intrinsic value via financials, economy,
industry and company quality; suited to longer-term investing.
- Technical Analysis — Studying price and volume patterns to forecast price moves; suited
to shorter-term timing.
- Intrinsic Value — What a security is genuinely worth based on its fundamentals, as
opposed to its market price.
- Top-Down Approach — Analysis that moves from the economy to the industry to the
company - macro first.

- Bottom-Up Approach — Analysis that starts with the individual company on its own
merits, with less macro emphasis.
- Investment Horizon — The time frame of a decision - longer for fundamental investing,
shorter for technical timing.


### Concept — Primary vs Secondary Research and Where Information

Comes From
Research runs on information, and information is either primary or secondary. Primary
research is original data the analyst gathers FIRST-HAND - meeting management, surveying
customers, doing channel checks with dealers, visiting plants or stores. It is harder to obtain
but offers an edge. Secondary research uses information that ALREADY EXISTS in published
form - annual reports, quarterly results, regulatory filings, databases, news and other
analysts' reports. A good analyst combines both: secondary research builds the foundation,
primary research adds the differentiated insight. The key SOURCES of information include
company sources (annual reports, investor presentations, earnings conference calls, the
prospectus or RHP for IPOs); regulatory and exchange sources (SEBI filings, stock-exchange
disclosures); macro sources (RBI bulletins, government statistics, the Economic Survey); and
third-party sources (financial databases, brokerage research, credible news, and platforms
such as screener.in for Indian companies). The analysis itself is then quantitative (the
numbers) or qualitative (management quality, the moat, governance) - and good research
needs both.


> **Analogy.** Primary versus secondary research is like writing about a chef. Secondary research

is reading every review and menu already published about the restaurant. Primary research is
actually going there, eating the food and interviewing the chef yourself. The published reviews
give you the lay of the land cheaply; your own visit gives you something nobody else has.

**Key terms**

- Primary Research — Original, first-hand information gathered by the analyst
(management meetings, surveys, channel checks, site visits).
- Secondary Research — Information from already-published sources (annual reports,
filings, databases, news, other research).
- Annual Report — A company's primary published disclosure document and a core
secondary source.
- Conference Call (Concall) — An earnings call where management discusses results and
answers analyst questions.
- Quantitative Analysis — Analysis of measurable, numerical data such as ratios and
growth rates.
- Qualitative Analysis — Analysis of non-numerical factors such as management quality,
moat and governance.

### Deep dive — Choosing the Approach and Sourcing the Information

Fundamental vs technical - when each fits
Fundamental analysis dominates longer-horizon investment research, where the goal is to
estimate intrinsic value. Technical analysis dominates short-term trading and timing, where
the goal is to read price momentum. They are not mutually exclusive: many practitioners use
fundamentals to decide WHAT to buy and technicals to time WHEN. Different horizons,
different data, different questions.

Top-down vs bottom-up in practice
The top-down approach is powerful when macro or sector trends dominate - an interest-rate
cycle, a sector boom, a policy shift. The bottom-up approach shines for finding hidden gems
regardless of the macro backdrop. Most real research blends them: a top-down read on the
attractive sectors, then bottom-up selection of the best companies within them.

Primary research - the edge
Management meetings and earnings calls, channel checks with distributors and retailers,
expert networks and site visits yield insight that is not in the filings. This is what separates
differentiated research from a mere summary of public data. Crucially, primary research
must stay within insider-trading limits - an analyst must never act on or pass along
unpublished price-sensitive information (linking to the legal chapter).

Secondary sources and where Indian analysts look
For company information: annual reports, investor presentations and concall transcripts. For
regulatory and price data: stock-exchange and SEBI filings. For the macro picture: RBI
bulletins, government statistics and the Economic Survey. For convenient aggregated data
on Indian companies: databases such as screener.in. Knowing the right source for each
question is a core practical research skill.


### Real-world references

- Quarterly earnings conference calls and investor presentations are widely used by Indian
analysts to hear management directly and probe the results.
- Stock-exchange and SEBI filings, along with databases like screener.in, are standard
secondary sources for company financials in India.
- RBI bulletins, the Economic Survey and official government statistics are the go-to sources
for up-to-date Indian macroeconomic data.


### Real-world example — One Idea, Many Methods: Researching a

Consumer Company
Context. An illustrative end-to-end research process

Situation. An analyst is researching a consumer company and deliberately combines

Data. She begins TOP-DOWN: the consumption macro looks supportive and the sector is

Analysis. No single method carries the work. The top-down view frames the opportunity, the

drives the valuation, while a light technical read informs entry timing. Each piece answers a
different question, and together they form one coherent process ending in a
recommendation.


### Analyst takeaway. — Real research is rarely one method - it is a deliberate combination of

approach (fundamental, with a top-down and bottom-up blend) and sources (a secondary
foundation plus a primary edge), all aimed at a defensible recommendation.


### Common mistakes — The Research-Process Traps That Lose Marks

1. Mistake: Treating fundamental and technical analysis as rivals where one must be 'right'.
Why it happens: They feel like opposing camps.

Fix: They answer different questions - value versus price and timing - over different horizons. Both are
legitimate and are often complementary.

2. Mistake: Confusing top-down and bottom-up.
Why it happens: Both are 'directions' of analysis.

Fix: Top-down goes economy, then industry, then company (macro first). Bottom-up starts with the
company itself (stock-specific first). The direction is the distinguishing feature.

3. Mistake: Confusing primary and secondary research.
Why it happens: The terms are easy to swap.

Fix: Primary is data YOU gather first-hand (meetings, surveys, channel checks). Secondary is data that
already EXISTS (annual reports, filings, databases).

4. Mistake: Relying on secondary data alone.
Why it happens: Published data is easy to access.

Fix: Secondary research is the foundation but offers little edge - everyone has it. Primary research
provides differentiated insight, though it must stay within insider-trading limits.

5. Mistake: Equating 'more data' with 'better research'.
Why it happens: Volume feels like rigour.

Fix: Research is about the RIGHT information and sound judgement, not sheer volume. Knowing which
source answers which question matters more than gathering everything.

### Hook — The Tide That Lifts or Sinks Every Boat

pandemic froze the entire economy. A few quarters later, a flood of cheap money and a
reopening sent even mediocre companies soaring. The lesson is permanent: the macro
economy is the TIDE beneath every single stock, and it lifts or sinks all the boats together.
This is precisely why top-down analysis starts with the economy, before any industry or
company. Read GDP, inflation, interest rates and the business cycle correctly, and you
understand the force acting on every position you hold. On the exam this chapter is worth 5
marks and blends concepts with light calculations on growth, inflation, and

> *Hook question. If two companies are equally well-run, what economy-wide force can still cut*

### Concept — The Macro Dashboard: GDP, Inflation and the Two Policies

Economic analysis reads a handful of key gauges. First, GDP - the total value of goods and
services a country produces; its growth rate is the headline measure of economic health. By
the expenditure method, GDP = Consumption + Investment + Government spending + Net
exports, written C + I + G + (X - M). Real GDP strips out inflation, while nominal GDP does
not. Second, inflation - the rate at which prices rise, measured by the CPI (retail prices) and
the WPI (wholesale prices). Moderate inflation is normal; high inflation erodes purchasing
power and pushes interest rates up. Third, monetary policy - run by the RBI, which adjusts
the policy interest rate (the repo rate) and bank reserve ratios (CRR and SLR) to manage
inflation and growth. Cutting rates (expansionary) stimulates the economy; raising them
(contractionary) cools it and fights inflation. Fourth, fiscal policy - run by the government
through spending and taxation; higher spending or lower taxes (expansionary) boost
demand, financed by the fiscal deficit. Together these gauges reveal which way the
economic tide is flowing.


> **Analogy.** Picture the economy as a giant swimming pool and companies as the swimmers.

GDP growth is the water level rising or falling. Inflation is the water temperature - pleasant in
moderation, dangerous if it spikes. Monetary policy (the RBI) is the tap controlling how fast
cheap money flows in. Fiscal policy (the government) is a second hose adding water through
spending. An analyst watches all of them to know whether the swimmers face an easy lap or a
hard struggle.

**Key terms**

- Gross Domestic Product (GDP) — The total value of goods and services produced in a
country; its growth rate gauges economic health.
- Real vs Nominal GDP — Real GDP adjusts for inflation; nominal GDP does not.
- Inflation (CPI / WPI) — The rate of price increase - CPI measures retail prices, WPI
measures wholesale prices.
- Monetary Policy — RBI management of interest rates (repo) and reserve ratios (CRR, SLR)
to steer inflation and growth.

- Fiscal Policy — Government use of spending and taxation to manage demand, financed by
the fiscal deficit.
- Repo Rate — The RBI's key policy rate at which it lends to banks; the main lever of
monetary policy.


### Formula — The Macro Formula Bank (With Worked Numbers)

Chapter 5's calculations are light but reliably tested. The two that matter most are the
real-versus-nominal adjustment (real = nominal minus inflation) and the simple growth and
inflation rate formulas. Get the base of each percentage right - growth is always measured
against the PREVIOUS period.

> Shared example. Illustrative figures: GDP moves from 140 to 150.5 lakh crore; nominal GDP
growth is 11% with inflation of 5%; the CPI rises from 150 to 159; and government expenditure
is 45 lakh crore against revenue of 35 lakh crore.
GDP - Expenditure Method
> Formula: GDP = C + I + G + (X - M)
> Example: C = 80, I = 30, G = 25, Exports = 20, Imports = 15: 80 + 30 + 25 + (20 - 15) = Rs
140 lakh crore (illustrative).
> Meaning: Adds consumption, investment, government spending and net exports to size the
whole economy.

GDP Growth Rate
> Formula: GDP Growth = (GDP_current - GDP_previous) / GDP_previous x 100
> Example: GDP rises from 140 to 150.5 lakh crore: (150.5 - 140) / 140 = 10.5 / 140 = 7.5%.
> Meaning: The headline rate of economic expansion over a period.

Real GDP Growth (approximation)
> Formula: Real GDP Growth = Nominal GDP Growth - Inflation
> Example: Nominal growth 11%, inflation 5%: 11 - 5 = 6%.
> Meaning: Strips inflation out of nominal growth to show the true rise in output.

Inflation Rate
> Formula: Inflation Rate = (CPI_current - CPI_previous) / CPI_previous x 100
> Example: CPI rises from 150 to 159: (159 - 150) / 150 = 9 / 150 = 6%.
> Meaning: The percentage rise in the price index over the period.

Real Rate of Return (Fisher approximation)
> Formula: Real Return = Nominal Return - Inflation (exact: (1 + nominal)/(1 + inflation) - 1)
> Example: Nominal return 12%, inflation 6%: 12 - 6 = about 6% (exact: 1.12/1.06 - 1 = 5.66%).
> Meaning: What an investor truly earns after inflation; the approximation is fine for the exam.

Fiscal Deficit
> Formula: Fiscal Deficit = Total Expenditure - Total Revenue (excluding borrowings)
> Example: Expenditure 45, revenue 35 lakh crore: 45 - 35 = Rs 10 lakh crore. As a share of a
150 lakh crore GDP: 10/150 = 6.7%.
> Meaning: The gap the government must borrow to fill; often expressed as a percentage of
GDP.

### Deep dive — Reading the Cycle, the Policies and the Indicators

The business cycle and sector rotation
The economy moves through expansion, a peak, contraction or recession, a trough, and
recovery. Early in a recovery, cyclical sectors - autos, metals, banks - tend to lead as demand
and credit revive. In a contraction, defensive sectors - FMCG, pharma, utilities - hold up
because people keep buying essentials. This connects directly to industry analysis: analysts
rotate toward cyclicals early in an upturn and toward defensives when a slowdown looms.

Monetary policy decoded
The RBI's tools are the repo rate (its key policy rate - cutting it lowers borrowing costs and
stimulates the economy), the cash reserve ratio (CRR) and the statutory liquidity ratio (SLR),
which set how much banks must hold back rather than lend. The RBI targets inflation around
4% within a tolerance band. Lower rates generally lift equity valuations - cheaper discounting
and more liquidity - while higher rates cool both the economy and markets.

Fiscal policy and the deficit
The government manages demand through spending and taxation. A larger fiscal deficit -
spending beyond revenue - can stimulate growth but risks higher inflation and borrowing
costs, and can crowd out private investment. The Union Budget is the marquee fiscal event
each year, watched closely for its stance and its deficit target.

Leading, lagging and coincident indicators
Leading indicators move BEFORE the economy and help forecast it - the stock market, the
PMI, and new orders. Coincident indicators move WITH it - GDP and industrial production (IIP).
Lagging indicators move AFTER and confirm a turn already underway - unemployment and
inflation. For forecasting, an analyst leans most on the leading indicators.


### Real-world references

- The RBI operates a formal inflation-targeting framework centred on roughly 4% CPI inflation
within a tolerance band, with the repo rate as its primary signal.

- The Union Budget each year sets the government's fiscal stance and deficit target, which
markets scrutinise closely.
- GDP, CPI inflation, the IIP and the PMI are among the headline data releases that Indian
markets react to.


### Real-world example — Following the Macro Chain: From Inflation to

Your Portfolio
Context. An illustrative top-down macro read

Situation. An analyst notices CPI inflation climbing above 6% and rising further. Rather than
picking a stock, the analyst traces what this means for the whole market.

Data. With inflation running hot, the RBI is likely to RAISE the repo rate to cool prices. Higher
policy rates push up borrowing costs and bond yields, and they raise the discount rate used
to value equities. Separately, note the real-versus-nominal point: if nominal GDP is growing
11% while inflation is 6%, real growth is only about 5% - the figure that reflects genuine extra
output.

Analysis. The chain runs: inflation up, then the RBI tightens, then interest rates rise, then
borrowing costs rise and equity valuations compress - hitting rate-sensitive and high-growth
sectors hardest (real estate, autos, richly valued growth stocks), while some sectors weather
it better. The reverse chain - falling inflation and a rate-cutting cycle - tends to support
equities broadly. None of this names a single stock, but it tells the analyst the DIRECTION of
the tide and which sectors to lean toward or away from.


### Analyst takeaway. — Macro signals do not pick stocks for you; they reveal the direction of the

tide and which sectors face a headwind or a tailwind. That is exactly why top-down analysis
begins with the economy before drilling into industries and companies.


### Common mistakes — The Macro Traps That Lose Marks

1. Mistake: Confusing real and nominal figures.
Why it happens: Both are quoted as percentages.

Fix: Nominal includes inflation; real strips it out. Real growth or return is roughly nominal minus
inflation. A high nominal number with high inflation can mean weak real growth.

2. Mistake: Confusing CPI and WPI.
Why it happens: Both are inflation measures.

Fix: CPI tracks retail (consumer) prices and is the RBI's target measure; WPI tracks wholesale prices.
They can diverge significantly.

3. Mistake: Mixing up monetary and fiscal policy.
Why it happens: Both influence the economy.

Fix: Monetary policy is the RBI's domain - interest rates and money supply. Fiscal policy is the
government's - spending and taxation. Different actors, different tools.

4. Mistake: Getting the interest-rate logic backwards.
Why it happens: The link from rates to markets is indirect.

Fix: Cutting rates (expansionary) stimulates the economy and generally supports equities; raising rates
(contractionary) cools it and pressures valuations. Higher rates are not good for stocks.

5. Mistake: Confusing leading and lagging indicators.
Why it happens: All are 'economic indicators'.

Fix: Leading indicators (the stock market, PMI) predict the future; lagging indicators (unemployment,
inflation) confirm the past. Forecast with leading indicators, not lagging ones.

## Session 6 — Industry Analysis — Porters Five Forces,

Life Cycle and Dynamics
*Exam weight: 8 marks*


### Hook — Judge the Pond Before You Judge the Fish

Two equally talented management teams. One runs a company in a structurally beautiful
industry - high entry barriers, loyal customers, real pricing power. The other runs a company
in a brutal commodity industry - wafer-thin margins, constant price wars, no moat. Five years
on, the first has compounded wealth for its owners; the second has fought just to stay alive.
The managers were not the difference. The INDUSTRY was. There is an old line in investing:
when a manager with a great reputation takes on an industry with a bad reputation, it is
usually the industry's reputation that survives intact. Industry analysis is how you size up the
pond before you bother judging the fish - because even a brilliant company swimming in
shark-infested water struggles to thrive. On the exam this chapter carries 8 marks, the
heaviest outside the fundamental, technical and legal blocks, and examiners love turning it
into case studies that ask you to APPLY the frameworks.


> *Hook question. If two companies are equally well-managed, what single factor best explains*

why one earns fat, durable profits while the other barely survives?


### Concept — Porter's Five Forces: What Makes an Industry Attractive

Porter's Five Forces is the master framework for judging how much profit an industry can
sustainably earn. One: the threat of new entrants - high barriers to entry (heavy capital
needs, strong brands, regulation, economies of scale) keep newcomers out and protect
incumbents' profits; low barriers invite competition that erodes them. Two: the bargaining
power of suppliers - when suppliers are few and their inputs are critical, they raise input costs
and squeeze the industry's margins. Three: the bargaining power of buyers - large,
concentrated, price-sensitive buyers push the industry's selling prices down. Four: the threat
of substitutes - readily available alternatives from other industries cap how much the
industry can charge. Five: competitive rivalry - intense rivalry (many similar players, slow
growth, high fixed costs) triggers price wars. The more favourable each force, the more
attractive and profitable the industry. An analyst runs these five gauges to decide whether an
industry is worth being in at all.


> **Analogy.** Picture an industry as a fortress guarding a treasure of profits. The five forces are

the five gates an attacker can come through: new entrants storming the main gate, suppliers
raising the drawbridge toll, buyers demanding a ransom, substitutes tunnelling under the wall,
and rivals already inside fighting over the gold. The fortress is only as profitable as its weakest
gate is strong.

**Key terms**

- Porter's Five Forces — A framework assessing industry attractiveness through five
competitive forces.
- Barriers to Entry — Obstacles (capital, brand, regulation, scale) that keep new
competitors out and protect incumbents.
- Bargaining Power of Suppliers — The leverage input providers have to raise costs and
squeeze an industry's margins.
- Bargaining Power of Buyers — The leverage customers have to push an industry's selling
prices down.
- Threat of Substitutes — The risk that alternative products from other industries satisfy
the same need and cap pricing.

- Competitive Rivalry — The intensity of competition among existing players, which can
spark price wars.


### Concept — Life Cycle, PESTEL & Industry Types

Three more lenses complete the picture. The industry life cycle: every industry passes
through phases - Pioneering/Introduction (new product, few players, high risk, little or no
profit, heavy investment), Growth/Expansion (rapid revenue growth, rising profits, many new
entrants), Maturity/Stabilisation (slowing growth, consolidation, strong cash flows, price
competition), and Decline (falling demand, exits, shrinking profits). The phase tells you what
to expect from growth, margins and competition. PESTEL analysis: the macro-environmental
forces shaping an industry - Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Environmental and
Legal. Where Porter looks inside the industry, PESTEL scans the external big picture. Industry
types by behaviour: cyclical industries (auto, metals, cement, real estate) swing sharply with
the economy; defensive or non-cyclical industries (FMCG, pharma, utilities) hold up through
downturns; growth industries are structurally expanding. And competitive structure ranges
from fragmented (many small players) to consolidated, with market forms from perfect
competition through monopolistic competition and oligopoly to monopoly.


> **Analogy.** If Porter's Five Forces is a health check on the industry's body, PESTEL is the

weather forecast for the whole region it lives in - you can be perfectly healthy, but a storm (a
new law, a technology shift) still changes everything. And the life cycle is simply the industry's
age: a toddler (pioneering) behaves nothing like a retiree (decline).

**Key terms**

- Industry Life Cycle — The phases an industry passes through: pioneering, growth,
maturity and decline.
- PESTEL Analysis — Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Environmental and Legal
forces shaping an industry's external environment.
- Cyclical Industry — An industry whose demand and profits swing with the economic cycle
(e.g., auto, metals).
- Defensive Industry — A non-cyclical industry with stable demand through downturns
(e.g., FMCG, pharma, utilities).
- Competitive Structure — How concentrated an industry is, from fragmented to
consolidated.
- Oligopoly — A market dominated by a few large firms that often hold meaningful pricing
power.

### Deep dive — Applying the Frameworks: Structure, Stage & Cycle

Applying Porter to a real industry
Take Indian paints. Threat of new entrants: historically LOW - building a nationwide dealer
network and a trusted brand takes decades, a high barrier. Supplier power: moderate - key
inputs are crude-linked, so costs swing with oil. Buyer power: low - millions of small,
fragmented customers have little leverage. Substitutes: limited. Rivalry: historically
disciplined among a few large players. Net verdict: a structurally attractive industry - which is
exactly why incumbents earned high, stable returns, and why a large new entrant changes
the rivalry calculus.

Reading the life cycle for investment implications
Pioneering industries are speculative and cash-burning. Growth industries can deliver the
best returns but demand caution on valuation. Mature industries are cash cows that pay
dividends and consolidate. Declining industries are often value traps to avoid. Mapping Indian
sectors: quick commerce sits in growth, mainstream FMCG in maturity, and fixed-line
telephony in decline.

Cyclical versus defensive: timing the economy
Cyclical industries - auto, metals, cement, real estate - amplify the business cycle, booming
in expansions and slumping in downturns. Defensive industries - FMCG, pharma, utilities -
hold up because people keep buying soap and medicine regardless. This links directly to
economic analysis (Chapter 5): analysts rotate towards cyclicals early in a recovery and
towards defensives when a slowdown looms.

Competitive structure and pricing power
A fragmented industry of many small players usually competes on price and earns thin
margins. A consolidated or oligopolistic industry of a few disciplined players tends to hold
pricing power and higher margins. Consolidation - fewer, larger competitors - is therefore
often a positive structural shift for the survivors, as seen when an industry moves from many
players to a handful.


### Real-world references

- Indian telecom consolidated from many operators to a handful, which improved pricing
discipline for the survivors - a textbook competitive-structure shift.
- The paints industry long enjoyed high barriers (distribution reach and brand), historically
protecting incumbents' returns, with a large new entrant now testing that rivalry dynamic.
- Metals, autos and cement are classic cyclical sectors whose profits track the economic
cycle, while FMCG and pharma behave defensively through downturns.


### Real-world example — Why Structure Beats Effort: Branded FMCG vs a

Commodity Industry
Context. A branded consumer (FMCG) industry vs a commodity metals industry

Situation. Two well-run companies, one in branded FMCG and one in commodity metals,
earn very different returns year after year. Running Porter's Five Forces on each industry

explains why - before any company-specific analysis.

Data. Branded FMCG: barriers to entry HIGH (brand, distribution); supplier power moderate;
buyer power LOW (fragmented consumers); substitutes limited; rivalry disciplined - a
structurally ATTRACTIVE industry with pricing power, producing high and stable ROCE.
Commodity metals: barriers MODERATE but the product is undifferentiated; supplier power
can be high (ore, energy); buyer power higher (industrial buyers); substitutes exist (other
materials); rivalry intense and global, with firms as price-takers to global commodity prices -
a structurally HARSH, cyclical industry with volatile, generally lower returns.

Analysis. The same effort produces very different outcomes because the industry structures
differ. FMCG's favourable five forces let even an average player earn decent, steady profits;
metals' hostile forces mean even a good operator is hostage to the cycle and global prices.
Industry structure, not management heroics, explains most of the gap.


### Analyst takeaway. — Before you value any company, judge its industry. A company in a

structurally attractive industry has a permanent tailwind; one in a brutal industry fights a
headwind every single quarter - which is why industry analysis comes before company
analysis in the top-down approach.


### Common mistakes — The Industry-Analysis Traps That Lose Marks

1. Mistake: Analysing the company in isolation and skipping the industry.
Why it happens: Company numbers feel more concrete than frameworks.

Fix: Go top-down. A great company in a bad industry usually underperforms; always size up the pond
before judging the fish.

2. Mistake: Confusing supplier power with buyer power (and substitutes with new entrants).
Why it happens: The forces sound similar.

Fix: Suppliers sell INTO the industry (input side); buyers purchase FROM it (output side). Substitutes are
alternatives from OTHER industries; new entrants and rivalry concern players in the SAME industry.

3. Mistake: Assuming high growth means high profit.
Why it happens: Growth feels unambiguously good.

Fix: Pioneering and growth phases often burn cash and attract competition; maturity frequently has the
strongest cash flows. Match expectations to the life-cycle phase.

4. Mistake: Treating all industries as equally affected by the economy.
Why it happens: Macro effects are assumed to be uniform.

Fix: Cyclicals (auto, metals) swing hard with the cycle; defensives (FMCG, pharma) do not. Classify the
industry before judging its sensitivity.

5. Mistake: Thinking a fragmented industry is good because it offers lots of choice.
Why it happens: Fragmentation is read as healthy competition.

Fix: For the producer and the investor, fragmentation usually means price wars and thin margins;
consolidation and oligopoly tend to bring pricing power and better returns.

## Session 7 — Company Analysis I — Business Model,

Management and Governance
*Exam weight: 6 marks*


### Hook — Beautiful Numbers Can Still Lose You Money

You can find a company with gorgeous financials - growing revenue, fat margins, a strong
return on equity - and still lose money owning it. How? Because numbers describe the PAST.
What determines the future is two things the income statement never shows: the durability
of the moat protecting those margins, and the integrity of the people running the company. A
great business with weak governance can be quietly looted by its own promoters. A merely
good business with a powerful moat can compound for decades. Chapter 8 measured the
numbers; Chapter 7 judges the QUALITY behind them - the business model, the competitive
advantage, the management and the governance. On the exam this is worth 6 marks, and
NISM tests judgement here, often through case studies, not just definitions.


> *Hook question. Two companies have identical financials this year. What would make you*

confidently buy one and refuse to touch the other?


### Concept — Business Model and the Economic Moat

A business model is simply how a company makes money: its value proposition (the problem
it solves), its revenue model (how it charges) and its cost structure. But a good model alone
is not enough - what protects profits over time is the economic MOAT, a durable competitive
advantage that stops rivals from competing those profits away. The main sources of moat
are: brand and intangibles (a trusted brand earns pricing power); cost advantage (being the
lowest-cost producer through scale, process or location); network effects (the product grows
more valuable as more people use it, as with exchanges and platforms); switching costs (it is
painful or expensive for customers to leave, as with enterprise software or a primary bank);
and regulatory or licence barriers that legally limit competition. A wide, durable moat is what
turns a good business into a great long-term investment, because it shields the high returns -
a sustained high ROCE - from erosion.


> **Analogy.** A moat is exactly what it sounds like: the water around a castle. The castle is the

company's profits, and competitors are the attacking army. A wide moat - a strong brand, a
cost edge, network effects - keeps the army from storming in and plundering the treasure. A
business with no moat is a castle with no water around it: anyone can walk in and take the
gold, which is precisely why its high profits do not last.

**Key terms**

- Business Model — How a company creates value and makes money - its value proposition,
revenue model and cost structure.
- Value Proposition — The specific problem a company solves or need it meets for its
customers.
- Economic Moat — A durable competitive advantage that protects a company's profits from
competitors.
- Pricing Power — The ability to raise prices without losing significant customers - the acid
test of a strong moat.
- Network Effects — A moat where a product becomes more valuable as more people use it
(platforms, exchanges).
- Switching Costs — The cost or difficulty for a customer of moving to a competitor, which
locks in revenue.


### Concept — Management Quality and Corporate Governance

Even a great business can be ruined by the wrong people, so analysts assess management
and governance. Management quality covers track record, integrity, execution, and above all
capital allocation skill - whether profits are deployed wisely or squandered - plus whether pay
is aligned with shareholders. Corporate governance is the system of rules and structures
ensuring a company is run for ALL shareholders, not just insiders. Its key components: board
structure (enough independent directors to challenge management; ideally a separation of
the Chairman and CEO roles); promoter holding and pledging (high ownership signals skin in
the game, but heavily PLEDGED promoter shares - borrowed against - are a serious red flag);
related party transactions, or RPTs (deals with promoter-connected entities, which if large or
non-arm's-length can siphon value out); auditor independence (frequent auditor changes or
qualified opinions are warning signs); and disclosure and transparency, governed in India by
SEBI's Listing Obligations and Disclosure Requirements (LODR) Regulations. Strong
governance protects minority shareholders; weak governance lets insiders enrich themselves
at others' expense.


> **Analogy.** If the moat protects the company from outside attackers, governance protects it

from INSIDE betrayal. Picture a household staff managing a wealthy family's estate while the
family is away. Good governance is the system of audits, independent oversight and
transparency that ensures the staff do not quietly help themselves to the silver. Promoter
pledging is like the manager having secretly mortgaged the house; a large related-party deal
is like the manager hiring his own cousin's firm at inflated prices.

**Key terms**

- Corporate Governance — The system of rules and oversight ensuring a company is run in
the interest of all shareholders.
- Independent Directors — Board members with no material ties to management who
provide objective oversight and protect minority shareholders.
- Promoter Pledging — Promoters borrowing against their shares; high pledging signals
hidden leverage and forced-sale risk - a red flag.
- Related Party Transactions (RPTs) — Deals between the company and
promoter-connected entities that can siphon value if not at arm's length.
- Capital Allocation — How management deploys profits - reinvestment, acquisitions,
dividends, buybacks or debt repayment - a key quality measure.
- SEBI LODR — The Listing Obligations and Disclosure Requirements Regulations governing
listed-company governance and disclosure in India.

### Deep dive — Reading the Business, the Moat, the Managers and the Red

Flags
Dissecting the business model
To understand any company, ask: what does it sell, to whom, and how does it charge -
one-time or recurring? What is the cost structure, and what are the unit economics? Does it
scale cheaply? Asset-light businesses (services, platforms) need little capital and can earn
very high returns; asset-heavy businesses (manufacturing, infrastructure) need large
investment and usually earn lower, steadier returns. The model shapes everything that
follows.

Judging the moat's width and durability
A moat is not permanent - technology, regulation and competition can erode it. The clearest
evidence of a real moat is a return on capital that has STAYED high for many years; an
eroding ROCE signals a weakening moat. Pricing power is the acid test: can the company
raise prices without losing customers? If yes, the moat is real.

Capital allocation - the CEO's most important job
What management does with profits often matters more than the profits themselves:
reinvest in the core business, acquire, pay dividends, buy back shares, or repay debt. Skilled
allocators compound shareholder wealth; poor ones make value-destroying acquisitions into
unrelated areas - sometimes called 'diworsification'. Capital allocation is a crucial, frequently
overlooked dimension of management quality.

Governance red flags - the forensic link
This is where qualitative analysis meets the red-flag work of forensic accounting. Watch for
heavy promoter pledging (forced-sale risk and hidden leverage), large or opaque
related-party transactions (value siphoning), frequent auditor resignations or qualified
opinions, needlessly complex group structures, and aggressive accounting. In India, SEBI's
LODR Regulations mandate board composition, disclosures and approvals for related-party
transactions.


### Real-world references

- Brand-led consumer franchises, exchange and depository businesses with network effects,
and genuine low-cost producers are classic Indian examples of durable moats that sustain
high returns over long periods.
- Several documented Indian corporate failures were preceded by recognisable governance
red flags such as heavy promoter share pledging and large related-party transactions.
- SEBI's LODR Regulations require listed companies to maintain a minimum proportion of
independent directors and to disclose and seek approval for material related-party
transactions.


### Real-world example — Identical Numbers, Opposite Verdicts

Context. Two companies with matching financials but very different quality

Situation. Company A and Company B post identical revenue, margins and ROE this year.
On the numbers alone they look like twins. The qualitative analysis pulls them sharply apart.

Data. Company A: a strong consumer brand with demonstrable pricing power and an ROCE
that has stayed high for a decade; governance is clean - an independent board, zero
promoter pledging and only minor, arm's-length related-party transactions. Company B: a
commodity product with no pricing power and an ROCE that has been drifting down;
governance is troubling - promoter shares are heavily pledged, related-party transactions are
large, and the auditor was changed last year.

Analysis. Identical current numbers do not mean identical futures. Company A's moat
suggests its high returns will persist, and its clean governance means those returns actually
reach minority shareholders. Company B has neither protection: no moat to stop competition
from eroding its margins, and weak governance that exposes minority holders to value being
siphoned out. A disciplined analyst buys A with confidence and avoids B - despite the
matching headline figures.


### Analyst takeaway. — Financials are necessary but not sufficient. The moat tells you whether

the profits will LAST; governance tells you whether they will reach YOU, the minority
shareholder, or be quietly diverted to insiders.


### Common mistakes — The Company-Quality Traps That Lose Marks

1. Mistake: Judging a company only on current financials and ignoring the moat.
Why it happens: Numbers feel objective and final.

Fix: A high current ROE means little without a moat to defend it - competition erodes unprotected
returns. Check whether returns have stayed high for years.

2. Mistake: Assuming high promoter holding is always good.
Why it happens: High ownership is read as alignment.

Fix: High ownership does signal skin in the game, but heavily PLEDGED promoter shares are a red flag
(hidden leverage, forced-sale risk). Always check the pledge level, not just the ownership percentage.

3. Mistake: Overlooking related-party transactions.
Why it happens: RPTs are buried in the notes to the accounts.

Fix: Large or non-arm's-length RPTs can transfer value from minority shareholders to insiders.
Scrutinise their size and rationale.

4. Mistake: Treating every acquisition as growth.
Why it happens: Deals look like expansion and ambition.

Fix: Many acquisitions destroy value ('diworsification'). Judge management's capital-allocation track
record, not the volume of deal-making.

5. Mistake: Confusing a charismatic promoter with good management.
Why it happens: A compelling personality is persuasive.

Fix: Charisma is not integrity or capital-allocation skill. Judge the track record, the governance
structures and the treatment of minority shareholders, not the personality.

## Session 8 — Company Analysis II — Financial

Statement & Ratio Analysis
*Exam weight: 12 marks*


### Hook — Same Profit, Two Very Different Companies

Two companies, A and B, both report a net profit of exactly 90 crore this year. On profit
alone, a beginner calls them twins. Then you compute the ratios. Company A earns that 90
crore on 500 crore of equity with almost no debt — an 18% return on equity, interest covered
five times over. Company B earns the same 90 crore but on 200 crore of equity propped up
by a mountain of borrowing — its return on equity flatters to 45%, yet its profit barely covers
its interest bill, and one bad quarter could sink it. Identical profit. Opposite health. This is the
entire point of Chapter 8: the exam will not ask 'is the profit big?' It hands you raw numbers
and expects you to convert them into a ratio in under a minute and read what it means. At 12
marks, this chapter is tied for the heaviest on the paper — and it is almost entirely numerical.
The good news: every question reduces to a handful of formulas you can drill until they are
reflex.


> *Hook question. If two companies post identical profits but one has an ROE of 18% and the*

other 45%, which one is actually the safer business — and which ratio gives away the trick?


### Concept — The Five Families of Ratios

Every financial ratio you need for the exam belongs to one of five families, and each family
answers one question. Profitability ratios ask: how efficiently does the company turn sales
and capital into profit? (margins, ROE, ROCE, ROA). Liquidity ratios ask: can it pay its bills
over the next few months? (current ratio, quick ratio). Solvency or leverage ratios ask: can it
survive its debt over the long run? (debt-to-equity, interest coverage). Efficiency or activity
ratios ask: how well does it use its assets and working capital? (inventory, receivables and
asset turnover). Valuation ratios ask: is the stock cheap or expensive for what you get? (P/E,
P/B, EV/EBITDA, EPS). The single most important rule: a ratio in isolation is meaningless. A
current ratio of 2 only means something against the industry norm, the company's own
history, and its peers.


> **Analogy.** Think of a full-body health check-up. No doctor declares you healthy from one

reading. Liquidity is your blood pressure (the immediate, short-term signal), solvency is your
cholesterol (the slow, long-term risk), profitability is your fitness level, and efficiency is your
metabolism. Valuation is the price the insurer charges for your policy given all of the above.
You read them together, and always against a benchmark for someone your age — the peer
group.

**Key terms**

- Profitability Ratios — Measure how efficiently a company generates profit from sales and
capital, e.g., net margin, ROE, ROCE.
- Liquidity Ratios — Measure ability to meet short-term obligations, e.g., current ratio, quick
ratio.
- Solvency / Leverage Ratios — Measure long-term debt-bearing capacity, e.g.,
debt-to-equity, interest coverage.
- Efficiency / Activity Ratios — Measure how well assets and working capital are used, e.g.,
inventory and receivables turnover.
- Valuation Ratios — Relate price to fundamentals to judge cheapness, e.g., P/E, P/B,
EV/EBITDA.

- Benchmarking — Comparing a ratio against industry norms, peers, and the company's
own history to make it meaningful.


### Formula — The Ratio Formula Bank (All From One Company)

Memorise these and Chapter 8's numerical questions become mechanical. Every formula
below is worked on the SAME illustrative company so you can see how one set of statements
produces every ratio. Drill them until the formula and its denominator are reflex.

> Shared example. Bharat Manufacturing Ltd, FY24 (Rs crore): Revenue 1,000 | COGS 600 |
Gross Profit 400 | EBITDA 200 | Depreciation 50 | EBIT 150 | Interest 30 | PBT 120 | Tax 30 |
PAT 90. Balance sheet: Shareholders' Equity 500 | Total Debt 300 | Total Assets 1,000 | Current
Assets 400 (Inventory 150, Receivables 120, Cash 130) | Current Liabilities 200 | Payables 100.
Market: 10 crore shares at Rs 180.
Gross Profit Margin
> Formula: (Gross Profit / Revenue) x 100
> Example: 400 / 1000 x 100 = 40%
> Meaning: 40 paise of gross profit per rupee of sales, before overheads.

EBITDA Margin
> Formula: (EBITDA / Revenue) x 100
> Example: 200 / 1000 x 100 = 20%
> Meaning: Core operating profitability before depreciation, interest and tax.

Operating (EBIT) Margin
> Formula: (EBIT / Revenue) x 100
> Example: 150 / 1000 x 100 = 15%
> Meaning: Profitability of core operations after depreciation.

Net Profit Margin
> Formula: (PAT / Revenue) x 100
> Example: 90 / 1000 x 100 = 9%
> Meaning: Bottom-line profit per rupee of sales, after everything.

Return on Equity (ROE)
> Formula: (PAT / Shareholders' Equity) x 100
> Example: 90 / 500 x 100 = 18%
> Meaning: Return generated on owners' money. Watch: leverage can inflate it.

Return on Capital Employed (ROCE)
> Formula: (EBIT / Capital Employed) x 100, where Capital Employed = Total Assets - Current
Liabilities
> Example: 150 / (1000 - 200) = 150 / 800 = 18.75%
> Meaning: Return on all long-term capital (equity + debt). Cleaner than ROE for comparing
across capital structures.

Return on Assets (ROA)
> Formula: (PAT / Total Assets) x 100
> Example: 90 / 1000 x 100 = 9%
> Meaning: How efficiently total assets generate profit.

DuPont ROE (3-step)

> Formula: Net Profit Margin x Asset Turnover x Equity Multiplier
> Example: 0.09 x 1.0 x 2.0 = 0.18 = 18%
> Meaning: Splits ROE into its three engines: profitability, efficiency, leverage. Equity Multiplier =
Total Assets / Equity = 1000/500 = 2.0.

Current Ratio
> Formula: Current Assets / Current Liabilities
> Example: 400 / 200 = 2.0
> Meaning: Two rupees of short-term assets for every rupee of short-term dues.

Quick (Acid-Test) Ratio
> Formula: (Current Assets - Inventory) / Current Liabilities
> Example: (400 - 150) / 200 = 1.25
> Meaning: Liquidity excluding slow-moving inventory; the stricter test.

Debt-to-Equity
> Formula: Total Debt / Shareholders' Equity
> Example: 300 / 500 = 0.6
> Meaning: 0.6 rupee of debt per rupee of equity. Lower is safer.

Interest Coverage Ratio
> Formula: EBIT / Interest Expense
> Example: 150 / 30 = 5.0 times
> Meaning: EBIT covers interest 5x over. Below ~1.5x is a danger zone.

Debt / EBITDA
> Formula: Total Debt / EBITDA
> Example: 300 / 200 = 1.5 times
> Meaning: Roughly how many years of operating profit to repay all debt.

Inventory Turnover
> Formula: COGS / Inventory
> Example: 600 / 150 = 4.0 times; Days of Inventory = 365 / 4 = ~91 days
> Meaning: How many times inventory is sold and replaced a year. Higher = leaner.

Receivables Turnover
> Formula: Revenue / Receivables
> Example: 1000 / 120 = 8.33 times; Days Sales Outstanding = 365 / 8.33 = ~44 days
> Meaning: How fast customers pay. Lower DSO is better.

Asset Turnover
> Formula: Revenue / Total Assets
> Example: 1000 / 1000 = 1.0 times
> Meaning: Sales generated per rupee of assets; the efficiency leg of DuPont.

Cash Conversion Cycle
> Formula: Days Inventory + Days Sales Outstanding - Days Payable Outstanding
> Example: 91 + 44 - 61 = ~74 days (DPO = 365 x 100/600 = ~61)
> Meaning: Days cash is tied up in operations. Lower (or negative) is stronger.

Earnings Per Share (EPS)

> Formula: PAT / Number of Shares
> Example: 90 / 10 = Rs 9
> Meaning: Profit attributable to each share.

Price-to-Earnings (P/E)
> Formula: Market Price per Share / EPS
> Example: 180 / 9 = 20 times
> Meaning: Rupees investors pay per rupee of annual earnings.

Price-to-Book (P/B)
> Formula: Market Price per Share / Book Value per Share
> Example: 180 / (500/10 = Rs 50) = 3.6 times
> Meaning: Price relative to net asset value per share.

EV / EBITDA
> Formula: Enterprise Value / EBITDA, where EV = Market Cap + Debt - Cash
> Example: (1800 + 300 - 130) / 200 = 1970 / 200 = 9.85 times
> Meaning: Capital-structure-neutral valuation; preferred for comparing leveraged firms.

### Deep dive — Beyond the Ratios: Trends, Cash Flow & the Chain

Common-size and trend analysis
Common-size (vertical) analysis restates every P&L line as a percentage of revenue and
every balance-sheet line as a percentage of total assets, so you can compare companies of
different sizes and spot cost creep. Trend (horizontal) analysis tracks the same line over
several years to reveal direction. A 40% gross margin is fine; a gross margin that has fallen
from 46% to 40% over three years is a story the exam loves.

Earnings quality: profit is an opinion, cash is a fact
A company can report strong profit while generating weak cash. Compare cash flow from
operations (CFO) with net profit: if profit keeps rising but CFO lags or turns negative, profits
may be propped up by aggressive accruals or receivables that are not converting to cash.
Free cash flow (CFO minus capex) shows what is truly left for owners and lenders.

A ratio is meaningless without a benchmark
Judge every ratio three ways: against the industry norm, against close peers, and against the
company's own history. A current ratio of 2 may be healthy for a manufacturer but unusual
for an asset-light services firm. Banks and NBFCs use an entirely different ratio set, so never
compare them with manufacturers.

Ratios connect into a chain
Ratios are not independent. Slowing inventory turnover lengthens days-of-inventory, which
lengthens the cash conversion cycle, which strains liquidity, which can force borrowing,
which weakens interest coverage. When one ratio moves, trace the chain it pulls. Reading
the family together is what separates an analyst from a calculator.


### Real-world references

- Asset-light IT majors such as TCS post very high ROCE because they need little capital,
whereas capital-heavy steel and cement firms run far lower ROCE for similar margins.
- Several Indian companies that later faced trouble showed rising reported profit alongside
deteriorating cash flow from operations in the years before the stress surfaced.
- Value retailers like DMart run thin margins but extremely high asset turnover, while
premium FMCG firms run the opposite mix - both can deliver strong ROE.


### Real-world example — Two Routes to the Same ROE: FMCG vs Value

Retailer
Context. A premium FMCG company vs a value retailer (representative figures)

Situation. Both businesses deliver a return on equity of roughly 23%, and a beginner
concludes they are equally good. DuPont tells a richer story about HOW each earns it.

Data. Premium FMCG: net margin 15%, asset turnover 1.2x, equity multiplier 1.3x -> ROE =
0.15 x 1.2 x 1.3 = 23.4%. Value retailer: net margin 4%, asset turnover 4.0x, equity
multiplier 1.4x -> ROE = 0.04 x 4.0 x 1.4 = 22.4%.

Analysis. Nearly identical ROE, opposite engines. The FMCG firm earns through fat margins
on relatively few rupees of sales per asset (pricing power, brand). The retailer earns through
razor-thin margins multiplied by enormous sales velocity (volume, footfall, low prices).
Neither relies much on leverage - both equity multipliers are modest, which is healthy. Their
risks differ entirely: the FMCG firm is exposed to margin pressure (input costs, competition),
the retailer to any drop in volume or footfall.


### Analyst takeaway. — Identical ROE can come from completely different sources. DuPont

identifies which lever is doing the work, and that tells you what to worry about. The one ROE
to distrust is the kind driven mainly by a high equity multiplier - that is borrowed shine, not
operational strength.


### Common mistakes — The Ratio Traps That Lose Marks (and Money)

1. Mistake: Comparing ratios across different industries.
Why it happens: Students treat a ratio as universally good or bad.

Fix: Benchmark within the industry. A 1.1x current ratio may be normal for an asset-light firm and
alarming for a manufacturer; banks use a different ratio set entirely.

2. Mistake: Judging core performance by net profit margin.
Why it happens: Net profit feels like the 'real' number.

Fix: Net margin includes one-offs, other income and tax. For core operations, read EBITDA or EBIT
margin; reconcile to net only after understanding the operating picture.

3. Mistake: Cheering a high ROE without checking its source.
Why it happens: Higher ROE looks unambiguously better.

Fix: A high ROE built on a large equity multiplier (heavy debt) is fragile. Cross-check ROE against
debt-to-equity, interest coverage and ROCE, and run DuPont to see the real driver.

4. Mistake: Ignoring whether profit converts to cash.
Why it happens: Ratios are computed off the P&L and balance sheet, so cash flow gets skipped.

Fix: Compare CFO with net profit. Rising profit with lagging or negative CFO is a classic earnings-quality
red flag.

5. Mistake: Reading one ratio in isolation.
Why it happens: Each ratio is computed separately, so it feels self-contained.

Fix: Read the family together and trace the chain. A high current ratio caused by bloated, unsold
inventory is poor liquidity, not strong liquidity.

### Hook — The Stock 'Crashed' 50% Overnight - and Nobody Lost a Rupee

One morning a shareholder opens their app and sees a stock they own has fallen from Rs
2,000 to Rs 1,000 overnight. They panic and nearly hit sell. But they have lost absolutely
nothing - the company did a 1:1 bonus issue, doubling everyone's share count. Twice as
many shares at half the price is exactly the same wealth. This is the essence of corporate
actions: most of them change the FORM of your holding, not its underlying value. Splits,
bonuses, rights and buybacks all reshape the share count and the price in precise, calculable
ways - and a beginner who does not know the mechanics either panics needlessly or
misreads the maths on a test. On the exam this chapter is worth 5 marks and is numerical:
you will compute adjusted prices, new share counts, and the theoretical price after a rights
issue. Learn the formulas and these are some of the easiest marks on the paper.


> *Hook question. If a Rs 2,000 stock you own suddenly trades at Rs 1,000 the next morning,*

how do you tell in seconds whether you have lost half your money or nothing at all?


### Concept — The Four Corporate Actions That Reshape Your Shares

A corporate action is a company-initiated event that affects its shares and shareholders. Four
are exam-critical. First, the stock SPLIT: the face value is cut and the shares multiply in
proportion - a split where each share becomes two turns one Rs 10-face share into two Rs
5-face shares. More shares, lower price, same total value, reserves untouched; it is cosmetic,
done to improve liquidity and affordability. Second, the BONUS issue: free extra shares given
to existing holders out of the company's RESERVES (reserves are capitalised into share
capital) - a 1:1 bonus gives one free share for every one held. More shares, lower price, same
total value, but unlike a split the face value stays the SAME and reserves convert to capital.
Third, the RIGHTS issue: existing holders get the RIGHT, not the obligation, to buy new shares
at a DISCOUNTED price in proportion to their holding (a 1:4 rights means one new share for
every four held). This raises fresh cash for the company. Fourth, the BUYBACK: the company
repurchases its own shares, cutting the share count (which lifts EPS) and returning cash to
shareholders. In short: split and bonus change neither value nor cash; a rights issue raises
cash; a buyback returns cash.


> **Analogy.** Think of a pizza. A split or bonus is just cutting the same pizza into more slices -

more slices, but the same amount of pizza, so your value is unchanged. A rights issue is being
offered the chance to pay for an extra slice at a discount - you put in more money and the
pizza gets bigger. A buyback is the restaurant buying some slices back from the table, leaving
fewer slices among those who remain, so each remaining slice is a bigger share of the pie.

**Key terms**

- Corporate Action — A company-initiated event (split, bonus, rights, buyback, dividend)
that affects its shares and shareholders.
- Stock Split — Face value is reduced and shares multiply proportionally; price falls, total
value and reserves unchanged.
- Bonus Issue — Free shares issued from reserves (capitalised into capital); face value
unchanged, total value unchanged.
- Rights Issue — Existing holders get the right to buy new shares at a discount, pro-rata;
raises fresh cash for the company.

- Buyback — The company repurchases its own shares, reducing the share count, lifting EPS
and returning cash.
- Face Value — The nominal value of a share; reduced by a split but left unchanged by a
bonus issue.


### Formula — The Corporate Action Formula Bank (With Worked Numbers)

These adjustments look intimidating but are just rescaling. Be precise about ratio direction -
1:4 rights means ONE new share for every FOUR held, not the reverse. The TERP and
value-of-a-right formulas are the most-tested calculations, so drill them. Most examples use
one base case so the mechanics stay clear.

> Shared example. Base case: a share trades at Rs 600 with a face value of Rs 10, and an
investor holds 100 shares. Splits, bonuses and rights are applied to this base; the buyback and
dividend examples use the figures stated within each line.
Adjusted Price After Split
> Formula: Adjusted Price = Old Price / Split Multiple; Shares = Old Shares x Split Multiple
> Example: Split where each share becomes 2 (face Rs 10 to Rs 5), price Rs 600: 600 / 2 = Rs
300; 100 shares become 200. Total value unchanged: 200 x 300 = 60,000 = 100 x 600.
> Meaning: A split only rescales price and share count; total value and reserves are unchanged.

Adjusted Price After Bonus
> Formula: Adjusted Price = Old Price x [Existing Shares / (Existing + Bonus Shares)]
> Example: 1:1 bonus (one bonus per one held), price Rs 600: 600 x [1 / (1 + 1)] = Rs 300. A 1:2
bonus (one per two held): 600 x [2 / (2 + 1)] = Rs 400.
> Meaning: Like a split, value is unchanged - but the face value stays the same and reserves
convert to share capital.

Shares After Bonus
> Formula: New Shares = Old Shares x (1 + Bonus Ratio)
> Example: 1:1 bonus on 100 shares: 100 x (1 + 1) = 200. A 1:2 bonus on 100: 100 x (1 + 0.5) =
150.
> Meaning: The bonus ratio is bonus shares per existing share (1:1 = ratio 1; 1:2 = ratio 0.5).

Rights Entitlement
> Formula: New Shares Entitled = Existing Shares x Rights Ratio
> Example: 1:4 rights (one new per four held) on 400 shares: 400 x (1/4) = 100 new shares.
> Meaning: Entitlement is strictly pro-rata to the existing holding.

Theoretical Ex-Rights Price (TERP)
> Formula: TERP = (Existing Shares x Cum-Rights Price + New Shares x Rights Price) / (Existing
+ New Shares)
> Example: 1:4 rights at Rs 450, cum price Rs 600. Per 4 existing + 1 new: (4 x 600 + 1 x 450) /
5 = 2,850 / 5 = Rs 570.
> Meaning: The price the stock should settle at after the rights - a weighted average of the old
price and the discounted rights price.

Value of One Right
> Formula: Value of a Right = Cum-Rights Price - TERP (equivalently (Cum Price - Rights Price) /
(N + 1))
> Example: Cum Rs 600, TERP Rs 570: 600 - 570 = Rs 30. Check: (600 - 450) / (4 + 1) = 150 / 5
= Rs 30.

> Meaning: What a shareholder can sell the right for if they choose not to subscribe (renounce).
N = existing shares needed per new share.

EPS After Buyback
> Formula: EPS After = Total Earnings / Reduced Share Count
> Example: Earnings Rs 100 cr, 10 cr shares (EPS Rs 10). Buy back 1 cr shares -> 9 cr shares:
100 / 9 = Rs 11.11.
> Meaning: Fewer shares lift EPS mechanically and return cash; value is added only if shares are
bought below intrinsic value.

Dividend Yield and Ex-Dividend Price
> Formula: Dividend Yield = Dividend per Share / Market Price; Ex-Dividend Price is about Cum
Price - Dividend per Share
> Example: DPS Rs 12, price Rs 600: yield = 12 / 600 = 2%; on the ex-date the price falls by
roughly the dividend, to about Rs 588.
> Meaning: A dividend returns cash directly; the price drops by approximately the dividend
amount on the ex-date.

### Deep dive — The Mechanics: Bonus vs Split, Rights, Dates and Buybacks

Bonus versus split - the subtle difference
Both increase the share count and lower the price with total value unchanged, but the
accounting differs. A bonus capitalises RESERVES (reserves move into share capital) and
keeps the face value the SAME. A split reduces the FACE VALUE and leaves reserves
untouched. Both improve liquidity and affordability and often signal management confidence.
The exam loves to test exactly this distinction.

Rights issue mechanics and TERP
Existing holders may buy discounted new shares pro-rata, and the company raises cash. The
price settles to the theoretical ex-rights price (TERP), a weighted average of the old price and
the rights price. A shareholder who does not wish to subscribe can RENOUNCE the right - sell

it - for its value. The discount is not a gift: it is offset by dilution, so the value of the right
roughly equals the price the stock gives up.

Record date, ex-date and book closure
The record date is the date on which you must be on the company's books to be eligible. The
ex-date is the date from which the stock trades WITHOUT the entitlement - buy on or after
the ex-date and you do NOT receive the benefit. Under India's T+1 settlement cycle, the
ex-date and record date typically fall on the same day. 'Cum' means with the entitlement;
'ex' means without it.

Buybacks and dividends - returning cash
Both return cash to shareholders. A buyback reduces the share count, mechanically lifting
EPS and often signalling that management sees the stock as undervalued; it can be done by
tender offer or in the open market. A dividend pays cash directly, and the share price falls by
roughly the dividend amount on the ex-date. A buyback adds value only if the shares are
repurchased below their intrinsic value.


### Real-world references

- Indian companies routinely announce bonus issues, stock splits and buybacks, and large IT
companies in particular have used sizeable buybacks to return cash to shareholders.
- India's move to a T+1 settlement cycle means the ex-date and record date for corporate
actions now generally coincide.
- A rights issue is a common way for Indian listed companies to raise fresh equity capital from
existing shareholders.


### Real-world example — Working Through a Rights Issue End to End

Context. An illustrative rights-issue calculation

Situation. An investor holds 100 shares trading at Rs 600 each (cum-rights). The company
announces a 1:4 rights issue - one new share for every four held - at a rights price of Rs 450.
What happens to the investor?

Data. Entitlement: 100 x (1/4) = 25 new shares. Cost to subscribe in full: 25 x 450 = Rs
11,250. Theoretical ex-rights price (TERP): (4 x 600 + 1 x 450) / 5 = Rs 570. Value of one
right: 600 - 570 = Rs 30, so the 100 rights are worth about 100 x 30 = Rs 3,000.

Analysis. After the issue the price drifts from Rs 600 toward the TERP of Rs 570. If the
investor SUBSCRIBES, they pay Rs 11,250 and end up with 125 shares worth about 125 x 570
= Rs 71,250 - exactly their original Rs 60,000 plus the Rs 11,250 they put in. If they instead
RENOUNCE (sell the rights), they collect roughly Rs 3,000, which compensates for the fall in
their existing shares from Rs 600 to Rs 570 (100 x 30 = Rs 3,000). Either way, the rights
mechanics alone leave them whole.


### Analyst takeaway. — A rights issue at a 'discount' is not free money - the discount is precisely

offset by dilution, and the price falls to TERP. What actually matters for value is what the
company DOES with the cash it raises, not the headline discount.


### Common mistakes — The Corporate-Action Traps That Lose Marks

1. Mistake: Panicking at the post-bonus or post-split price drop, thinking value was lost.
Why it happens: The price on screen falls sharply.

Fix: Bonus and split do not change your total value - you simply hold more shares at a proportionally
lower price.

2. Mistake: Confusing a bonus issue with a stock split.

Why it happens: Both raise the share count and cut the price.

Fix: A bonus capitalises reserves and keeps the face value the SAME; a split reduces the face value and
leaves reserves untouched.

3. Mistake: Treating a rights issue at a discount as free money.
Why it happens: The rights price is below the market price.

Fix: The discount is offset by dilution; the price falls to the TERP. The real benefit depends on how the
company uses the funds, not on the discount.

4. Mistake: Confusing the record date with the ex-date.
Why it happens: The two dates sound interchangeable.

Fix: You must own the shares BEFORE the ex-date to be eligible; buying on or after the ex-date means
you miss the entitlement. Under T+1, the ex-date and record date usually coincide.

5. Mistake: Believing a buyback always creates value.
Why it happens: A buyback mechanically raises EPS.

Fix: A buyback lifts EPS and returns cash, but it creates value only if the shares are repurchased
BELOW intrinsic value; overpaying destroys value.


### Summary — Chapter 9 in 60 Seconds

Key takeaways
- A stock split cuts the face value and multiplies the shares; a bonus issue capitalises
reserves and keeps the face value the same - both leave total value unchanged and raise no
cash.
- A rights issue offers existing holders discounted new shares pro-rata, raising fresh cash, and
the price settles to the theoretical ex-rights price (TERP).
- The value of a right equals the cum-rights price minus the TERP, so the discount is offset by
dilution rather than being free money.
- A buyback reduces the share count, mechanically lifting EPS and returning cash; it adds
value only if shares are bought below intrinsic value.
- The record date sets eligibility and the ex-date is when the stock trades without the
entitlement; under India's T+1 cycle the two typically coincide.
What you can now do. You can compute adjusted prices after splits and bonuses, calculate
rights entitlement and the theoretical ex-rights price, value a right, and explain record dates,
ex-dates and buyback effects - exactly the numerical tasks this chapter tests.

### Hook — The Price Is on the Screen. The Value Is Your Job.

A stock trades at Rs 600. One analyst glances at its P/E of 15 and shrugs 'fairly priced.' A

two verdicts. The market quotes a PRICE every moment of every day. It never tells you the
VALUE. Closing that gap - turning a company's future cash, dividends and growth into a
number you can compare against the quote - is the entire craft of valuation, and it is where
research analysts actually earn their keep. On the exam this chapter is worth 12 marks, tied
for the heaviest on the paper, and it is relentlessly numerical: you will be handed a dividend,
a growth rate and a discount rate and asked for an intrinsic value in under a minute. Master a
handful of formulas and these become free marks.


> *Hook question. If a stock trades at Rs 600 but your model says it is worth Rs 720, what*

exactly did your model account for that the market price on the screen did not?


### Concept — Two Roads to Value: Intrinsic vs Relative

Every valuation method belongs to one of two families. Intrinsic (absolute) valuation asks
'what is this business worth on its own fundamentals?' and answers it as the present value of
the cash it will generate - this is the world of discounted cash flow (DCF) and the dividend
discount model (DDM). Relative valuation asks 'is this stock cheap compared to its peers?'
and answers it with multiples - P/E, P/B, EV/EBITDA, PEG. The two are complementary:
intrinsic models give you an anchored, theory-based value but depend heavily on
assumptions; multiples are quick and market-grounded but only tell you cheap-or-dear
RELATIVE to whatever the peers are priced at. The unifying idea behind all of it: the value of
any asset is the present value of its expected future cash flows, discounted at a rate that
reflects its risk. Market price and intrinsic value are not the same thing - and the gap
between them is the opportunity an analyst hunts for.


> **Analogy.** Think of valuing a flat in Mumbai. The intrinsic (DCF) approach estimates the rent it

will earn over decades and discounts it back to today's money. The relative approach says
'comparable flats on this street sold for Rs X per square foot, so this one is worth about...'.
Professional valuers use both - the rental-yield view and the per-square-foot view - because
each catches what the other misses.

**Key terms**

- Intrinsic Value — What an asset is worth based on its own fundamentals - the present
value of its expected future cash flows.
- Relative Valuation — Valuing a stock by comparing its multiples (P/E, EV/EBITDA, etc.) to
those of comparable peers.
- Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) — An intrinsic method that discounts a company's projected
free cash flows (and a terminal value) to the present.
- Dividend Discount Model (DDM) — An intrinsic method that values a share as the
present value of its expected future dividends.
- Discount Rate — The rate used to bring future cash flows to present value; for equity it
reflects the cost of equity (often via CAPM).
- Terminal Value — The value of all cash flows beyond the explicit forecast period, usually
estimated with the Gordon growth formula.


### Formula — The Valuation Formula Bank (With Worked Numbers)

These formulas turn Chapter 10's numerical questions into mechanical drills. Most multiples
below are worked on ONE illustrative company so the relationships are visible; the
present-value primer, two-stage DDM and terminal-value lines use simple standalone figures
for clarity. Pay special attention to the r - g denominator in every DDM/terminal formula - it is
where tiny assumption changes cause huge value swings.

> Shared example. Surya Consumer Ltd, FY24: Market price Rs 600 | EPS Rs 40 | Last dividend
(D0) Rs 20 | Book value per share Rs 150 | 10 crore shares (Market cap Rs 6,000 cr) | EBITDA
Rs 500 cr | Total debt Rs 600 cr | Cash Rs 200 cr | Sales Rs 3,000 cr | expected EPS growth
12% | cost of equity (r) 11% | stable dividend growth (g) 5% | payout 50%. (Note: on dividends
alone the Gordon value is Rs 350, below the Rs 600 price - because 5% dividend growth does
not capture the 12% earnings growth the market is pricing. That tension is the whole 'which
method' debate.)
Present Value of a Future Cash Flow
> Formula: PV = CF / (1 + r)^n
> Example: Rs 100 received in 1 year at r = 11%: 100 / 1.11 = Rs 90.09
> Meaning: A rupee tomorrow is worth less than a rupee today; discounting quantifies exactly
how much less.

Gordon Growth Model (single-stage DDM)
> Formula: P0 = D1 / (r - g), where D1 = D0 x (1 + g)
> Example: D0 = Rs 20, g = 5%, r = 11%: D1 = 20 x 1.05 = Rs 21; P0 = 21 / (0.11 - 0.05) = 21 /
0.06 = Rs 350
> Meaning: Value of a share as the present value of a perpetually growing dividend. Only valid
when r > g.

Two-Stage DDM
> Formula: Value = PV of dividends in the high-growth phase + PV of the terminal value
> Example: D1 = Rs 10, D2 = Rs 11.50 (15% growth), then 5% forever, r = 11%. TV at end of yr2
= 11.50 x 1.05 / 0.06 = Rs 201.25. Value = 10/1.11 + (11.50 + 201.25)/1.11^2 = 9.01 +
172.67 = ~Rs 182
> Meaning: Used when a firm grows fast for a few years before settling to stable growth.

Terminal Value (Gordon method)
> Formula: TV = CF_final x (1 + g) / (r - g)
> Example: Final-year FCF Rs 100 cr, g = 5%, r = 11%: 100 x 1.05 / 0.06 = Rs 1,750 cr
> Meaning: Captures all cash flows beyond the forecast horizon; often 60-80% of a DCF's total
value, so g is critical.

Free Cash Flow to Firm (FCFF)
> Formula: FCFF = EBIT x (1 - tax) + Depreciation - Capex - Increase in Working Capital
> Example: EBIT 150, tax 25%, Dep 50, Capex 60, WC increase 20: 150 x 0.75 + 50 - 60 - 20 =
Rs 82.5 cr
> Meaning: Cash available to ALL capital providers (debt + equity); discounted at WACC to value
the whole firm.

Free Cash Flow to Equity (FCFE)
> Formula: FCFE = FCFF - Interest x (1 - tax) + Net Borrowing
> Example: FCFF 82.5, Interest 30, tax 25%, net borrowing 10: 82.5 - 30 x 0.75 + 10 = Rs 70 cr

> Meaning: Cash available to EQUITY holders only; discounted at the cost of equity to value the
equity directly.

Price-to-Earnings (P/E)
> Formula: P/E = Market Price per Share / EPS
> Example: 600 / 40 = 15 times
> Meaning: Rupees investors pay per rupee of annual earnings; the most-quoted multiple.

PEG Ratio
> Formula: PEG = P/E / EPS Growth Rate (in %)
> Example: 15 / 12 = 1.25
> Meaning: Growth-adjusted P/E. Around 1.0 is often seen as fair; it stops you calling a
high-growth stock 'expensive' on P/E alone.

Justified Leading P/E
> Formula: Justified P/E = Payout Ratio / (r - g)
> Example: Payout 50% (DPS 20 / EPS 40): 0.50 / (0.11 - 0.05) = 0.50 / 0.06 = 8.33 times
> Meaning: The P/E a stable firm 'deserves' given its payout, growth and risk; links the DDM to
the P/E multiple.

Price-to-Book (P/B)
> Formula: P/B = Market Price per Share / Book Value per Share
> Example: 600 / 150 = 4.0 times
> Meaning: Price relative to net asset value per share; the standard multiple for banks and
financials.

Price-to-Sales (P/S)
> Formula: P/S = Market Capitalisation / Sales
> Example: 6,000 / 3,000 = 2.0 times
> Meaning: Useful for loss-making or early-stage firms where earnings are not yet meaningful.

Enterprise Value (EV)
> Formula: EV = Market Capitalisation + Total Debt - Cash & Equivalents
> Example: 6,000 + 600 - 200 = Rs 6,400 cr
> Meaning: The cost to acquire the whole business; the numerator for capital-structure-neutral
multiples.

EV/EBITDA
> Formula: EV / EBITDA
> Example: 6,400 / 500 = 12.8 times
> Meaning: Capital-structure-neutral, so it compares leveraged and unleveraged firms fairly;
preferred for cross-company comparison.

EV/Sales
> Formula: EV / Sales
> Example: 6,400 / 3,000 = 2.13 times
> Meaning: An enterprise-level revenue multiple, useful when margins are temporarily
depressed or negative.

Dividend Yield
> Formula: Dividend per Share / Market Price per Share

> Example: 20 / 600 = 3.33%
> Meaning: The cash return from dividends alone; matters for income-oriented and mature
stocks.

### Deep dive — Getting Valuation Right: Method Fit, Discount Rates &

Traps
Match the method to the company
DDM fits mature, stable, dividend-paying firms - banks, utilities, established FMCG. DCF
(FCFF/FCFE) fits firms with predictable cash flows but irregular or no dividends. Relative
multiples fit quick comparison when peers are genuinely comparable in growth and risk.
Asset-based and price-to-book approaches fit financials and holding companies. Using the
wrong method - say, a DDM on a no-dividend growth firm - produces nonsense.

The discount rate is everything
Intrinsic values hinge on the r - g denominator. Because value = D1/(r - g), shrinking the gap
between the required return and growth inflates value dramatically and makes it
hyper-sensitive. The cost of equity is typically built with CAPM (risk-free rate + beta x equity
risk premium - see Chapter 12). Get r or g slightly wrong and the answer moves a lot:
garbage in, garbage out.

Terminal value dominates a DCF
In a typical DCF, 60-80% of the total value sits in the terminal value, so the perpetual growth
assumption carries enormous weight. A hard rule: terminal growth must not exceed long-run
nominal GDP growth - a company cannot outgrow the whole economy forever. Analysts who
assume an aggressive terminal g quietly manufacture the valuation they wanted.

Multiples have traps
P/E breaks for loss-making firms (negative earnings) and misleads for cyclicals at peak or
trough margins. EV/EBITDA is capital-structure-neutral, so it compares a debt-heavy and a

debt-light firm fairly where P/E cannot. Always compare like with like - same sector, similar
growth and risk - and use PEG to adjust P/E for growth before judging cheap or dear.


### Real-world references

- Banks and NBFCs are valued mainly on price-to-book and dividend/excess-return models,
not EV/EBITDA, because interest is their core business rather than a financing item.
- High-growth or loss-making firms are often valued on EV/Sales or DCF, since trailing P/E is
meaningless when earnings are negative.
- For cyclical commodity firms, a low trailing P/E at the top of the cycle can be a value trap,
because peak earnings are about to fall.


### Real-world example — Is a High P/E Really Expensive? Surya vs Its Peer

Context. Surya Consumer Ltd vs a slower-growing peer

Situation. Surya trades at a P/E of 15 while a peer in the same sector trades at a P/E of 12. A
beginner concludes Surya is the more expensive stock. Adjusting for growth tells a different
story.

Data. Surya: P/E 15, expected EPS growth 12% -> PEG = 15 / 12 = 1.25. Peer: P/E 12,
expected EPS growth 6% -> PEG = 12 / 6 = 2.00. On a pure dividend basis, Surya's Gordon
value (D0 Rs 20, g 5%, r 11%) is Rs 350, well below its Rs 600 price - confirming that
dividends alone do not capture its growth.

Analysis. Although Surya carries the higher P/E, its PEG of 1.25 is far lower than the peer's
2.00 - meaning investors pay less per unit of growth for Surya. The higher headline multiple
is justified by faster earnings growth, not by the stock being 'overpriced'. The DDM gap is the
clue that an income-style model is the wrong lens here; a growth-aware view (PEG, or a DCF
on rising cash flows) fits Surya far better.


### Analyst takeaway. — Never label a stock expensive on a single multiple or a single model.

Triangulate: adjust the multiple for growth (PEG), check whether the method even fits the
company, and cross-read intrinsic against relative. A higher P/E with much higher growth can
be the cheaper stock.


### Common mistakes — The Valuation Traps That Cost Marks

1. Mistake: Calling a high-P/E stock 'expensive' without considering growth.
Why it happens: A bigger P/E looks dearer at a glance.

Fix: Use PEG. A fast-growing firm can deserve a high P/E; a high-P/E, high-growth stock can be cheaper
than a low-P/E, low-growth peer.

2. Mistake: Applying the dividend discount model to a firm that pays little or no dividend.
Why it happens: The Gordon formula is easy, so students reach for it everywhere.

Fix: DDM only fits stable dividend payers. For low or no-payout firms use FCFE/DCF or relative
multiples.

3. Mistake: Comparing P/E across firms with very different capital structures.
Why it happens: P/E is the default multiple.

Fix: P/E is distorted by leverage. Use EV/EBITDA, which is capital-structure-neutral, when debt levels
differ.

4. Mistake: Assuming a terminal growth rate above long-run GDP.
Why it happens: A higher terminal g conveniently lifts the valuation.

Fix: Terminal growth must be at or below long-run nominal GDP growth - no company outgrows the
whole economy forever. Otherwise the value is manufactured.

5. Mistake: Treating a single DCF output as a precise, certain number.
Why it happens: A model produces one neat figure.

Fix: DCF is extremely sensitive to r and g. Treat the output as a range, run sensitivity, and sanity-check
it against market multiples.

## Session 11 — Fundamental Analysis of Commodities —

### Hook — The Paint Company Model That Ignored Crude Oil

A sharp analyst builds a flawless model for a paint company - and gets the margins
completely wrong, because she ignored that crude oil, the feedstock for paint, had just
jumped 40%. Commodities are not a separate world from equities; they are the raw materials
flowing UNDER thousands of listed companies. Crude oil sets a paint company's costs and an
airline's fuel bill; gold drives a jeweller's economics; palm oil moves an FMCG firm's margins;
steel and copper shape manufacturers and infrastructure players. Understanding what
moves commodity prices is part of understanding the companies that depend on them. The
revised exam gives this chapter real weight at 5 marks, and most equity-focused candidates
skim it - which makes it a reliable source of easy marks for anyone who does not.


> *Hook question. Why might an equity analyst who never trades commodities still need to*

### Concept — What Moves Commodity Prices: Demand, Supply and the

Dollar
Commodities are physical raw materials, split into two families. HARD commodities are
mined or extracted - metals (gold, silver, copper, aluminium) and energy (crude oil, natural
gas). SOFT commodities are grown - agricultural goods such as grains, oilseeds, sugar, cotton
and spices. At their core, commodity prices are driven by DEMAND and SUPPLY. Demand
rises with economic growth and industrial activity (more construction lifts copper and steel)
and with seasonal and consumption patterns. Supply depends on production - mining output,
the harvest, OPEC decisions for oil - and is disrupted by weather, geopolitics and the level of
inventories. But three macro forces matter enormously too. First, the US DOLLAR: most
commodities are priced in dollars, so a stronger dollar tends to push commodity prices down,
and for Indian buyers a weaker rupee raises import costs. Second, INTEREST RATES: higher
rates raise the cost of holding commodities and the opportunity cost of holding non-yielding
gold. Third, GOVERNMENT POLICY: import and export duties, minimum support prices and
stocking limits. Gold is special - it behaves as a safe-haven and inflation hedge rather than a
purely industrial input.


> **Analogy.** Think of a commodity's price like the water level in a tank with an inflow pipe

(supply: mines, harvests, oil wells) and an outflow tap (demand: factories and consumers).
When a harvest fails or OPEC cuts output, inflow drops and the level - the price - rises; when a
recession cuts demand, the tap closes and the level falls. The US dollar is like the size of the
measuring cup: when the dollar strengthens, the same commodity is measured as 'cheaper' in
dollar terms.

**Key terms**

- Hard Commodity — A mined or extracted commodity - metals (gold, copper) and energy
(crude oil, natural gas).
- Soft Commodity — A grown, agricultural commodity - grains, oilseeds, sugar, cotton,
spices.
- Demand-Supply Balance — The core driver of commodity prices; shortages lift prices,
surpluses depress them.
- US Dollar Linkage — Most commodities are priced in dollars, so a stronger dollar generally
pushes prices lower (an inverse relationship).

- Safe-Haven Asset — An asset like gold that investors buy in times of crisis as a store of
value.
- Inventories — Held stocks that buffer or amplify price moves; low inventories make prices
spike on any shock.


### Formula — The Commodity Formula Bank (With Worked Numbers)

Most of this chapter is conceptual, but a few relationships are reliably tested. The
cost-of-carry link between spot and futures, the contango-versus-backwardation distinction,
and the rupee import-cost calculation are the ones to know. The examples below thread a
single gold case where possible.

> Shared example. Threaded figures: gold spot Rs 60,000 per 10g with a cost of carry of Rs
600 (storage Rs 200 + interest Rs 500 - convenience yield Rs 100); a separate agri commodity
has 20 lakh tonnes of ending stock against 100 lakh tonnes of annual consumption; and crude
oil trades at USD 80 per barrel with the rupee at Rs 84 per US dollar.
Futures Price (Cost-of-Carry Model)
> Formula: Futures Price = Spot Price + Cost of Carry
> Example: Spot gold Rs 60,000 per 10g, cost of carry Rs 600: futures = about Rs 60,600.
> Meaning: In a normal market the futures price exceeds the spot by the cost of carrying the
commodity to delivery.

Cost of Carry
> Formula: Cost of Carry = Storage Costs + Financing (interest) Costs - Convenience Yield
> Example: Storage Rs 200 + interest Rs 500 - convenience yield Rs 100 = Rs 600.
> Meaning: The net cost of holding a physical commodity; the main reason a futures price differs
from the spot price.

Basis
> Formula: Basis = Spot Price - Futures Price
> Example: Spot Rs 60,000, futures Rs 60,600: basis = -Rs 600 (a negative basis indicates
contango).
> Meaning: Tracks the gap between cash and futures prices; it narrows to zero as the contract
nears expiry.

Contango vs Backwardation
> Formula: Contango: Futures > Spot. Backwardation: Futures < Spot.
> Example: Futures Rs 60,600 above spot Rs 60,000 = contango. If futures were Rs 59,500
(below spot) it would be backwardation.
> Meaning: Contango is the normal positive-carry state; backwardation signals near-term supply
tightness or strong immediate demand.

Stock-to-Use Ratio
> Formula: Stock-to-Use Ratio = Ending Inventory / Annual Consumption x 100
> Example: Ending stock 20 lakh tonnes, annual consumption 100 lakh tonnes: 20 / 100 = 20%.
> Meaning: A key supply gauge, especially for agri commodities; a low ratio means tight supply
and price support.

Rupee Import Cost
> Formula: Import Cost (Rs) = International Price (USD) x Exchange Rate (Rs per USD), before
duties

> Example: Crude at USD 80 per barrel, exchange rate Rs 84 per USD: 80 x 84 = Rs 6,720 per
barrel.
> Meaning: Shows how a weaker rupee directly raises the cost of imported commodities like
crude oil, gold and edible oil.

### Deep dive — Demand-Supply, Macro Links, Futures and India's

Exchanges
Demand, supply and inventories
Demand tracks industrial activity and the economic cycle - base metals and energy are
cyclical - plus seasonal and consumption patterns. Supply depends on production (mines,
harvests, OPEC) and is hit by weather (the monsoon for agri), strikes and geopolitics.
Inventories, captured by the stock-to-use ratio, buffer or amplify moves: when inventories are
low, any supply shock sends prices spiking.

The macro linkages every analyst must know
Most commodities are priced in dollars, so a stronger dollar generally pressures prices, while
for India a weaker rupee raises the import cost of crude, gold and edible oils. Higher interest
rates increase carrying costs and the opportunity cost of holding non-yielding gold. Gold's
dual role as a safe haven and inflation hedge means it often rises during crises and when real
interest rates fall.

Spot, futures, contango and backwardation
The spot price is the cash price for immediate delivery; the futures price reflects the spot plus
the cost of carry (storage plus financing, less convenience yield). When futures trade above
spot the market is in contango (the normal carry state); when futures trade below spot it is in
backwardation, which signals near-term supply tightness or strong immediate demand.

India's commodity exchanges and regulation
MCX (the Multi Commodity Exchange) is the main venue for metals, energy and bullion;
NCDEX (the National Commodity and Derivatives Exchange) is the main agri venue. Since
2015, commodity derivatives have been regulated by SEBI, following the merger of the

Forward Markets Commission into SEBI. Government policy - minimum support prices, import
and export duties, and stocking limits - heavily shapes agricultural commodities.


### Real-world references

- India imports most of its crude oil and a large share of its gold and edible oil, so the rupee
and global prices feed directly into import costs and domestic inflation.
- MCX is the dominant Indian exchange for bullion, base metals and energy, while NCDEX is
the main exchange for agricultural commodities.
- Commodity derivatives in India have been regulated by SEBI since 2015, after the Forward
Markets Commission was merged into it.


### Real-world example — One Commodity Move, Many Sectors: A Crude Oil

Spike
Context. An illustrative crude-oil shock traced through equities

Situation. Crude oil jumps from USD 80 to USD 110 a barrel. An equity analyst, rather than
trading oil, traces what the move means across listed sectors.

Data. Paint and tyre companies, which use crude derivatives as feedstock, face higher input
costs and margin pressure. Airlines see their fuel bills jump, squeezing margins. Oil
marketing companies' margins depend on whether they can pass the rise on to consumers.
Because India imports most of its crude, the rupee tends to weaken and the import bill and
inflation rise - which can push the RBI toward tighter policy (the link back to economic
analysis).

Analysis. A single commodity move ripples through multiple sectors at once, and not all in
the same direction: upstream oil producers may benefit while downstream users suffer. The
analyst who tracks crude can anticipate the margin hit to paints, tyres and airlines, the
pass-through question for oil marketers, and the second-round macro effects on the rupee,
inflation and interest rates. Gold, by contrast, would tend to rise in such a risk-off, inflationary
environment, reflecting its safe-haven role.


### Analyst takeaway. — Commodities are an input variable in equity research. Knowing the

direction of crude, gold or a key metal tells you which sectors face a cost headwind and
which enjoy a tailwind - which is why this 'commodities' chapter is really part of company and
sector analysis.

### Hook — Same Return, One Was the Better Bet

Two funds both delivered 15% last year. A beginner calls them equal. But Fund A got there on
a smooth, steady climb, while Fund B lurched violently up and down - at one point nearly
halving - before clawing back to the same finish. Identical return, wildly different
stomach-churn. And Fund A is clearly the better investment, because it earned the same
reward with far less risk. This is the single deepest idea in finance: a return on its own tells
you almost nothing. What matters is the return PER UNIT OF RISK you took to earn it. An
investor who chases returns while ignoring risk is the one who eventually blows up. On the
exam this chapter is worth 7 marks and is heavily numerical - you will compute returns,
measure volatility with standard deviation, gauge market sensitivity with beta, and price risk
with the CAPM.


> *Hook question. If two investments both returned 15% last year, what extra number do you*

### Concept — Two Kinds of Risk (and Why Only One Pays)

Risk comes in two flavours, and the distinction is the heart of this chapter. Systematic risk -
also called market risk or non-diversifiable risk - affects the entire market at once:
interest-rate moves, inflation, recessions, wars. You cannot diversify it away, because it hits
everything you could own. Unsystematic risk - specific or diversifiable risk - is company- or
industry-specific: a factory fire, a fraud, a product recall. You CAN diversify it away simply by
holding many different stocks, where one company's bad news is offset by another's good
news. The crucial consequence: the market rewards you only for bearing systematic risk
(which is unavoidable), not unsystematic risk (which you could have eliminated for free). That
is why beta, a measure of systematic risk, drives expected return, while standard deviation
measures TOTAL risk (both kinds). In short: total risk = systematic risk + unsystematic risk.


> **Analogy.** Think of risk as the chance of getting wet. Unsystematic risk is rain falling on your

particular street - just move to another street (diversify) and you stay dry. Systematic risk is a
region-wide monsoon - whichever street you move to, you still get soaked, so you demand
compensation (a higher return) for living in that region at all. The market pays you for
enduring the monsoon, never for standing in a puddle you could have walked around.

**Key terms**

- Systematic Risk — Market-wide, non-diversifiable risk (interest rates, inflation, recession)
measured by beta and rewarded with higher return.
- Unsystematic Risk — Company- or industry-specific risk that can be diversified away and
is therefore not rewarded.
- Diversification — Holding many different assets so company-specific shocks cancel out,
reducing unsystematic risk.
- Beta — A measure of a stock's sensitivity to market movements - its systematic risk.
- Standard Deviation — A measure of total risk - how widely returns vary around their
average.
- Risk-Return Tradeoff — The principle that higher expected return requires accepting
higher risk.


### Formula — The Risk and Return Formula Bank (With Worked Numbers)


This chapter is mostly arithmetic once you know the formulas. Drill the return measures
(HPR, CAGR, expected return), the CAPM, and the Sharpe and Treynor ratios - those are the
ones most likely to appear as calculations. The CAPM example threads through ERP, CAPM
and alpha so you can see how they connect.

> Shared example. Threaded figures: an investor buys at Rs 200, sells at Rs 230 with a Rs 10
dividend; a separate holding grows from Rs 100 to Rs 133.10 over 3 years; CAPM inputs are
risk-free rate 7%, beta 1.2, expected market return 13%; and a portfolio returns 16% with a
standard deviation of 18%.
Holding Period Return (HPR)
> Formula: HPR = (End Value - Begin Value + Income) / Begin Value
> Example: Bought at Rs 200, sold at Rs 230, dividend Rs 10: (230 - 200 + 10) / 200 = 40 / 200
= 20%
> Meaning: Total return over the holding period, capturing both price change and income.

Compound Annual Growth Rate (CAGR)
> Formula: CAGR = (End Value / Begin Value)^(1/n) - 1
> Example: Rs 100 grows to Rs 133.10 over 3 years: (133.10/100)^(1/3) - 1 = (1.331)^(1/3) - 1
= 1.10 - 1 = 10%
> Meaning: The annual rate that compounds the start value to the end value; the right measure
for multi-year, compounded returns.

Expected Return
> Formula: Expected Return = Sum of [Probability x Return] across scenarios
> Example: 30% chance of 20%, 50% chance of 10%, 20% chance of -5%: 0.3(20) + 0.5(10) +
0.2(-5) = 6 + 5 - 1 = 10%
> Meaning: The probability-weighted average of possible returns; the forward-looking estimate
of return.

Variance and Standard Deviation
> Formula: Variance = average of (Return - Mean Return)^2; Standard Deviation = square root
of Variance
> Example: Returns of 8% and 12%: mean = 10%; variance = [(8-10)^2 + (12-10)^2]/2 = (4 +
4)/2 = 4; SD = sqrt(4) = 2%
> Meaning: Standard deviation measures total risk - how far returns scatter around the average.
Higher SD means more volatility.

Beta
> Formula: Beta = Covariance(stock, market) / Variance(market)
> Example: Interpretation: beta = 1 moves with the market; beta > 1 amplifies market moves
(e.g., a beta of 1.5 tends to rise ~15% when the market rises 10%); beta < 1 dampens them;
beta < 0 moves opposite.
> Meaning: Measures systematic (market) risk - the only risk the market pays for. It is the risk
input in the CAPM.

Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM)
> Formula: Required Return = Rf + Beta x (Rm - Rf)
> Example: Rf = 7%, beta = 1.2, Rm = 13%: 7 + 1.2 x (13 - 7) = 7 + 7.2 = 14.2%
> Meaning: The return an investor should require for a stock's systematic risk; also used as the
cost of equity in valuation (Chapter 10).

Equity Risk Premium (ERP)

> Formula: ERP = Rm - Rf
> Example: Rm = 13%, Rf = 7%: 13 - 7 = 6%
> Meaning: The extra return investors demand for holding equities over the risk-free asset; the
slope input in CAPM.

Sharpe Ratio
> Formula: Sharpe = (Portfolio Return - Rf) / Standard Deviation
> Example: Return 16%, Rf 7%, SD 18%: (16 - 7) / 18 = 9 / 18 = 0.5
> Meaning: Excess return per unit of TOTAL risk. Higher is better; use it to compare standalone
portfolios.

Treynor Ratio
> Formula: Treynor = (Portfolio Return - Rf) / Beta
> Example: Return 16%, Rf 7%, beta 1.2: (16 - 7) / 1.2 = 9 / 1.2 = 7.5
> Meaning: Excess return per unit of SYSTEMATIC risk (beta). Use it when the portfolio is part of
a larger diversified holding.

Jensen's Alpha
> Formula: Alpha = Actual Return - CAPM Expected Return
> Example: Actual 16%, CAPM expected 14.2%: 16 - 14.2 = +1.8%
> Meaning: Outperformance over what CAPM predicted for the risk taken. Positive alpha means
the manager added value.

### Deep dive — Diversification, Beta, CAPM & Risk-Adjusted Returns

Why diversification works

Across many stocks, company-specific surprises cancel out - one firm's bad news offsets
another's good news - so unsystematic risk shrinks. What remains is systematic risk, which
moves everything together. Correlation is key: combining assets with low or negative
correlation cuts portfolio risk the most. The benefit fades after roughly 15 to 30 well-chosen
stocks; beyond that, little additional risk is removed.

Beta decoded
Beta measures how strongly a stock moves with the market. A beta of 1 moves in line with
the market; above 1 amplifies market swings (aggressive, typical of cyclicals and small caps);
below 1 dampens them (defensive, typical of FMCG and utilities); a negative beta moves
opposite to the market. Because beta captures the non-diversifiable part of risk, it is the risk
that gets priced.

CAPM and the cost of equity
CAPM gives the return an investor should require for a stock's systematic risk: Rf + beta x
(Rm - Rf). The same figure serves as the cost of equity in valuation, linking this chapter to
Chapter 10. Plotted as the Security Market Line, stocks above the line offer more return than
their risk warrants (undervalued), while those below offer too little (overvalued).

Risk-adjusted performance
Raw return flatters risky funds. The Sharpe ratio measures excess return per unit of total risk
(standard deviation); the Treynor ratio measures it per unit of systematic risk (beta); Jensen's
alpha measures outperformance versus CAPM. A fund with a high headline return but huge
volatility can have a worse Sharpe ratio than a steadier fund - which is the fairer comparison.


### Real-world references

- High-beta segments such as cyclicals and small caps tend to swing more than the market,
while defensives like FMCG and utilities carry low betas.
- Equity research routinely uses a CAPM-derived cost of equity as the discount rate when
valuing companies.
- Mutual fund comparisons in India frequently report the Sharpe ratio so investors can judge
return per unit of risk rather than raw return alone.


### Real-world example — Higher Return Is Not Always the Better Fund

Context. Two funds compared on a risk-adjusted basis

Situation. Fund A returned 16% last year; Fund B returned 13%. On headline return, A looks
like the obvious winner. Adjusting for the risk each took tells a different story. The risk-free
rate is 7%.

Data. Fund A: return 16%, standard deviation 18% -> Sharpe = (16 - 7)/18 = 0.50. Fund B:
return 13%, standard deviation 10% -> Sharpe = (13 - 7)/10 = 0.60. A check on Fund A
against CAPM (Rf 7%, beta 1.2, Rm 13%) gives an expected 14.2%, so A did beat its required
return, earning a positive alpha of about 1.8%.

Analysis. Despite the lower headline return, Fund B delivered MORE return per unit of risk -
a Sharpe ratio of 0.60 against A's 0.50 - because it achieved its result with far less volatility.
Fund A is not bad (it even cleared its CAPM hurdle), but on a like-for-like, risk-adjusted basis,
Fund B is the superior performer. The higher raw number was hiding the higher risk taken to
earn it.


### Analyst takeaway. — The investment with the bigger return is not automatically the better

one. Risk-adjusted measures like Sharpe and Treynor reveal who actually delivered more
reward per unit of risk - which is the comparison that matters.


### Common mistakes — The Risk-and-Return Traps That Lose Marks

1. Mistake: Judging an investment by its return alone.
Why it happens: Return is the number everyone quotes.

Fix: Always look at return PER UNIT of risk (Sharpe or Treynor). The same return earned with less risk is
the better investment.

2. Mistake: Confusing systematic and unsystematic risk.
Why it happens: Both are just called 'risk'.

Fix: Systematic risk is market-wide and non-diversifiable (priced via beta); unsystematic risk is
company-specific and diversifiable (not rewarded).

3. Mistake: Believing diversification eliminates ALL risk.
Why it happens: Diversification is sold as the free lunch of investing.

Fix: It removes only unsystematic risk. Systematic (market) risk always remains - you cannot diversify
away a recession.

4. Mistake: Using the arithmetic mean instead of CAGR for multi-year returns.
Why it happens: A simple average is easier to compute.

Fix: For compounded multi-period returns, use CAGR (the geometric mean). The arithmetic mean
overstates actual growth, especially when returns are volatile.

5. Mistake: Mixing up the Sharpe and Treynor ratios.
Why it happens: Both divide excess return by a risk measure.

Fix: Sharpe divides by total risk (standard deviation); Treynor divides by systematic risk (beta). Use
Sharpe for a standalone portfolio and Treynor when it is part of a larger diversified holding.

Key takeaways
- Return measures include holding period return, CAGR (the compounded annual rate) and
probability-weighted expected return.
- Total risk (standard deviation) splits into systematic risk (market-wide, measured by beta,
and priced) and unsystematic risk (company-specific and diversifiable).
- Diversification removes only unsystematic risk; the systematic, market-wide floor always
remains.
- CAPM prices systematic risk as Rf + beta x (Rm - Rf) and doubles as the cost of equity used
in valuation.
- Risk-adjusted measures compare investments fairly: Sharpe uses total risk, Treynor uses
beta, and Jensen's alpha measures outperformance versus CAPM.
What you can now do. You can compute holding-period and annualised (CAGR) returns,
expected return and standard deviation, distinguish systematic from unsystematic risk, apply
CAPM to find a required return, and compute the Sharpe and Treynor ratios - exactly the
numerical toolkit this chapter tests.

- B) Move opposite to the market
- C) Move broadly in line with the market ✓
- D) Have no market risk

> Answer: C. A beta of 1.0 means the stock tends to move in line with the market. A beta above 1

**Q4. (medium) The risk-free rate is 7%, a stock's beta is 1.2, and the expected**

- A) 7.0%
- B) 13.0%
- C) 16.2%
- D) 14.2% ✓

> Answer: D. CAPM: Required return = Rf + beta x (Rm - Rf) = 7 + 1.2 x (13 - 7) = 7 + 7.2 = 14.2%.


**Q5. (medium) A stock has a 40% chance of returning 15% and a 60% chance of**
returning 5%. Its expected return is:
- A) 9% ✓
- B) 10%
- C) 20%
- D) 11%

> Answer: A. Expected return = sum of probability x return = 0.4(15) + 0.6(5) = 6 + 3 = 9%.


**Q6. (medium) Diversification across many stocks primarily reduces:**
- A) Systematic risk
- B) Unsystematic (company-specific) risk ✓
- C) Market risk
- D) Interest-rate risk

> Answer: B. Diversification reduces unsystematic, company-specific risk because individual shocks

offset one another. Systematic (market) risk cannot be diversified away.


**Q7. (hard) A portfolio returns 16%, the risk-free rate is 7%, and the portfolio's**

- A) 0.90
- B) 1.80
- C) 0.50 ✓
- D) 0.39

> Answer: C. Sharpe ratio = (Portfolio return - Rf) / Standard deviation = (16 - 7) / 18 = 9 / 18 = 0.5.


**Q8. (hard) An investment grows from Rs 100 to Rs 133.10 over three years. Its**
compound annual growth rate (CAGR) is:
- A) 33.1%
- B) 11.0%
- C) 9.0%
- D) 10.0% ✓

> Answer: D. CAGR = (End / Begin)^(1/n) - 1 = (133.10/100)^(1/3) - 1 = (1.331)^(1/3) - 1 = 1.10 - 1 =

10%.

- B) Fund A is better, because its return is higher

- D) It cannot be determined

> Answer: A. Sharpe A = (16 - 7)/18 = 0.5; Sharpe B = (13 - 7)/10 = 0.6. Despite the lower headline

- B) Systematic risk (beta) ✓
- C) Unsystematic risk

> Answer: B. The Treynor ratio divides excess return by beta (systematic risk), whereas the Sharpe ratio

## Session 13 — Qualities of a Good Research Report —

Structure, Objectivity, Disclosures
*Exam weight: 5 marks*


### Hook — Buy This Stock! - and Why That Message Is Worthless

A message like 'Buy this stock, it will double!' floods every investor's phone. It is a tip, and it
is worthless - because it shows no reasoning, no evidence, and hides whether the sender
even owns the stock. A genuine research report is its opposite: it states a clear
recommendation AND shows its entire working - the thesis, the financial analysis, the
valuation method, the time horizon, the key RISKS that could prove it wrong, and full
disclosure of the analyst's own interests. A report invites you to challenge it; a tip asks only
for blind faith. This chapter defines what makes research credible and professional - the
structure, the objectivity, and the disclosures - and on the exam it is worth 5 marks of mostly
straightforward recall.


> *Hook question. What turns 'buy this stock' from a worthless tip into a credible research*

recommendation you could actually act on?


### Concept — What Goes Into a Research Report

A research report communicates an analyst's investment opinion together with the reasoning
behind it, and a complete report has a recognisable structure. At the top sit the company
name, the date, the analyst name, the RECOMMENDATION (Buy, Hold or Sell), the TARGET
PRICE, the current market price, and the time horizon (often 12 months). The body then
builds the case: the investment thesis (the core argument in a few lines), a company and
business overview, the financial analysis and projections, the VALUATION with the method
used (DDM, multiples or DCF), the sector and industry context, and - critically - the KEY RISKS
that could prove the thesis wrong. At the end come the mandatory DISCLOSURES (the
analyst's holdings, interests and relationships) and the disclaimer. The rating system maps
the view to an action: Buy, Hold or Sell (some firms use Outperform, Neutral and
Underperform, or Overweight and Underweight), and the target price implies the expected
upside or downside over the horizon. A target price without a horizon and a rationale is
meaningless.


> **Analogy.** A research report is like a doctor's full diagnosis, not a one-word verdict. A good

doctor does not just say 'you are unwell' - they state the diagnosis (the recommendation),
explain the symptoms and tests that led there (the analysis), give a prognosis with a
timeframe (the target price and horizon), warn you what could go wrong (the risks), and
disclose any conflict (the disclosures). A tip is the stranger on the street who says 'you look ill'
and walks off.

**Key terms**

- Research Report — A written analysis that communicates an investment recommendation
along with its supporting reasoning.
- Recommendation (Buy/Hold/Sell) — The analyst's actionable opinion on a security; some
firms use Outperform/Neutral/Underperform equivalents.
- Target Price — The price the analyst expects over the stated horizon, implying the upside
or downside from the current price.
- Investment Thesis — The core argument, in a few lines, for why the recommendation
holds.
- Time Horizon — The period over which the recommendation and target apply, commonly
12 months.

- Rating System — The scale that maps the analyst's view to an action (Buy/Hold/Sell or
equivalents).


### Concept — What Makes a Report GOOD: Objectivity, Balance and

Disclosure
Having the right sections is not enough - quality is what makes a report trustworthy. The
hallmarks of a good research report are: objectivity and independence (an honest, unbiased
view, NOT a sales pitch for the company or the analyst's own position); balance (it presents
the RISKS and the bear case, not just the positives - a report that only lists reasons to buy is
propaganda); clarity and conciseness (the argument is easy to follow and not padded);
evidence-based reasoning (every claim is supported by data, not mere assertion);
transparency (the assumptions and valuation methodology are stated so the reader can
judge and challenge them); actionability (a clear recommendation and target the reader can
act on); and timeliness (issued while the information is still relevant). Underpinning all of this
is DISCLOSURE: the report must reveal the analyst's holdings, financial interests,
compensation, and any relationship with the subject company - as required by SEBI's
Research Analyst Regulations (the legal chapter) - so the reader can weigh the analyst's
independence. Disclosure is what lets a reader trust, or appropriately discount, the opinion.


> **Analogy.** If the structure is the skeleton of a report, objectivity and disclosure are its

conscience. Think of a referee in a match: the referee must not only know the rules (structure)
but be visibly IMPARTIAL (objectivity) and declare any connection to a team (disclosure). A
referee who secretly bet on one side, or who only ever blew the whistle one way, is worthless -
no matter how well they know the rulebook.

**Key terms**

- Objectivity — An unbiased, evidence-based view free from the influence of the analyst's
interests.
- Independence — Freedom from pressures (banking ties, holdings, management access)
that could distort the view.
- Balanced View — Presenting the key risks and the bear case alongside the positives.
- Risk Disclosure — A clear statement of the key risks that could cause the thesis or target
to fail.
- Transparency — Stating the assumptions and valuation methodology so the reader can
evaluate them.
- Conflict-of-Interest Disclosure — Revealing holdings, interests and relationships so the
reader can judge the analyst's independence.

### Deep dive — The Sections That Matter, Objectivity and the Disclosure

Link
The non-negotiable sections
Four elements form the backbone: the recommendation plus target plus horizon (the
actionable core); the thesis (the argument); the valuation WITH its methodology (so it can be
checked); and the KEY RISKS (the balance). A report that omits the risks or hides the
valuation method is incomplete, however polished it looks.

Objectivity and the conflict problem
Research can be skewed by the analyst's own holdings, the firm's banking relationships, or
pressure to keep management access - a particular issue on the sell-side. A good report
resists this: it is willing to issue a Sell or a Hold, presents the bear case fairly, and discloses
every conflict so the reader can judge for themselves.

Rating systems and target prices
The rating (Buy/Hold/Sell or equivalents) maps the view to an action; the target price implies
the expected upside or downside; the horizon frames it. Ratings should be applied
consistently and revised when the facts change. A 'Buy' with no target, no horizon and no
rationale is not research - it is a tip.

Disclosures and the regulatory link
SEBI's Research Analyst Regulations (the legal chapter) make specific disclosures mandatory
- the analyst's holdings, interests, relationships and any role in offerings - plus a disclaimer
that the report is not tailored advice. Disclosure does not remove a conflict, but it lets the
reader weigh it. This is the bridge between report QUALITY and regulatory COMPLIANCE.


### Real-world references

- Indian sell-side reports carry standardised rating scales, target prices, dedicated risk
sections and lengthy disclosure pages, as required under SEBI's rules.
- A 12-month target price is a common convention in equity research, framing the horizon
over which the recommendation is expected to play out.
- Regulated research reports differ sharply from the unregulated stock tips circulated by
unregistered 'finfluencers', which carry no reasoning, risks or disclosures.


### Real-world example — Same Stock: A Credible Report vs a Worthless Tip

Context. An illustrative contrast on one stock

Situation. Two people give a view on the same stock, currently at Rs 410. One sends a tip;
the other writes a research report. Comparing them shows exactly what makes research
credible.

Data. The TIP reads in full: 'Buy XYZ, target Rs 500, it will fly!' - no reasoning, no risks, no
disclosure of whether the sender owns it. The REPORT states 'Buy, 12-month target Rs 500
(about 22% upside from Rs 410)', then lays out the thesis (margin expansion and

market-share gains), shows the financial projections, names the valuation method (15 times
FY26 earnings), lists the key risks (input-cost inflation, a new competitor, a possible
regulatory change), and discloses that the analyst holds no position and the firm has no
banking relationship with the company.

Analysis. Both end at the same target, but only the report is actionable. The tip asks for
blind faith; the report shows its reasoning, states the basis for the target, fairly presents what
could go wrong, and discloses the analyst's interests so the reader can judge independence.
A reader can challenge the report's assumptions; the tip can only be trusted or ignored.


### Analyst takeaway. — The recommendation is the LEAST important part of a report. What

makes research valuable is the reasoning, the risks and the disclosure that let a reader
independently decide whether to trust the call.


### Common mistakes — The Research-Report Traps That Lose Marks

1. Mistake: Thinking a report is good simply because it has a clear 'Buy'.
Why it happens: The recommendation is the most visible part.

Fix: The recommendation is only credible WITH a thesis, valuation, risks and disclosure. A bare call is a
tip, not research.

2. Mistake: Confusing objectivity with having no opinion.
Why it happens: 'Unbiased' is misread as 'neutral'.

Fix: Objectivity means an unbiased, evidence-based opinion, which can be a strong Buy or Sell. It is not
the absence of a view; a good report takes a clear, defensible stance.

3. Mistake: Treating a report that only lists positives as thorough.
Why it happens: A long list of positives looks comprehensive.

Fix: A credible report is BALANCED - it must present the key risks and the bear case. An all-positive
report is a sales pitch.

4. Mistake: Believing a target price alone is meaningful.
Why it happens: The target is a concrete-looking number.

Fix: A target needs a TIME HORIZON and a RATIONALE (the valuation method). 'Target Rs 500' with no
horizon or basis is meaningless.

5. Mistake: Assuming disclosure removes a conflict of interest.
Why it happens: Disclosure feels like it 'clears' the issue.

Fix: Disclosure does not eliminate a conflict - it lets the READER weigh it. The analyst must still strive
for objectivity; disclosure is transparency, not a free pass.

## Session 14 — Legal & Regulatory Environment — SEBI

(RA) Regulations
*Exam weight: 10 marks*


### Hook — The 'Boring' Chapter That Quietly Decides Your Result

Picture an analyst who quietly buys a stock, then publishes a glowing 'Buy' report to
thousands of clients. The price jumps. She sells into the rally and pockets the gain. The
clients who acted on her report never knew it was a pump for her own position. That single
scenario is exactly what the SEBI (Research Analysts) Regulations exist to prevent - because
an analyst who can move a price with words holds real power, and power without disclosure
is just fraud with a research logo on it. Now the exam angle, and it is a gift: this chapter is
worth 10 marks, the second-highest on the entire paper, and it contains no formulas and no
judgement calls. It is pure recall. Every mark here is yours for the taking if you simply
memorise the rules - which is why strong candidates treat the 'boring' legal chapter as one of
the highest-yield blocks on the paper.


> *Hook question. If an analyst can move a stock's price just by publishing an opinion, what*

stops them from front-running their own reports - and which rule makes sure you would find
out if they tried?


### Concept — The RA Regulations: Who Registers, and the New Rules

The SEBI (Research Analysts) Regulations, 2014 govern anyone who prepares or issues
research reports or recommendations to the public. Two requirements sit at the gate:
registration with SEBI, and the NISM Series XV certification. Note who registers - the research
analyst or research ENTITY registers; individuals merely EMPLOYED by a registered entity do
not register separately, but they must hold the NISM certification and comply. Registration is
now administered through the RAASB (Research Analyst Administration and Supervision
Body), run by BSE. The framework was significantly amended in December 2024
(operationalised through SEBI's January 2025 guidelines), and the revised exam tests these
changes: (1) the qualification bar was eased - broadly a graduate degree now suffices and
the prior experience requirement was removed, with existing RAs grandfathered (they still
need NISM certification); (2) the old NET WORTH requirement was replaced by a DEPOSIT,
kept with a scheduled bank and marked as a lien in favour of RAASB/BSE, scaled by the
number of clients; (3) dual registration as both an Investment Adviser and a Research Analyst
is now allowed; (4) part-time RAs can register; (5) use of artificial intelligence in research
must be disclosed (the RA stays responsible); (6) client-level segregation of research and
distribution is required.


> **Analogy.** Think of it like a licence to move markets with your words. The NISM exam is the

driving test, SEBI registration is the licence itself, the deposit (which replaced net worth) is the
security you must post, and RAASB/BSE is the licensing authority that supervises you on the
road. Pass the test, get the licence, post the deposit, follow the supervisor.

**Key terms**

- SEBI (Research Analysts) Regulations, 2014 — The framework governing the
registration and conduct of research analysts in India.
- Research Analyst / Research Entity — The person or firm that prepares or issues
research reports to the public and must register; mere employees of a registered entity
need not register separately.
- NISM Series XV Certification — The mandatory certification (this very exam) that every
research analyst must hold.

- RAASB — Research Analyst Administration and Supervision Body, administered by BSE,
which administers and supervises RAs on SEBI's behalf.
- Deposit Requirement — The 2024 amendment replaced net worth with a deposit, held in
a scheduled bank, lien-marked to RAASB/BSE and scaled by client numbers.
- Dual Registration — An individual or firm may now be registered as both an Investment
Adviser and a Research Analyst, subject to conditions and client segregation.


### Concept — Code of Conduct, Disclosures & the Rules That Bite

Once registered, an RA lives under a strict code of conduct and a disclosure regime. The code
of conduct demands honesty, good faith, professional diligence, independence and
confidentiality, and the avoidance of conflicts of interest. Every research report must carry
mandatory disclosures: whether the analyst or their relatives hold a position in the subject
company, any financial interest, compensation or business relationship with that company,
whether the analyst's entity managed a public offering of it, and whether AI was used. There
are also trading restrictions: an RA must NOT trade contrary to their own recommendation
and is subject to blackout/restriction periods around publication. Beyond the RA Regulations,
two sibling regulations bind every analyst. The SEBI (Prohibition of Insider Trading)
Regulations, 2015 (PIT) prohibit trading on - or communicating - unpublished price-sensitive
information (UPSI); analysts are especially exposed because they often receive UPSI in
management meetings. The SEBI (Prohibition of Fraudulent and Unfair Trade Practices)
Regulations, 2003 (PFUTP) prohibit market manipulation, price rigging and misleading
statements. Investor grievances are handled through SCORES (SEBI's complaint system) and
the SMART online dispute resolution (ODR) mechanism.


> **Analogy.** If registration is the licence, the code of conduct is the traffic law you must obey

daily. Disclosures are the indicators you are required to signal; the trading restrictions are the
speed limits; and PIT and PFUTP are the laws against drunk and dangerous driving. SEBI is the
traffic police - with the power to fine, suspend or cancel your licence.

**Key terms**

- Code of Conduct — RA obligations of honesty, diligence, independence, confidentiality and
avoidance of conflicts of interest.
- Mandatory Disclosures — Conflict-of-interest disclosures in every report: holdings,
financial interest, business relationships, role in offerings, and AI use.
- Trading Restrictions — An RA must not trade against their own recommendation and
faces blackout periods around publication.
- Insider Trading (PIT, 2015) — Prohibits trading on or communicating unpublished
price-sensitive information (UPSI).
- UPSI — Unpublished Price-Sensitive Information - non-public information likely to materially
affect a security's price.
- PFUTP (2003) — Prohibition of Fraudulent and Unfair Trade Practices - bans manipulation,
price rigging and misleading statements; SCORES and ODR handle grievances.

### Deep dive — From Registration to Enforcement: The Full Regime

Registration and eligibility after the amendment
The research entity registers, not its individual employees - though all must be
NISM-certified. The December 2024 amendment eased the qualification bar (a graduate
degree broadly suffices, the experience requirement was removed) while grandfathering
existing RAs, who still need NISM certification. It replaced net worth with a deposit
(lien-marked to RAASB/BSE, scaled by client count), allowed dual registration as IA and RA,
and permitted part-time RAs. RAASB, administered by BSE, supervises the profession on
SEBI's behalf.

The disclosure regime
Disclosure is the heart of the framework. Each report must reveal whether the analyst or
relatives hold positions in the subject company, any financial interest, compensation or
business relationship, whether the analyst's entity managed an offering of the company, and
whether AI was used in the research. The logic is simple: the reader cannot judge an
opinion's independence without knowing the analyst's stake in it.

Insider trading (PIT, 2015) in brief
An 'insider' is a connected person or anyone in possession of unpublished price-sensitive
information (UPSI). PIT prohibits trading while in possession of UPSI and communicating UPSI
to others. Research analysts are particularly exposed because management interactions can
hand them UPSI - so they must be careful not to trade or leak it, and entities use trading
windows and codes of conduct to manage this.

PFUTP and enforcement
The PFUTP Regulations, 2003 prohibit fraud, market manipulation, price rigging and
misleading statements - the legal basis for action against pump-and-dump schemes. SEBI's
enforcement powers include inspection, investigation, monetary penalties and suspension or
cancellation of registration. Investors escalate grievances through SCORES and the online
dispute resolution (ODR) mechanism.


### Real-world references

- RAASB, administered by BSE, is the body through which research analysts are now
registered, administered and supervised on SEBI's behalf.
- SEBI has in recent years acted against unregistered 'finfluencers' and analysts who
recommended securities while holding undisclosed positions, underscoring why the
disclosure rules exist.
- The 2024 amendment deliberately aligned the rigour of RA regulation - qualification,
deposits, disclosures, client segregation - more closely with the regime already applied to

Investment Advisers.


### Real-world example — Owning a Stock You Cover: The Right Way and

the Wrong Way
Context. An illustrative research-analyst compliance scenario

Situation. An analyst personally owns shares in a company and now wants to publish a 'Buy'
recommendation on it. Owning the stock is not, by itself, prohibited - but how she handles it
decides whether she is compliant or in serious trouble.

Data. The compliant path: she prominently DISCLOSES her holding (and any relatives'
holdings) in the report, discloses any other financial interest or relationship with the
company, does NOT trade contrary to her recommendation, and respects the blackout period
around publication. The non-compliant path: she HIDES her holding, the report pushes the
price up, and she quietly sells into the rally she helped create.

Analysis. The same starting fact - owning a covered stock - leads to opposite outcomes. The
disclosed path satisfies the RA code of conduct and disclosure norms. The hidden path
breaches those disclosure and conduct requirements, and using a research report to inflate a
position one then sells can amount to a fraudulent and unfair trade practice under PFUTP. If
she also acted on unpublished price-sensitive information, PIT would be engaged as well.


### Analyst takeaway. — The regime does not forbid an analyst from owning a stock they cover -

it forbids HIDING it and acting against the reader's interest. Disclosure is the dividing line
between a legitimate recommendation and market abuse, which is exactly what the exam's
'is this a violation?' questions test.


### Common mistakes — The Regulatory Traps That Lose Easy Marks

1. Mistake: Thinking every employee of a registered research entity must individually register.
Why it happens: Students assume registration is personal.

Fix: The ENTITY registers. Employees need not register separately, but they must hold the NISM
certification and comply with the regulations.

2. Mistake: Believing the net worth requirement still applies.
Why it happens: Older study material predates the amendment.

Fix: The December 2024 amendment REPLACED net worth with a deposit - held in a scheduled bank,
lien-marked to RAASB/BSE, and scaled by the number of clients.

3. Mistake: Thinking research analysts register with NISM.
Why it happens: NISM is the most visible name in the process.

Fix: NISM only provides the certification (this exam). SEBI grants registration, now administered
through RAASB (BSE). NISM is not the registering or supervisory authority.

4. Mistake: Confusing the PIT and PFUTP regulations.
Why it happens: Both are SEBI anti-abuse rules.

Fix: PIT (2015) covers insider trading and UPSI. PFUTP (2003) covers fraud, manipulation and price
rigging. Different regulations, different scope.

5. Mistake: Assuming an analyst can never own a stock they cover.
Why it happens: Conflict of interest is read as an outright ban.

Fix: Ownership is allowed IF disclosed and IF the analyst does not trade contrary to the
recommendation. Non-disclosure is the violation, not ownership itself.

Key takeaways

### Hook — The Chart Saw It Coming. The Balance Sheet Didn't.

A stock with excellent fundamentals keeps falling, month after month. The fundamental
analyst is baffled - 'but the numbers are fantastic!' The technician shrugs; she saw it weeks

actually DOING with the stock, right now, in real money. Every price tick is a decision by a
real buyer and a real seller, and the chart is the footprint of all of them combined. Here is the
exam reality you cannot ignore: this single chapter is worth 15 marks - the most on the entire
paper, more than Financial Analysis or Valuation. Many candidates come from a

Treat this chapter as the biggest single block of marks on the exam, because it is.

### Concept — The Three Pillars of Technical Analysis

All of technical analysis rests on three assumptions, inherited from Charles Dow. One: the
price discounts everything - every known fact, from fundamentals to news to sentiment, is
already reflected in the price, so the price itself is the richest thing to study. Two: prices
move in trends - up, down, or sideways - and a trend in motion tends to continue until
something clearly reverses it. Three: history repeats itself - because human psychology, fear
and greed, is constant, the same recognisable patterns recur across stocks and eras. From
this flows the key division of labour: fundamental analysis answers WHAT to buy (intrinsic
worth, longer horizon); technical analysis answers WHEN to buy or sell (timing, price action,
often shorter horizon). They are different tools for different questions, and many
professionals use both.


> **Analogy.** Technical analysis is reading footprints in the sand. You do not need to know WHY

the herd moved - the tracks already tell you which way it went, how fast it was moving
(volume), and exactly where it hesitated or turned (support and resistance). The footprints are
the collected decisions of everyone who has traded the stock.

**Key terms**

- Technical Analysis — The study of price and volume data to forecast future price
movements.
- Price Discounts Everything — The assumption that all known information is already
reflected in the market price.
- Trend — The general direction of price - uptrend (higher highs and higher lows), downtrend
(lower highs and lower lows), or sideways.
- Support — A price level where buying tends to emerge and halt a decline (a floor).
- Resistance — A price level where selling tends to emerge and halt a rise (a ceiling).
- Momentum — The speed or strength of a price move, measured by oscillators such as RSI.


### Formula — The Indicator Formula Bank (With Worked Numbers)

Technical questions split into two types: identifying patterns (pure recall) and computing
indicators (a few set formulas). Master the formulas below - especially the simple moving

average and RSI, the two most likely to appear as a calculation - and the numerical part of
this 15-mark chapter becomes free marks. All examples use the same illustrative figures so
the indicators are comparable.

> Shared example. Illustrative data: recent daily closing prices are Rs 100, 102, 104, 106, 108.
For RSI, assume over the look-back the average gain is 3% and the average loss is 1%. For
MACD, assume the 12-period EMA is 110 and the 26-period EMA is 106. For Bollinger Bands,
assume the 20-day SMA is 100 with a standard deviation of 5.
Simple Moving Average (SMA)
> Formula: SMA = Sum of closing prices over n periods / n
> Example: 5-day SMA = (100 + 102 + 104 + 106 + 108) / 5 = 520 / 5 = Rs 104
> Meaning: Smooths price to reveal the trend; the most basic technical indicator. Price above a
rising SMA is bullish.

Exponential Moving Average (EMA)
> Formula: EMA = (Close x k) + (Previous EMA x (1 - k)), where k = 2 / (n + 1)
> Example: n = 5 so k = 2/6 = 0.333. Today's close 110, previous EMA 104: (110 x 0.333) + (104
x 0.667) = 36.67 + 69.33 = ~Rs 106
> Meaning: Weights recent prices more heavily, so it reacts faster to new moves than the SMA
(which weights all periods equally).

Moving Average Crossover (Golden / Death Cross)
> Formula: Signal occurs when a short-period MA crosses a long-period MA (e.g., 50-day vs
200-day)
> Example: Short MA crossing ABOVE long MA = Golden Cross (bullish). Short MA crossing
BELOW long MA = Death Cross (bearish).
> Meaning: A widely watched trend-change signal; the 50-day vs 200-day crossover is the classic
version.

Relative Strength Index (RSI)
> Formula: RSI = 100 - [100 / (1 + RS)], where RS = Average Gain / Average Loss over n periods
(typically 14)
> Example: Average gain 3%, average loss 1%: RS = 3/1 = 3; RSI = 100 - (100/4) = 100 - 25 =
75
> Meaning: Momentum oscillator from 0 to 100. Above 70 = overbought, below 30 = oversold,
around 50 = neutral.

MACD (Moving Average Convergence Divergence)
> Formula: MACD line = 12-period EMA - 26-period EMA; Signal line = 9-period EMA of the MACD
line; Histogram = MACD line - Signal line
> Example: 12-EMA 110, 26-EMA 106: MACD = 110 - 106 = +4 (above the zero line, bullish)
> Meaning: MACD crossing above its signal line is a bullish trigger; crossing below is bearish. The
histogram shows momentum strength.

Bollinger Bands
> Formula: Middle Band = 20-period SMA; Upper Band = SMA + 2 x Standard Deviation; Lower
Band = SMA - 2 x Standard Deviation
> Example: 20-day SMA 100, SD 5: Upper = 100 + (2 x 5) = 110; Lower = 100 - (2 x 5) = 90.
Band range = 90 to 110
> Meaning: Bands widen with volatility and narrow when it falls. Price near the upper band can
signal overbought; a 'squeeze' often precedes a big move.

On-Balance Volume (OBV)
> Formula: OBV is a running total: ADD the day's volume when the close is up, SUBTRACT it
when the close is down
> Example: If volume is 1,000 on an up day, OBV rises by 1,000; if volume is 800 on the next,
down, day, OBV falls by 800 (net +200)
> Meaning: Confirms whether volume supports the price trend - rising OBV with rising price
validates an uptrend (Dow Theory).

### Deep dive — Dow Theory, Patterns, Candles & Indicators

Dow Theory: the foundation
The framework everything is built on. Its key tenets: the averages discount everything; the
market has three trends (primary, secondary, minor); the primary trend has three phases -
accumulation (informed buying), public participation or markup (the broad advance), and
distribution (informed selling); the major indices must confirm each other; volume must
confirm the trend; and a trend is assumed to continue until a clear reversal signal appears.

Patterns: reversal vs continuation
Reversal patterns warn that a trend is ending - Head and Shoulders (and its inverse), Double
Top and Double Bottom, Triple Top and Bottom. Continuation patterns signal a pause before
the trend resumes - triangles (ascending, descending, symmetrical), flags, pennants and
rectangles. The exam frequently asks you to classify a named pattern as reversal or
continuation, so commit the lists to memory.

Candlestick signals
Single candles and combinations capture short-term sentiment. A Doji (open and close nearly
equal) signals indecision; a Hammer at the bottom of a downtrend hints at a bullish reversal;
a Shooting Star or Hanging Man warns of a bearish turn; Bullish and Bearish Engulfing
patterns, and the Morning Star and Evening Star three-candle patterns, mark stronger
reversals.

Indicators: trend-followers vs oscillators
Trend-following indicators (moving averages, MACD) shine in trending markets but lag at
turns. Oscillators (RSI, Stochastic) shine in range-bound markets, flagging overbought and
oversold extremes. Watch for divergence: when price makes a new high but the oscillator
makes a lower high, momentum is weakening and a reversal may be near. No single
indicator is reliable alone.


### Real-world references

- The 200-day moving average is one of the most-watched long-term trend gauges for the
Nifty and Sensex, and crossovers around it draw heavy commentary.
- A golden cross (50-day crossing above the 200-day) and a death cross (the reverse) on the
headline indices are routinely reported by Indian financial media.
- Index breakouts above well-defined resistance levels are widely tracked on heavy volume,
since volume confirmation is central to Dow Theory.


### Real-world example — Reading the Confluence: A Stock at Resistance

Context. An illustrative stock approaching a known resistance level

Situation. A stock has rallied strongly into a price zone that capped it twice before - a clear
resistance level around Rs 114. A technician now reads several signals together rather than
acting on any one.

Data. The RSI has climbed to about 75 (overbought, above the 70 line). Even as price prints
a new higher high near resistance, the RSI prints a LOWER high - a bearish divergence. On
the latest session a Shooting Star candle forms right at resistance, and the up-move arrives
on falling volume.

Analysis. Individually, none of these is a sell. An overbought RSI can persist in a strong
trend; one candle can mean nothing; resistance can break. But here FOUR independent
signals line up: an overbought oscillator, bearish momentum divergence, a bearish
candlestick exactly at a proven resistance level, and weak volume failing to confirm the new
high. That confluence sharply raises the odds of at least a short-term pullback, and a
disciplined technician would tighten risk rather than chase the high.


### Analyst takeaway. — No single indicator is a signal - technicians trade CONFLUENCE, where

trend, pattern, momentum and volume all point the same way. And remember the division of
labour: technicals tell you WHEN, fundamentals tell you WHAT.


### Common mistakes — The Technical Traps That Lose Marks (and Money)

1. Mistake: Acting on a single indicator in isolation.
Why it happens: One clean signal feels decisive.

Fix: Trade confluence - wait for trend, pattern, momentum and volume to agree. An overbought RSI
alone is not a sell; a strong uptrend can stay overbought for weeks.

2. Mistake: Confusing reversal and continuation patterns.
Why it happens: Both are 'chart patterns', so they blur together.

Fix: Memorise the lists: Head and Shoulders and Double/Triple Tops and Bottoms are reversals;
triangles, flags and pennants are usually continuations.

3. Mistake: Reading RSI mechanically as 'overbought = sell now'.
Why it happens: The 70/30 rule is taught as a hard trigger.

Fix: Use RSI with trend context and divergence. In a strong trend, overbought readings can persist;
divergence is the more reliable warning.

4. Mistake: Using trend-following indicators in a sideways market.
Why it happens: Moving averages are the default tool.

Fix: Moving averages whipsaw in range-bound markets. Use oscillators (RSI, Stochastic) for ranges and
trend tools for trends.

5. Mistake: Ignoring volume when judging a breakout or pattern.
Why it happens: Price is what shows on the chart first.

Fix: Volume confirms price. A breakout or pattern completed on weak volume is suspect - volume
confirmation is a core Dow Theory principle.
